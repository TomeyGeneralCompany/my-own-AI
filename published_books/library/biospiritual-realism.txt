I am a great partisan of what, with deliberate irreverence, I have called esoteric Biospiritual
Realism; by which curious appellation I mean something at once simpler and more severe than
the name suggests: that the Hyperborean ethos, however high its metaphysical superstructure
may rise, ought at its foundation to remain intelligible even to an illiterate rider of the steppe, a
man who has never opened Plato, never parsed Aristotle, and yet knows perfectly well whom he
worships, whom he loves, and against whom he must stand. Strip away the scholia, the
genealogies, the biology, the metaphysics, and all the learned furniture, and three imperatives
remain: honor God, love your kin, and defeat your enemies. These are the pillars; everything
thereafter is commentary, elaboration, and collaboration. I would have the principle carried, not
as an Eleusinian mystery reserved for initiates, but as a standard upon the open plain: intelligible
beneath the sun, memorable beside the fire, and serviceable when philosophy has no leisure to
arrive.
Before I descend to biospirit, culture, morality, or even man himself, the argument must ascend
farther toward its principium, for Richard Hooker, with the sound instinct of an architect who
will not raise Corinthian columns upon marshland, begins in Book I of Of the Laws of
Ecclesiastical Polity not with the creature but with God and law. The order of created things
cannot be adequately explained by beginning with created things as though they were self-
grounding termini. Hooker's First Eternal Law is the order of God's own working, in his phrase,
“that order which God before all ages has set down with himself” (Hooker I.2.2); there is,
consequently, no Platonic senate seated above the Almighty, no celestial statute anterior to Him,
no Form of the Good before whose tribunal God must appear and plead conformity. God is first;
His operation is measured by no ontological antecedent, because no such antecedent exists.
From this first and unreceived order Hooker proceeds to the Second Eternal Law, and here the
architecture upon which the remainder of my argument depends begins to disclose itself. The
Second Eternal Law is the order communicated to creation, received by creatures, as Hooker
says, “according to the several condition with which he hath endued them” (I.3.1); and that
phrase, several condition, must not be hurried past, for creation is no undifferentiated heap of
matter awaiting the imposition of one monotonous manner of operation. God bestows
determinate natures, powers, conditions, and ends; the law is one in its divine source, yet
manifold in creaturely operation, because reception follows the kind, capacity, and condition of
the recipient. Unity at the fountain does not entail uniformity in every channel through which the
water runs.
Hooker consequently says that this eternal law receives “different and sundry kinds of names”
according to the things subject to it (I.3.1). Natural agents have the law proper to natural agents;
angels have the celestial law; rational creatures are bound through the law of reason; revelation
supplies divine law; and human communities make human law from what reason and revelation
disclose as fitting. This is not an accidental catalogue; it is a hierarchy of operation. The mode of
law corresponds to the mode of the creature, because what a thing can do follows from what sort
of thing it is.
That is why the ladder of creation matters; a rock possesses the powers proper to an inanimate
body. A plant possesses materiality but also life and growth; an animal possesses material and
vegetative powers together with sensation and appetite. Man possesses all of that embodied
creaturely life and, beyond it, rational powers proper to man. The higher does not erase the
lower. I do not stop being material because I am alive; I do not stop being alive because I sense; I
do not stop being an embodied animal because I reason. Reason belongs to the human creature. It
is not a little god floating above biology and deciding afterward what sort of creature it wishes to
inhabit. The scala naturae is not a ladder upon which each higher rung kicks away the one
beneath it; rather, like Atlas bearing the heavens without ceasing to stand upon the earth, the
higher power bears the lower within the unity of the creature.
Hooker makes the same point from the side of human action. Nature gives a creature a field of
possible perfection, while action is the actual employment of powers within that field. His
account of reason and will presupposes a creature already made capable of reasoning and
willing: man desires the perfection “of which Nature has made it capable” (I.8.1). Capacity
therefore precedes its exercise; if I possess the power to think, thinking is that power operating.
If I possess strength, exertion is strength operating; if I possess a power and never exercise it, the
power remains mine, but it has not passed into action. The sequence is therefore God, law,
created order, nature, power, operation, action.
Only now do I want to introduce biospirit, because biospirit sits inside this order rather than
floating beside it as a poetic metaphor. By biospirit I mean something biological and inherited:
the ancestral essence of a people manifested through their biological nature, the thing underneath
customs, institutions, moral systems, artistic forms, and characteristic ways of acting which gives
those manifestations their particular character in the first place. A people does not begin as an
empty rational abstraction and then vote itself into an essence. The living people precedes the
cultural expression produced by that people.
The biological point underneath that statement is modest in form but enormous in consequence:
inheritance precedes instruction. Human beings arrive as organisms possessing inherited
variation before anybody teaches them a philosophy. Polderman and colleagues’ meta-analysis
of 17,804 human traits found that “across all traits the reported heritability is 49%” (Polderman
et al. 2015, 702–709). That figure is not being asked to define biospirit for us. It supports the
prior biological proposition on which the argument depends: human variation is not
manufactured wholly by postnatal cultural instruction. The creature who receives instruction
already possesses an inherited biological constitution.
The same point appears when the field is narrowed from the enormous catalogue of human traits
to personality itself. Vukasović and Bratko's meta-analysis of behavior-genetic studies, drawing
on more than 100,000 participants, reported an average personality heritability estimate of
approximately 40 percent (Vukasović and Bratko 2015, 769–785). Personality is not identical
with biospirit, but personality matters because action is never performed by an abstract genome.
It is performed by embodied persons possessing temperaments, tendencies, thresholds, appetites,
aversions, and characteristic modes of response. If substantial variation in personality is
heritable, then inherited biological constitution reaches considerably farther into the field of
possible action than a catalogue limited to height, eye color, or other obviously physical traits
would suggest.
Once that is joined to Hooker, the place of biospirit becomes clearer. Created nature supplies
powers and dispositions; inherited biological nature gives those powers a concrete embodied and
genealogical existence; operation brings powers into action; repeated actions become habits;
habits become visible in family life, worship, craftsmanship, warfare, law, morality, art,
institutions, and the other things by which a people leaves its shape upon the world. Culture is
therefore downstream from the living people producing it. Biospirit is not manufactured by
culture; culture is one of the manifestations produced when the powers belonging to a biospirit
are put into operation. Thus the movement is not from an incorporeal abstraction downward into
life, as though Pallas must spring armed from the head of Zeus, but from received nature outward
into deed, custom, monument, and memory.
Nor does saying that culture is downstream make culture causally inert. Once a people has built a
way of life, that way of life becomes part of the environment in which later generations live.
Laland, Odling-Smee, and Myles describe human evolution as shaped by gene–culture
interactions, while Richerson, Boyd, and Henrich observe that culture can create “novel
environments that expose genes to new selective pressures” (Richerson, Boyd, and Henrich
2010, 8985–8992). The movement can therefore become recursive: inherited organisms produce
characteristic actions; actions build cultural environments; those environments alter
developmental and selective conditions; later generations inherit and act within the resulting
world. The feedback does not require culture to create the organism ex nihilo; it requires living
organisms to alter the conditions under which living organisms continue.
Modern genotype-by-environment research makes the same recursive point at the level of
complex traits. Boye and colleagues describe genotype × environment interaction as a key
mechanism underlying human phenotypic variation, while Herrera-Luis and colleagues likewise
treat genetic variation and environmental exposure as jointly operative rather than as mutually
exclusive causes (Boye et al. 2024, 1057–1068; Herrera-Luis et al. 2024, 768–784). The
significance here is not that environment displaces inheritance, nor that inheritance renders
environment irrelevant. It is that the effect of an inherited constitution can depend upon the
conditions in which it operates, while those conditions are themselves partly produced, selected,
and maintained by acting organisms. The organism and its environment therefore enter into the
same causal circuit.

Hooker gives this recursion a metaphysical grammar. Nature is not merely a label attached to a
creature before history begins; it is the received constitution from which powers proceed and
through which those powers operate. Culture, accordingly, need not be imagined as a second
creator standing over against nature. It can be understood as accumulated creaturely operation:
powers received according to nature are exercised in action, actions acquire regularity,
regularities harden into habits, customs, institutions, and built environments, and those products
of operation thereafter become part of the conditions within which later operations occur. The
Second Eternal Law therefore does not terminate at the bare possession of nature. A creature
receives a determinate condition, possesses powers proper to that condition, and by exercising
them leaves consequences in the world which become inputs into subsequent creaturely life.
Culture is therefore less a second Demiurge than an inheritance of operations: yesterday's deeds
become today's circumstances, and today's circumstances enter tomorrow's field of action.
Niche-construction theory gives the environmental return-path still greater precision. Kendal,
Tehrani, and Odling-Smee describe niche construction as an endogenous evolutionary process
reciprocal to natural selection: organisms modify the selection pressures in their environments,
and the altered environment can persist as an ecological inheritance affecting later generations
(Kendal, Tehrani, and Odling-Smee 2011, 785–792). Laland, Odling-Smee, and Feldman's
models of cultural niche construction make the specifically human point sharper still: culturally
transmitted practices can alter selection pressures and thereby participate in gene–culture
coevolution (Laland, Odling-Smee, and Feldman 2001, 22–33). Culture is therefore not merely
an output displayed after biology has finished its work. Once produced by living populations, it
returns upon those populations as a changed field of operation.
Bertalanffy gives this exchange between organism and environment a general systems form. In
General System Theory he treats the living organism as an open system: not an isolated thing
sealed against its surroundings, but an organized whole maintained through continuous exchange
with them (von Bertalanffy 1968). Openness therefore does not mean indeterminacy. The
organism remains a determinate organization while matter, energy, information, pressure, and
consequence pass through its relation with the environment. This is precisely the architecture
required by the recursion described here. A people can remain a bounded biospiritual system
without being causally insulated from climate, neighboring peoples, institutions, technologies,
religion, disease, scarcity, abundance, or the cultural world produced by its own prior action. The
boundary preserves the system; exchange supplies the conditions under which the system
continues to operate.
                          Boundary + Exchange -> Persistent Organization
This also joins Bertalanffy's open system directly to the Luhmannian boundary. The alternative
is not between a perfectly closed identity and an identity dissolved into its surroundings. A
system may be open to exchange precisely while remaining differentiated from its environment.
What crosses the boundary enters an already organized field; what leaves it is likewise the
operation of something determinate. Biospiritual recursion is therefore neither environmental
isolation nor environmental absorption, but organized exchange across a maintained distinction:
the people acts upon its world, receives the consequences of that action together with other
environmental pressures, and continues from the altered conditions without thereby ceasing to be
the system undergoing the exchange.
                               S_t |B E_t ; S_t <-> E_t ; S_t -> S_(t+1)
Luhmann supplies the systemic form of the same return-path. A system is not first an
undifferentiated piece of the world which later happens to acquire an edge; it exists as a
determinate system through the difference between itself and its environment. As Luhmann puts
the matter, the system is constituted through the system/environment distinction, and its
operations continually reproduce that distinction (Luhmann 2006, 37–57). This gives the
boundary a positive rather than merely prohibitive office. The boundary is what permits a living
historical people to remain something determinate while encountering an environment vastly
more complex than itself. Without selection there is no stable identity, because every possible
environmental difference would possess equal standing within the system and the distinction
between what belongs to it and what does not would collapse.
For biospiritual realism this means that the environment does not simply pour itself into a people
as though the latter were an empty vessel. The inherited organism, the accumulated culture, the
habits of response, the institutions, the memories, and the distinctions already reproduced within
the people condition what is selected from environmental complexity and what becomes
meaningful enough to enter subsequent operation. Luhmann's language of complexity reduction
is useful precisely here: the environment always contains more possibilities than any determinate
system can actualize, and the maintenance of a boundary allows the system to select among those
possibilities according to its own organization (Baraldi, Corsi, and Esposito 2021, 235–238).
Thus the recursive circuit already described is bounded recursion: environment acts upon the
system, but the system encounters that environment through the difference by which it remains
itself.

This is also why I reject the picture of man as a blank rational machine into which culture is
poured. Reason is real, and in Hooker it has a genuine governing office, but reason is itself a
power of an already existing creature. It can judge, discipline, redirect, restrain, and articulate
dispositions. It cannot reach backward through the Second Eternal Law and manufacture the
nature whose power of reasoning it is exercising. Biospirit therefore names the inherited
ancestral particularity operating within this larger created order: essence manifested through
biological nature, biological nature expressed through powers and dispositions, and those powers
made visible through characteristic human energies and actions.
McCulloch and Pitts supply a striking formal counterpart from neurophysiology. Their 1943
logical calculus begins from nervous activity itself and shows how relations among neural events
can be represented through propositional logic; logical operation is thus modeled as an operation
of an organized nervous net rather than as an incorporeal faculty descending upon biology from
outside (McCulloch and Pitts 1943, 115–133). The importance for the present argument is
immediate. Rationality need not be imagined as evidence that man has escaped the biological
order. The embodied nervous system is itself capable of operations describable in logical form.
Reason can therefore remain genuinely rational while remaining an activity of the living creature
whose nervous organization makes such operation possible.
                 Biological Organization -> Neural Operation -> Logical Operation
Placed beside Hooker, the point becomes sharper. Nature precedes power; power precedes
operation; and the logical character of an operation does not reverse that order of dependence.
McCulloch and Pitts do not make logic unreal by locating its calculable operation in nervous
activity; rather, they demonstrate how extraordinarily elaborate formal relations can be realized
through an embodied network. The rational act is consequently no ghostly exception to received
nature. It is one of the highest operations available to a creature whose nature includes the
biological powers required for nervous and rational activity.
The point can be stated in Hooker's own order of dependence. Reason has a real office because
rationality belongs to the nature of man, but the office cannot become sovereign over the nature
from which it proceeds. The operation presupposes the power, and the power presupposes the
creature whose power it is. Rational reflection upon nature therefore arrives after nature in the
order of being, even when reason later governs, restrains, cultivates, or redirects particular
actions. Reason may govern action; it does not create the nature whose action it governs.

I do not believe in being some grand philosopher chasing universal truths, perfect forms, or a
single cultural morality supposedly waiting above every people on Earth. The point of beginning
with Hooker is not to sneak Platonism back in through another door. It is almost the opposite;
created order is genuinely differentiated. The unity is in God; the creatures are not therefore
interchangeable; the Second Eternal Law orders things according to the conditions with which
God has endowed them. From that starting point, difference is not an embarrassment to be
rationalized away. It belongs to the created field in which particular beings actually live, act,
inherit, build, and become.
That does not require me to say that Forms are unreal. They can be real, and in that technical
sense universal, without therefore appearing everywhere as one identical thing. A Form can be
an archetype whose concrete shape depends upon the creature and people through which it is
manifested. Courage can be real without courage having to look culturally identical in every
people; kingship can be real without every people producing the same king; beauty can be real
without every biospirit recognizing and embodying beauty through the same forms. The
universal reality of the archetype does not erase the particularity of its manifestation.
That is where biospirit enters the question of Forms. The Form is not created by the biospirit, but
its lived manifestation passes through the biological and cultural substrate of an actual people.
What is universal at the level of archetype therefore becomes particular in expression. This is not
perennialism, because it does not require contradictory religions, moral systems, or peoples to be
secretly saying the same thing. It only means that a real archetype can be encountered and
embodied differently by genuinely different peoples.
Here Hooker's phrase “severall conditions” becomes useful again. Unity of source does not entail
uniformity of reception. The same divine order can be received according to different created
conditions without ceasing to possess one source; likewise, if an archetype is real, the unity of
the archetype does not require every embodied recipient to manifest it in an identical fashion.
The particular manifestation is conditioned by the particular creature through which it becomes
operative. The universal, therefore, need not abolish the several; it can become visible through
the several.
Luhmann's system/environment distinction gives this severalness an additional formal
expression. Every determinate system confronts more environmental complexity than it can
reproduce internally; it therefore selects, distinguishes, and responds according to the
organization by which it has already differentiated itself. The boundary is consequently a lens in
the strict systemic sense: not a transparent pane through which an identical world is copied
inward, but the condition under which environmental differences become information for this
particular system. The same object, event, archetype, command, danger, or opportunity can
therefore be encountered differently by differently constituted systems without the object itself
having to multiply into separate realities.

Perennialism does not work; here's why: in many these faiths, you have to have certain beliefs
that cancel out other beliefs. Christianity is actually a very good example of why this does not
work. Christianity does not teach that God discovered some universal morality floating around
outside Himself and then decided to obey it. There is nothing above God. There is no abstract
form called “the Good” sitting over God and telling Him what He is allowed to do. This is
exactly where Hooker's First Eternal Law comes back into the picture. “The being of God is a
kinde of lawe to his working,” Hooker writes, and therefore God is “a law both to himselfe, and
to all other things besides” (Laws I.2.2). The order according to which God works is not imposed
upon Him by something prior to Himself. There is nothing prior to Himself. There is no eternal
legislature above the Creator, no independent Good before which God and man alike must
appear, and no Platonic form whose authority God recognizes because even God has to follow
the rules.

God is the ultimate Stirnerite Egoist. He is the supreme Unique One, the Einzige, because there
is literally nothing external to Him that possesses authority over Him. God does not follow the
rules; his will creates the rules. Hooker's First Eternal Law gives us the Christian reason why this
can actually be said of God in a way it cannot finally be said of any creature. The human egoist
finds himself already in a world he did not make, possessing a body he did not originate,
surrounded by beings whose existence does not depend upon his permission. God does not; god
is uncreated. There is no world behind Him, no ancestor before Him, no nature assigned to Him
by somebody else, and no higher tribunal from which His authority descends. The Good cannot
exist independently of God because then you would have something above God to which God
Himself was subject, and Hooker's entire movement in the First Eternal Law runs the other
direction: the ultimate terminus of law is not something behind God, but God Himself.

The decisive distinction is therefore not quantitative, as though God were merely a creature
possessing more force than every other creature combined. It is ontological. The human egoist
possesses received being and consequently discovers himself already under an order he did not
establish; God possesses no received being at all. Hooker's First Eternal Law belongs precisely
here, because the order of God's working does not descend to Him from a superior source. The
creature receives both being and order; God receives neither. That is why the analogy between
divine sovereignty and creaturely self-assertion finally breaks at the point where it becomes most
interesting.
      Unreceived Being -> Unreceived Authority | Received Being -> Received Order

Moreover, that is where “might makes right” becomes interesting, because if you follow that
principle all the way up, eventually you arrive at God. God has the ultimate might, and therefore
the ultimate right; he created the universe. He owns the universe. Everything in creation exists
because He willed it into existence, and there is nobody above Him who gave Him permission to
do that. There is no cosmic court where God has to prove that He has legitimate title to His own
creation. There cannot be, because the existence of the court, the law invoked by the court, the
beings sitting upon the court, and the world in which the court convened would themselves
depend upon God for their existence. If you want to put it in Stirnerite terms, the entire universe
is God's property because He created it, possesses it, sustains it, and nobody can take it away
from Him. He is the ultimate supreme and unanswerable sovereign.

That does not mean Christianity has no morality for us; obviously it does. And here the
distinction between Hooker's First and Second Eternal Laws becomes essential. The First Eternal
Law concerns God; the Second Eternal Law concerns the order communicated by God to
everything He creates. Creatures receive that order, in Hooker's words, “according to the severall
conditions wherewith he hath indued them” (Laws I.3.1). God is not simply the biggest creature
sitting at the top of the same ladder. He is the Creator of the ladder; we, meanwhile, are creatures
upon it. We possess received being, received natures, received powers, and received conditions
of operation.

Morality therefore does not bind God from the outside; it binds us because God has the authority
to bind us. We are creatures and He is the Creator. Human submission to God therefore is not
submission to some moral abstraction that God also happens to obey. We obey God because God
is God; his authority does not come from morality. Morality comes from His authority. Hooker's
two Eternal Laws let us state the relation exactly: there is no lawgiver behind God from whom
the First Eternal Law receives its authority, while the creature really does receive its order from
God through the Second. The direction of dependence only goes one way. The sword, in this
account, is not a syllogism written in iron; it is the visible terminus at which an order ceases to
petition and begins to command.

Ancient genomics makes the population beneath such historical names increasingly concrete.
Allentoft and colleagues' population-genomic reconstruction of post-glacial western Eurasia,
using more than 1,600 ancient genomes after imputation, identified major ancestry transitions
associated with Neolithic farming populations and later with the spread of Yamnaya-related
ancestry across much of Europe roughly five thousand years ago (Allentoft et al. 2024, 301–
311). More recent work on Yamnaya origins has reconstructed the ancestry of the steppe
complex through large ancient-DNA datasets and extensive admixture among Eneolithic
populations before the later Yamnaya expansion. The important point for the present argument is
simple: historical populations are not merely literary categories. Population movement, mixture,
replacement, continuity, and ancestry can increasingly be reconstructed from biological remains
themselves.
Moreover, that biological nature does not develop in a vacuum; a population exists somewhere.
It has a climate, a geography, a resource base, diseases, dangers, periods of scarcity and periods
of abundance, and all of those things create pressures. If one environment punishes a particular
disposition while another rewards it, generation after generation, those populations are not being
subjected to the same conditions. Selection pressure works on living populations, and over
enough generations those different pressures help produce real distinctions in the nature through
which biospirit manifests. That does not mean one people has to be universally superior to
another. A screwdriver is better than a hammer at driving a screw; the hammer is better at
driving a nail. Difference is not the same thing as supremacy. It means different populations can
become fitted to different worlds, and those differences can then appear downstream in behavior,
family structure, preferences, norms, morality, and the cultures they build.

Moreover, this matters for everything I have already said about nature and biospirit. The same
Second Eternal Law by which the creature is bound is the order under which creatures receive
their different natures and powers. Man does not invent himself any more than he invents God;
he receives his existence and acts from within the condition of the creature he actually is. A
people likewise does not begin outside nature and subsequently select a biological identity, an
ancestry, and a collection of inherited dispositions as though ordering from a menu. Those things
precede the act of rational reflection. Biospirit therefore belongs on the creaturely side of
Hooker's distinction: it is not sovereign like God, nor does it legislate reality into existence, but it
is part of the received nature through which an embodied people acts within the order God has
created.

not opposites.

the recipient. What enters the field of a people is selected, interpreted, embodied, remembered,
and reproduced through distinctions already operative within that people. The Gospel can
therefore be one without its historical embodiment becoming ethnographically identical
everywhere. The boundary does not negate reception; it makes determinate reception possible. A
system which could receive only by ceasing to be itself would not have received anything as a
system at all; it would merely have dissolved the distinction through which it possessed a

give language to an instinct, or place somebody back in contact with values which already
resonate with his inherited nature, but it cannot manufacture the ancestral nature which makes
that resonance possible in the first place.
Accordingly, the recognition is instinctual before it is systematic. Heroic action, honor, loyalty to
kin, adventure, glory, retaliation, fate, and the other Hyperborean values described here are not a
personality test from which somebody deduces an identity. Within this account, their resonance
is evidence of an antecedent alignment: the person recognizes himself in them because there was
already something in him capable of answering to them. Someone who has to reason himself into
possessing that biospirit has misunderstood the claim, because reasoning can articulate what is
there, but it cannot reason the blood into becoming something else.
Luhmann's account of system-specific selection makes the language of resonance useful here. An
environmental event does not determine an identical internal consequence in every system. It
becomes consequential according to the distinctions and organization of the system encountering
it. The same heroic story, ancestral symbol, command, insult, ritual, or image can therefore pass
before two men and produce radically different operations. One experiences recognition; the
other experiences indifference or repulsion. The difference need not be located in the external
message, which may be identical, but in the bounded organization through which that message is
selected and made meaningful. Reversion is therefore not the passive copying of an external
ideology into an empty recipient; it is the activation of a response possible because the recipient
was already constituted in a manner capable of that response.

This is also why debate cannot function as a missionary mechanism for biospirit. If this were
merely an ideology, then the obvious project would be to produce the strongest syllogism, defeat
rival systems, and convert as many people as possible. But there is nothing to convert them into.
Argument may clarify the philosophy, and reason may describe its implications, but neither can
give a person an ancestry or manufacture the inherited nature from which the philosophy says
these instincts arise. If it resonates, the point of reversion is that the resonance was not created by
the argument.
Reverting, right? How they say it is reverting to Islam? Well, you are going to revert to being a
Hyperborean; i do not want to proselytize, convert, engage in apologetics, debate. There is no
debate; you do not debate an axe. You do not debate an aircraft carrier.
Debate implies that there is some shared truth amongst us all; i do not believe it. I do not see it; i
do not feel it. And if you want me to have to read the whole Western canon to find this truth,
when by the time I am done I am going to be so so extravagantly rationalistic that I am just going
to rationalize myself away anyway, why would I do that?
That is the rationalization problem I was talking about earlier. If every instinct, inherited
attachment, heroic impulse, and obligation has to pass through an indefinitely expanding chain of
abstractions before I am permitted to act upon it, the likely result is not some purified version of
myself. The likely result is that I have argued myself farther and farther away from the things I
began by actually being. That is what I mean by rationalizing yourself away.
I would rather go in my backyard and swing a mace around for 15 minutes than sit down and
read some Plato. That is why I think, if you want to go into philosophy, if you want to go down
that route, it is the Continental Germanic philosophy that I resonate with. I think that is what
exemplifies the Hyperborean spirit the most: Spengler, Nietzsche, Heidegger, Schopenhauer,
these people. If philosophy is to accompany such a life, I prefer a philosophy that smells of the
mountain path, the forge, and the storm, rather than one which leaves the reader forever seated in
the Academy while the world passes outside.

Chapter 5: Morality
Not saying that we do not have a sense of justice, we do not have a sense of morality, but for me
you cannot separate revenge from the equation of morality. In fact, I think revenge is the basis
for all morality; that is how blood feuds start.
If you lay hands on our family members, we are obligated by honor to have to retaliate.
Otherwise, we lose our prestige; we lose our standing. And now you can do whatever you want
to us because there is no retaliation.
The obligation is therefore relational before it is abstract; the injury is not experienced merely as
a violation of a proposition called Justice. Somebody has done something to one of ours; that fact
changes the position of everybody bound to the injured person by kinship and honor. If nobody
answers, the family has demonstrated that its members can be injured without consequence. If
somebody answers, the family demonstrates that harming one member creates a problem with
the others. That is why retaliation and prestige become connected.
This is where wergild makes sense alongside blood revenge rather than against it. Both assume
that an injury creates an obligation which cannot simply be dissolved by telling the injured group
to stop being angry. Blood can answer blood, or an accepted payment can answer the injury and
restore a settlement, but something has to answer it. The important thing is that the wrong
produces a consequence recognized by the people whose honor was implicated. Forgiveness
without satisfaction breaks that logic because it tells the aggressor that the injury can terminate
without an answering cost.
That is why revenge was one of the best moral systems, I think, ever; the Abrahamic—they put
their women in sheets and treat them like property. But if you did that in the Hyperborean world,
if you dishonored a family member, be them male or female, heads would have to roll. They had
to roll; otherwise, you were screwed. You had no more standing anymore; you had no more
prestige. You were seen as a weak family.
That is what in a certain measure kept everybody in check, is that fear of revenge. Turning the
other cheek was the basis of ruining morality; there is no morality if you turn the other cheek.
There is only real morality if there is revenge for a slight, now be that being a wergild payment
or a blood payment.
Yet to me, that makes more sense to me personally than any type of forgiveness. How do you
forgive somebody for—how do I say this in YouTube terms—violating a female member of your
clan? Do you turn the other cheek, or do heads roll?
If heads roll is your answer, you might be more inclined to be a Hyperborean. Just letting you
know, because heads rolling is my initial thought.
Actually, this morality does still pervade within us despite turning the other cheek being this
virtue that was trying to be hammered into us. I know many instances of people doing backyard
justice, so to speak, when people within their family were violated. It is still around in this world;
you cannot escape it because it is part of our biospirit.
Again, no matter how much we try to put on a veneer of forgiveness and love and universal
morality, the biospirit always shines through.
