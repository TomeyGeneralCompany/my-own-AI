When it comes to examining Melito
of Sardis, there is really only one
place that we can properly begin,
and that is by examining his view of
the Old Testament canon of
Scripture. This is the issue for which
Melito is most famous, because the
list he puts forth is actually the
oldest known list of Old Testament
Scriptures ever compiled by a
Christian author. What is especially
important is that Melito's
understanding of the Old Testament
canon vindicates the Protestant
canon of the Old Testament as the
one that most accurately reflects the
canon of the very early Christian
Church.

The importance of Melito's
testimony becomes still clearer when
the canonical question is treated as a
problem of information, recognition,
transmission, and systemic
continuity. Within the framework of
Prototectonic Cybernetics, a canon is
not an abstract, floating theological
ideal, nor is it merely an accidental
pile of ancient literature. A canon is
a determinate state of a
communicative community. It
consists not merely in the physical
existence of texts, but in the
operational recognition of certain
texts as belonging to the
authoritative, inspired corpus.

Consequently, when a historical
canon is compared with a later
canon, what must be measured is not
simply whether the lists look
superficially alike to a casual
observer, but what transformations
of state are required to pass from one
configuration to the other. We must
analyze the system's architecture to
see how information is maintained,
how boundaries are drawn, and how
the system self-regulates across
centuries of transmission.

To ground our mathematical
comparisons and prevent linguistic
ambiguity, we must formally define
the three distinct modern collections
of Old Testament Scripture under
consideration. Each tradition
represents a unique membership
vector containing a specific set of
operational boundaries.

The received Protestant Old
Testament configuration (𝑀𝑃)
consists of exactly 39 books. Its
membership is structurally identical
to the Hebrew Bible (the Tanakh),
categorizing books into the
Pentateuch, Historical books,
Poetical/Wisdom literature, and the
Prophets. Protestants view books
outside this vector as apocryphal and
non-canonical, meaning they cannot
be used to formulate or regulate
church doctrine.

The Roman Catholic configuration (
𝑀𝑅) consists of exactly 46 books.
This vector encompasses the 39
books found in the Protestant canon
alongside an additional layer termed
the Deuterocanon ("second canon").
This layer includes seven distinct
books: Tobit, Judith, Wisdom of
Solomon, Sirach (Ecclesiasticus),
Baruch (including the Letter of
Jeremiah), 1 Maccabees, and 2
Maccabees, as well as distinct Greek
expansions to the books of Esther
and Daniel (such as Susanna and Bel
and the Dragon).

The Eastern Orthodox configuration
(𝑀𝑂) typically contains between 49
and 51 books, depending on specific
national jurisdictions and historical
enumerations. Grounded primarily
in the ancient Greek Septuagint
translation network, this vector
accepts all 46 books of the Roman
Catholic canon but expands the
boundaries to include 1 Esdras, 3
Maccabees, Psalm 151, and the
Prayer of Manasseh. Within Eastern
Orthodoxy, these
extra-protocanonical books are
historically classified as the
Anagignoskomena ("things that are
read").

Melito's homily on Passover is the
only writing of his that has been
preserved in its entirety to the
present day. In addition to this,
however, we have a number of
fragments of other writings from
Melito preserved by other writers in
Christian history, which gives us
further insight into what Melito
believed about various topics. From
one of these fragments comes
Melito's famous canon list of the Old
Testament Scriptures. That fragment
is especially valuable because it
gives us an observed canonical state
from approximately the second
century. If we represent the
canonical state at a given time as 𝐶𝑡,
then Melito gives us evidence
concerning a particular historical
state 𝐶𝑀. Later Christian traditions
can likewise be represented as
distinct states, such as 𝐶𝑃 for the
Protestant canon, 𝑀𝑅 or 𝐶𝑅 for the
Roman Catholic canon, and 𝐶𝑂 for
an Orthodox canonical
configuration. The historical
question is therefore capable of
being stated with precision: how
much transformation is required to
move from 𝐶𝑀 to each of these later
states?
Melito's canon list of the Old
Testament comes to us from the
Ecclesiastical History of Eusebius of
Caesarea, a highly respected early
Church historian. Melito's canon can
be found in Book 4, Chapter 26 of
the Ecclesiastical History. His
understanding of the Old Testament
canon is as follows:

      There are five books of
      Moses: Genesis,
      Exodus, Numbers,
      Leviticus,
      Deuteronomy; Joshua,
      the son of Nave; Judges;
      Ruth; four books of the
      kingdoms; two books of
      omissions; the Psalms
      of David; the Proverbs
      and the Wisdom of
      Solomon; Ecclesiastes;
      the Song of Songs; Job;
      and among the prophets,
      Isaiah and Jeremiah.
      There are twelve
      prophets in one book,
      and Daniel, Ezekiel, and
      Esdras. From these I
      have made my extracts
      which are divided into
      six books.

The first thing to note is that the
order in which Melito reports these
books is different from the manner
in which we order them today. The
contents of the books themselves,
however, are nearly identical to the
Protestant canon of the Old
Testament. This distinction is
important because not every
difference between two canonical
descriptions constitutes a difference
in the canonical state itself. If one
description places Numbers before
Leviticus, while another places
Leviticus before Numbers, there has
been a change in ordering, but no
corresponding change in
membership. Likewise, if Samuel is
represented within the books of the
Kingdoms, the encoding of the
corpus has changed without
necessarily changing the corpus
itself.

The canonical question may
therefore be represented as a
comparison between historical states
rather than as an assertion that one
tradition merely "looks more
ancient" than another.

𝐶𝑡 = (𝑀𝑡, 𝑂𝑡, 𝐺𝑡)

Here 𝐶𝑡 represents the total
canonical state at time 𝑡. The
variable 𝑀𝑡 represents the specific
membership vector of the canonical
corpus at that time—the actual
identity and count of the distinct
books recognized as divine
Scripture. The variable 𝑂𝑡 represents
the structural ordering in which
those books are reported or arranged
within the list. The variable 𝐺𝑡
represents their grouping, taxonomy,
or division into sub-collections (such
as combining multiple books into a
single unit or under a broader
umbrella title).

This clear cybernetic distinction is
absolutely necessary because two
canonical lists may differ wildly in
their ordering (𝑂𝑡) or their grouping
(𝐺𝑡) without differing materially or
substantively in the actual
membership (𝑀𝑡) of the books
which they recognize as holy
Scripture. If a system rearranges its
data blocks or alters its indexing
titles, the representation has shifted,
but the core information boundary
remains completely identical.

Thus, when Melito places Numbers
before Leviticus, groups Samuel
with the Kingdoms, or places
Lamentations under Jeremiah, these
are changes in 𝑂𝑡 or 𝐺𝑡, not changes
in 𝑀𝑡. The substantive canonical
question is therefore whether the
membership of the corpus differs.

Accordingly, the differences in order
and grouping include listing
Numbers before Leviticus instead of
after it, grouping 1 and 2 Samuel
into the books of the Kingdoms,
referring to 1 and 2 Chronicles as the
books of omissions, grouping
Lamentations under Jeremiah, and
using Esdras to refer to both Ezra
and Nehemiah. These differences
tell us something about the historical
representation of the corpus, but
they do not substantially alter the set
of books being represented.

In cybernetic terms, the system has
changed its encoding without
changing the controlled state. The
distinction is directly analogous to
the difference between changing the
physical representation of
information (like switching from a
digital font to a printed script) and
changing the underlying information
which that representation is intended
to preserve. We cannot let apologists
confuse a change in indexing for a
change in substance.

There are only two aspects of this
canon list which differ from the
Protestant canon. The first is the
clear omission of Esther. Some
scholars do argue that this omission
is accidental, but I admittedly am not
convinced that this is the case. It is
worth mentioning that, out of all the
books of the Old Testament, the
exclusion of Esther is the least
surprising considering the history of
its reception not only in the
Christian Church, but also within
rabbinic Judaism, where its status
was long debated due to its lack of
the explicit name of God. In terms of
canonical state, however, this is a
genuine membership difference. If
Esther belongs to the Protestant
canonical state but is absent from
Melito's, then the membership
vectors differ at that specific
coordinate.

The second area in which Melito's
list potentially differs from the
Protestant canon is his inclusion of
the book of the Wisdom of Solomon,
or Wisdom for short. This seen in
Melito's exact phrasing of "the
Proverbs and the Wisdom of

Proverbs itself, which was widely

Melito lists the Wisdom of Solomon,

The correct treatment of such

to preserve it as uncertainty in the

separate membership coordinate;
under another, it does not.

Bruce Metzger, as one recent

Wisdom of Solomon. But even if we

canon than to both the Roman
Catholic and Eastern Orthodox

Just as important as this canon list

The objection can itself be expressed

\(V_{R}\ge V_{D}\)

In Ashby's formulation, 𝑉𝐷

The inequality expresses the strict

The Roman and Orthodox objection
