
Membership in the class of appointed ministries does

not, by itself, establish a rite for common sacramental

participation.


                            543
Consequence. The church cannot recover the Roman

sevenfold classification by placing heterogeneous rites

into one undifferentiated set. Lawful ordination must

retain the promise and ministry to which its sign

belongs; baptism and the Supper retain their common

office of sealing the Gospel to believers. The

classification follows the actual instituted functions and

scopes rather than treating every use of the word

sacrament as the name of the same operation (Calvin,

Institutes IV.14.20; IV.19.28, 31).

Marriage tests the difference between a holy ordinance

and an instituted seal of a saving promise. God gives

marriage its real good through the union of husband and

wife and the obligations governing their common life.

Paul then teaches the husband’s love through Christ’s

love for the church. The reference to the mystery directs

attention to the saving union of Christ and his body. It

does not appoint the marital ceremony as a new
                          544
sacramental instrument conveying that union. The

doctrinal comparison supplies understanding and duty;

sacramental institution would require the further

appointment which the comparison does not give

(Ephesians 5:25–32; Calvin, Institutes IV.19.34–35).

Calvin’s argument turns upon the operation of the sign.

Scripture’s many comparisons direct the understanding

toward a truth without thereby instituting a sacrament

for every object compared. A vine identifies

dependence upon Christ, a shepherd identifies his care,

and marriage identifies the relation between Christ and

the church. Each comparison teaches through its proper

content. A sacramental sign additionally possesses the

divine appointment through which it seals the specified

promise to its recipients. Promoting the first operation

into the second would multiply sacraments whenever

Scripture teaches through a created relation. The result


                           545
would abolish the definition governing the dispute

(Calvin, Institutes IV.19.34–35).

The five judgments therefore require five actual

examinations. Confirmation changes the promise

claimed beyond baptism; penance reclassifies the

penitent’s response and ecclesial reconciliation;

extreme unction transfers a sign into another

administration and effect; Roman orders confer a

priestly office contrary to Christ’s completed offering;

marriage receives a sacramental status which its

doctrinal comparison does not institute. The shared

definition governs those distinct failures. It also

preserves catechesis, repentance, care of the sick,

lawful ministry, and marriage in their appointed goods.

Calvin’s conclusion is secured through the specific

operation of each argument rather than through

repetition of the number two (Calvin, Institutes IV.19.3

–21, 22–31, 34–37).

IV.20.1–4 — Two governments

Calvin distinguishes spiritual and civil government to

protect both Christian liberty and political order. The

distinction is not modern separation of church and state;

the jurisdictions overlap in population and sometimes in

concern (Calvin, Institutes IV.20.1–4).

The Epistle to Diognetus describes Christians as

citizens of earthly communities whose decisive

citizenship is in heaven. They obey established laws

while their conduct exceeds the laws’ minimum

requirements. Heavenly allegiance orders their public

life without abolishing the obligations of earthly

residence. Calvin’s distinction of spiritual and civil

government develops this early Christian account:
                           547
Christ’s kingdom governs the conscience and final

hope, while civil order supplies the framework within

which believers discharge their temporal

responsibilities (Epistle to Diognetus 5).

Romans 13 identifies civil authority as ordained by God

and assigns it the public task of commending good and

punishing evil. Taxes and honor belong to the

obligations through which the governed sustain that

order. First Peter likewise directs submission for the

Lord’s sake, while naming the believers’ freedom and

their identity as God’s servants. Their liberty does not

remove the temporal obligations through which they

honor God in public life (Romans 13:1–7; 1 Peter 2:13–

17; Calvin, Institutes IV.20.1–4).

Spiritual and civil government consequently have

distinguishable objects. The conscience receives its

saving freedom through Christ, while civil office


                           548
governs temporal relations through law and public

judgment. Their common dependence upon God

excludes the absorption of either into an independent

sovereignty. Calvin’s distinction protects the

magistrate’s real office and the believer’s civil

obedience while refusing to make political subjection a

ground of justification or an unlimited authority over

worship. Two regulators can act on the same population

while possessing nonidentical domains and ends.

Civil and spiritual governments direct overlapping

persons through different commissions. Christian

liberty does not abolish civil obedience, and civil

jurisdiction does not grant the magistrate unlimited

government over conscience. Each office serves under

God through its entrusted operations. Their interaction

requires coordination of common concerns while

preserving the distinction of authority by which each

acts (Calvin, Institutes IV.20.1–4).
                            549
Cross-reference: See III.19 and IV.10.


IV.20.22–32 — Obedience, limits, lesser magistrates

Calvin strongly favors obedience, even under bad

rulers, but denies that human command can cancel

obedience to God. He also assigns constitutional

responsibilities to lesser magistrates where such offices

exist (Calvin, Institutes IV.20.22–32).

The apostles answer a prohibition upon their witness by

affirming the priority of obedience to God. Daniel 3

displays the same limit when the commanded act is

worship of an image: the faithful refuse that act while

facing the temporal punishment imposed upon them.

Romans 13 establishes the ordinary obligation of civil

obedience and identifies the office through its service

under God. The divine institution of authority therefore

cannot authorize a command that reverses the




                           550
authority’s own subordination (Acts 5:29; Daniel 3:16–

18; Romans 13:1–7; Calvin, Institutes IV.20.22–32).

Calvin distinguishes refusal of a sinful command from

the assumption of a general private power to depose

rulers. His further treatment of lesser magistrates

concerns a public office charged with restraining

another office, so the resistance follows an existing

constitutional responsibility. The biblical priority of

obedience to God establishes the conscience’s

boundary; the institutional argument identifies who

possesses authority to act within the temporal order.

Preserving both relations prevents civil obedience from

becoming idolatry and prevents a rightful refusal from

automatically granting every form of political

intervention. Hierarchy can contain return paths of

accountability. Subordinate office is not identical with

passivity.


                           551
The lesser magistrate’s corrective duty proceeds from

an office appointed to protect the people against

tyranny. Calvin distinguishes that public commission

from a private person’s assumption of coercive

authority. Where the commission exists, tolerating royal

oppression betrays the entrusted duty. The limit on the

supreme ruler is consequently administered through an

existing order of office rather than a self-created right

of each individual to govern by force (Calvin, Institutes

IV.20.31).

Cross-reference: See I.17 on providence and IV.8–10

on bounded authority.

Calvin closes the Institutes with civil government. The

placement is deliberate. Christian liberty could be

misunderstood as emancipation from political order,

and the spiritual kingdom of Christ could be confused

with civil rule. Calvin therefore distinguishes a spiritual


                           552
government that pertains to the soul and the worship of

God from a political government concerned with the

ordering of civil life (Calvin, Institutes IV.20.1).

Magistracy remains a lawful vocation under divine

sovereignty. Rulers are charged with justice, public

peace, and the protection of good order, and Calvin also

assigns them responsibilities concerning the public

maintenance of religion. Civil government therefore

operates within a theological account of public duty,

while its temporal office remains distinguishable from

the church’s ministry of Word and sacrament. The

distinction concerns the authority, means, and objects

of their action (Calvin, Institutes IV.20.4–9).

Subjects ordinarily owe obedience even to rulers who

perform their office badly, because the authority of the

office is not dissolved by every defect in the

officeholder (Calvin, Institutes IV.20.22–29). Yet


                            553
Calvin does not make political authority absolute.

Where rulers command what God forbids, obedience to

God has priority (Calvin, Institutes IV.20.32). He also

gives a qualified role to “popular magistrates” or lesser

magistrates who are constitutionally appointed to

restrain the tyranny of kings (Calvin, Institutes

IV.20.31).

The final chapter therefore returns to themes present

from the beginning: authority is real but bounded;

subordinate offices can be genuine instruments without

becoming ultimate; conscience belongs finally to God;

and creaturely order is lived under providence. The

Institutes ends not by withdrawing the Christian from

the world but by locating worldly obedience within the

larger government of God.

Calvin’s two governments are best represented as

partially distinct regulatory systems operating upon


                           554
overlapping human subjects but different primary

variables. Ecclesiastical government concerns Word,

sacrament, discipline, and conscience before God; civil

government concerns justice, peace, public order, and

outward conduct. Pathology arises when one regulator

attempts to absorb the entire variety of the other’s

domain.

Civil government preserves public justice and order

while ecclesial government teaches, nourishes, and

disciplines the church.

The same person belongs within both administrations,

and each office acts through its entrusted instruments.

Overlap of persons consequently requires coordination

while the distinct commissions preserve the limits of

authority developed below (Calvin, Institutes IV.20).

Calvin’s treatment of lesser magistrates constitutes a

distributed-control device within civil order. If all

                           555
corrective capacity is concentrated in the supreme

magistrate, then disturbances produced by that

magistrate cannot be corrected from within the system.

Constitutionally authorized lesser magistrates introduce

an internal countervailing channel. The point is not

democratic theory in modern form, but the structural

recognition that a regulator may itself become a

disturbance and therefore require regulation.

Obedience, however, remains the default because

Calvin fears a different instability: if every private

subject becomes final judge of public authority, the

civil system loses coordinated control and fragments

therefore does not maximize individual autonomy. It

places resistance within offices and limits. He prefers

authorised distributed regulation to uncontrolled

proliferation of controllers.

The command to obey God rather than rulers

authority has infinite gain over conscience. If A h is

human jurisdiction and A G divine authority, Calvin's

constitutional inequality is A h< A G. When a human

command requires violation of divine command, the

lower-order regulator has exceeded its legitimate

domain. The Institutes therefore ends where it began:

the creature knows itself truthfully only by knowing the

Creator, and every creaturely authority remains

bounded by the order of the God from whom its

authority derives.


                            560
The distinction of jurisdictions also allows Calvin to

preserve multiple centers of competence without

embracing sovereign pluralism in the ultimate sense.

Church and magistracy are coordinate only at the

creaturely level; both remain subordinate to God. The

architecture is therefore hierarchical at the highest level

and differentiated beneath it. This combination—

ultimate unity with proximate plurality—is one of the

recurring structural motifs of the Institutes.

Calvin's civil theory can finally be read as an attempt to

preserve social viability under conditions of moral

imperfection. Government exists because unregulated

human interaction does not reliably converge upon

justice. Law, office, sanction, custom, and adjudication

reduce the range of socially destructive outputs. Yet the

magistrate is drawn from the same fallen population

and therefore becomes a potential disturbance source.

The constitutional problem is recursive: the regulator
                          561
itself requires regulation. Lesser magistrates, law,

institutional office, and divine accountability are

Calvin's partial answers to that recursion.

This is where the end of the Institutes returns with

particular force to the beginning. Political order cannot

provide the ultimate reference because political rulers

are creatures whose self-knowledge is itself dependent

upon knowledge of God. A state that treats its own

continuation as the highest good has made an

endogenous variable absolute. Calvin's doctrine of

conscience denies that move. Civil viability matters,

obedience matters, and public order matters, but none

can become the terminal objective before which every

other variable is sacrificed. The final regulator remains

God, and the legitimacy of every subordinate control is

bounded by that confession.

The Flow of Calvin’s Institutes


The Architecture Considered as a Whole

The four books form one directed argument through the

source and conditions of salvation. Book One

establishes the Creator and the revelation by which he

is known; Book Two carries the creature’s corruption

into the necessity and sufficiency of mediation; Book

Three follows the completed work into communion and

its differentiated benefits; and Book Four gives

continuing dependence its public ministry and bounded

government. Each transition receives the conditions

already established. The church’s office depends upon

the saving gift, that gift upon Christ’s person and work,

and the creature’s knowledge of both upon divine

address.

The four books form a directed order of dependence.

The reference established in the doctrine of revelation
                          569
governs the diagnosis of fallen reception; the diagnosis

establishes the necessity of Christ and renewal;

communion with Christ communicates the benefits of

redemption; and the external means nourish that

communion through public teaching, worship,

discipline, and government. A later act inherits the

conditions established in the preceding argument.

The dependencies are actual operations: revelation

addresses the creature, grace renews willing, Christ’s

righteousness grounds acceptance, and the Spirit

communicates Christ through the appointed means.

Removing the source changes the operation; changing

the operation changes the consequence. The formal

account follows those directed acts through the same

saving and ecclesial history.

The equations specify these dependencies by fixing

their terms and deriving their consequences. An


                           570
invariant promise remains the reference of changing

confidence; a renewed transition opens responses

closed to fallen willing; a jurisdictional set admits some

acts and excludes others; and a coordinated repertoire

counts the responses actually available to a ministry.

Each expression develops the doctrine at the point

where its consequences govern the next step.

When the four books are read together, Calvin’s

arrangement becomes an argument in its own right.

Book One establishes the Creator before whom the

creature exists and the revelation by which that Creator

is known. The knowledge of God immediately

generates self-knowledge, while the corruption of the

knower explains the necessity of Scripture. Providence

then establishes that the Creator remains actively Lord

over the world he has made.




                           571
Book Two asks what kind of creature now stands

before this God. The answer is a fallen creature whose

faculties remain real but whose moral orientation

cannot repair itself. The Law exposes and restrains sin

while also instructing the renewed; the history of the

covenant directs the reader toward the mediator; and

Christ’s person and offices answer the problem that

anthropology has made insoluble from the human side.

Book Three begins with the decisive problem of

participation. An accomplished redemption outside us

must become ours. The Spirit unites believers to Christ;

faith receives the promise; repentance and sanctification

express the new life; justification establishes peace with

God; prayer exercises dependence; liberty orders the

conscience; election locates salvation in divine grace;

perseverance carries the believer toward resurrection.




                           572
Book Four then refuses to leave the saved individual

alone. God gathers a church and uses external means to

preserve it. Ministry, Word, sacraments, discipline, and

ecclesiastical order become instruments of divine care.

The discussion of civil government finally places the

Christian simultaneously within ecclesial and political

orders, both subordinate to God and neither entitled to

become God.

The movement is therefore genuinely systematic even

though the Institutes does not resemble a modern

dogmatics arranged under abstract loci. Calvin’s system

is a pilgrimage of relations: Creator and creature, judge

and sinner, mediator and redeemed, Spirit and believer,

Christ and church, conscience and institution,

providence and political order.

The four books form a cascade in which each solved

problem reveals the next control problem. Book One


                           573
establishes the source, norm, revelation, and

governance of the system; Book Two diagnoses the

failure of the human subsystem and introduces the

mediator; Book Three explains coupling, state

transformation, assurance, and perseverance; Book

Four supplies the institutional channels by which the

redeemed population is nourished and ordered in time.

The architecture is systematic: the output condition of

one book becomes the initial condition of the next.

Terms. B 1 establishes Creator, revelation, and

                          574
secures the dependence of ecclesiology upon revelation,

anthropology, Christology, and the reception of grace.

Ashby’s law of requisite variety and Beer’s recursive

organization together expose a repeated Calvinian

instinct: authority must possess enough differentiated

capacity to govern its proper disturbances, but higher-

order authority must not absorb functions that belong to

subordinate levels. Scripture governs church authority;

church authority orders communal religious life without

becoming lord of the conscience; civil magistracy

governs outward order without becoming God. The

resulting architecture is neither flat nor simply

centralized. It is layered, bounded, and recursively

accountable.

The recursive character of the system becomes clearer

as later books return to earlier principles. Scripture

established in Book One regulates church authority in


                           575
Book Four. Providence established in Book One

supplies the causal grammar for grace in Books Two

and Three. Christology established in Book Two

determines Eucharistic possibilities in Book Four.

Christian liberty established in Book Three limits

ecclesiastical and civil control in Book Four. The

system repeatedly feeds earlier determinations forward

into later domains, while later applications reveal

consequences latent in the earlier doctrines.

The Institutes develops ordered relations among Creator

and creature, Word and hearer, Law and sinner, Christ

and believer, Spirit and faith, minister and church, and

magistrate and subject. Their sources fix authority, their

acts communicate determinate content, and their

consequences change the persons and institutions

involved. Governance, communication, constraint, and

feedback occur within those effective relations and

carry the exposition forward through its four books.
                          576
The same argument employs different exact operations

where its objects require them. Shannon supplies the

measure of uncertainty over communicated distinctions.

Wiener follows correction through time; Ashby tests

the response repertoire against disturbances; Conant

and Ashby connect successful regulation to a

representation of its object; Beer organizes recurring

functions across institutional levels; and von Foerster

includes the observer’s act within the history it changes.

Each operation is defined where used and carries

forward the doctrinal content of its terms.

The mathematical developments proceed from explicit

premises. Set membership fixes jurisdiction and

reachability; implications fix saving dependence;

recurrences follow confidence, conduct, and correction

through time; and invariants fix the promise and ground

that remain settled while the creature’s condition

changes. Their consequences follow from the
                        577
definitions and conditions stated with each expression.

Historical results retain their source attribution, and the

developed equations retain the particular Institute

argument whose terms they express.

A further consequence follows from treating the work

as a cascade rather than a catalogue. Each book changes

the boundary conditions of the next. Book Two cannot

discuss human freedom as though Book One had not

established providence and revelation; Book Three

cannot discuss faith as though Book Two had not

established the incapacity of the fallen will and the

completed work of Christ; Book Four cannot discuss

church authority as though Book Three had not

established Christian liberty and the immediate lordship

of Christ over conscience. The sequence therefore

behaves like a constrained derivation: later propositions

inherit conditions established earlier. A commentary

that extracts a late doctrine without carrying those
                            578
inherited constraints forward will almost inevitably

misstate Calvin.

This inheritance is represented as a sequence of

admissible model spaces. Let M1 be the set of

interpretations compatible with Book One's account of

Creator, revelation, and providence. Book Two narrows

the admissible space to M2 contained in M1 by adding

Fall, bondage, Law, and mediator. Book Three adds

union, faith, justification, sanctification, liberty, and

election, producing M3 contained in M2. Book Four

then constrains ecclesiology and politics within M4

contained in M3. The notation does not imply that

Calvin formally deduces every later sentence from

earlier axioms; it states that the system becomes

progressively constrained by commitments already

made.




                            579
That progressive constraint explains Calvin's

characteristic polemical method. An opponent is often

rejected not because one isolated proposition sounds

implausible but because accepting it would force

revision elsewhere. Make the church the source of

Scripture's authority and Book One's hierarchy of

revelation changes. Make the will spiritually neutral

and Book Two's account of grace changes. Make

justification depend upon inherent righteousness and

Book Three's assurance architecture changes. Make the

pope an unrestricted earthly head and Book Four's

relation between Christ, Scripture, liberty, and office

changes. Systematic theology is therefore regulatory in

a second sense: propositions regulate the admissibility

of other propositions.

The same is true of error propagation. A small change

at a high-order doctrinal node can produce large

downstream consequences. Calvin's fierce attention to
                       580
first principles is intelligible under this condition. He is

often guarding what systems theory would call a high-

leverage variable: a commitment whose alteration

changes many dependent relations at once. Scripture's

position in Book One propagates into worship, church

office, councils, vows, sacraments, and civil

conscience. Christ's headship propagates into the

rejection of papal supremacy and into the limitation of

every ministerial office.

There are also feedback paths running backward

through the exposition. Later doctrines illuminate

earlier ones. The church's dependence upon Word and

sacrament clarifies why Scripture's authority had to be

settled so carefully in Book One. The Eucharist clarifies

why the two natures of Christ had to remain distinct in

Book Two. Christian liberty reveals the institutional

consequences of justification. Thus the reading process

itself becomes recursive: after Book Four, Book One is
                           581
not read by the attentive reader exactly as it was before.

The system has supplied additional context that changes

the observer's model of its earlier parts.

This recursive rereading is one reason the Institutes

rewards repeated study. A linear first pass acquires

propositions; a second pass perceives dependencies;

later passes increasingly perceive the organisation that

makes the propositions cohere. The reader is

constructing and revising a model of the text. The

model becomes better not merely by accumulating

more sentences but by discovering which distinctions

control other distinctions, where feedback occurs,

which variables are invariant, and which local

arguments are instances of a larger regulatory principle.

Seen whole, the Institutes begins to look less like four

books placed beside one another than a gigantic

dependency structure Calvin has spent years booby-


                            582
trapping against incoherence. Tug Scripture and

ecclesiology moves. Tug anthropology and grace

moves. Tug Christology and the Supper starts making

alarming noises from three books away. This is what a

system is: not a pile of doctrines but a set of relations in

which local alteration propagates.

And this is why slow reading becomes unavoidable.

Calvin is the sort of writer who will establish a

distinction on page two and then, hundreds of pages

later, quietly use it to hit the papacy with a chair. If the

reader has forgotten the distinction, the later argument

looks merely polemical. If the dependency is

remembered, the violence is architecturally

comprehensible.

Calvin’s final political argument carries providence and

conscience into civil obedience. Jeremiah’s command

to seek the peace of the city binds the exiles’ common


                            583
welfare to the order under which they live, despite their

ruler’s injustice. Daniel’s refusal of the king’s impious

command preserves allegiance to God while denying

the ruler authority over that act. The first passage

establishes a duty surviving the ruler’s wrongdoing; the

second establishes a boundary surviving the ruler’s

rank. Calvin joins them through the distinction between

divinely appointed office and a command contrary to its

divine source. Public resistance belongs to magistrates

entrusted with restraint of tyranny (Jeremiah 29:7;

Daniel 6:22; Calvin, Institutes IV.20.28, 31–32).




                           584
Final Commentary


Why the Institutes Still Rewards Slow Reading

The greatness of the Institutes lies less in the novelty of

every individual doctrine than in the pressure Calvin

places upon doctrines to coexist. Scripture and Spirit

must be distinguished without separation. Providence

and human responsibility must both be affirmed.

Justification and sanctification must not be confused,

yet Christ must not be divided. Sacramental signs must

not be identified with the realities they signify, yet they

must not be emptied of divine efficacy. Church

authority must be real without becoming sovereign over

revelation. Civil authority must be obeyed without

becoming lord of conscience.

Calvin’s doctrinal judgments retain the relations

through which he establishes them. Predestination

presupposes Christ and the appointed means of
                         585
assurance; church authority remains bounded by

Christian liberty; human corruption coexists with

retained civil and intellectual gifts. Removing those

dependencies changes the claim being read. The force

of each conclusion therefore follows through the

premises and distinctions governing its place in the

whole argument.

The commentary’s references return the reader to the

argument in its actual place. A brief conclusion receives

its force through the objection answered, the text

expounded, and the dependencies preserved. Calvin’s

extended treatment makes those relations visible

because a doctrine must coexist with the other truths

already confessed. The reader therefore follows the

locus through its reasoning and carries its consequence

into the later discussion which receives it. The

greatness of the work becomes apprehensible through

that sustained movement of mutually governing claims.
                         586
The basic movement remains simple enough to

remember: know God, know the creature before God,

confront the Fall, flee to Christ, receive Christ through

the Spirit by faith, live the double grace of justification

and sanctification, pray, hope, belong to the church,

receive Word and sacrament, and inhabit civil life

under God’s providence. The intellectual labor of the

Institutes lies in explaining what each of those

imperatives means and why none can safely be

detached from the others.

The recurrence of bounded secondary agency is the

single most important structural feature exposed by this

reading. Calvin repeatedly grants a creaturely

instrument genuine efficacy and then denies that the

efficacy makes the instrument ultimate. Human reason

truly knows many things but does not become the norm

of revelation. Ministers truly teach but do not originate

the Word. The Law truly restrains but does not
                         587
regenerate. Faith truly receives but does not become the

meritorious ground of justification. Sacraments truly

communicate and confirm but do not operate

independently of the Spirit. Magistrates truly govern

but do not own conscience. The same causal grammar

is instantiated across epistemology, soteriology,

ecclesiology, and politics.

Subordinate authority possesses a real act within a

received boundary. A minister who fails to teach leaves

his commission unperformed; a council which makes

its own decision sovereign over the Word exceeds that

commission. The two failures differ in operation while

sharing the same governing dependence. Calvin’s

defense of ministry, discipline, and civil order requires

the entrusted acts to be exercised, while his judgments

against papal usurpation and dominion over conscience

require their limits to remain effective. Ordered


                              588
authority preserves both the work appointed and the

source under which it is done.

The same pattern explains the importance of error

correction. Fallen human beings remain the media

through which preaching, government, interpretation,

and discipline occur. No institutional design can

therefore presuppose perfect transmitters. Calvin's

system continually supplies references outside the

immediate agent: Scripture corrects interpretation,

councils can correct individuals while remaining

corrigible themselves, discipline corrects members,

lesser magistrates can correct tyrannical rulers, and the

sacraments correct the believer's forgetful inward

estimation by returning faith to an external promise.

Error is expected; the question is what architecture

prevents error from becoming sovereign.




                           589
Created variety enters the order through gifts, offices,

vocations, circumstances, political forms, temptations,

and historical events. Their differences require fitting

responses rather than indiscriminate suppression.

Christian liberty preserves the range of lawful conduct,

the moral Law excludes destructive acts, and

differentiated ministries supply the responses needed by

the church’s common life. Providence encompasses

their whole history without erasing the causes through

which it occurs. The relevant constraint therefore

follows the object and office: a response must possess

sufficient capacity for its disturbance and remain

directed toward its commissioned end. The question

“how much control?” thus receives its answer through

the prior question “which variety belongs to which

office under which norm?”

Feedback returns the consequences of an act to the

judgment and conduct of the agent. The Law discloses
                         590
discrepancy to the sinner; discipline returns communal

judgment to the offender; preaching repeatedly directs

the congregation to the Word; prayer articulates need

before God; and providential trial exposes creaturely

weakness. These loops retain their different operations

and recipients. Accusation exposes error, restraint limits

conduct, and renewal changes the agent capable of

obedience. Their effects follow from the reference,

power, and end governing each relation.

The church preserves its identity through the

coordinated operations of teaching, worship, material

care, discipline, and common judgment. A local

congregation exercises these operations in its own life

and coordinates with other congregations through wider

offices. The wider body must preserve the local

operations through which its own common life is

sustained. Beer’s recursive organization states the

recurrence of these functions through nested
                           591
institutions, while Calvin’s account of ministry fixes

their scriptural commission and authority.

The observer remains within the order he describes.

The interpreter is a creature whose judgment requires

correction, the minister remains subject to the doctrine

he teaches, and the magistrate remains accountable

under the government whose public acts he serves.

Reflection therefore returns the governing rule to the

person who applies it to others. Von Foerster’s account

of observing systems gives this inclusion its explicit

form, while Calvin identifies the creaturely condition

and the divine reference governing it. The return does

not make the observer the source of truth; it exposes his

continuing dependence upon the truth received.

The equations identify dependencies among

distinguishable terms: juridical status and progressive

renewal, sign and saving reality, institutional office and


                           592
divine source, creaturely intention and providential

outcome. Their directed relations specify which act

grounds a later state, which means communicate a gift,

and which authority governs a subordinate office. The

resulting formal account preserves the distinctions

through which the Institutes coordinates its doctrinal

claims.

Calvin’s arguments establish premises, preserve

distinctions, assign offices, and trace consequences.

The equations continue that work by stating which act

produces a change, which source fixes an invariant,

which office carries a response, and which norm

bounds an authority. A reader can therefore follow the

dependency from its biblical premise through its

mathematical expression to the judgment Calvin draws.

Creaturely administration is distributed through real

commissions under one divine government. Parents,


                          593
magistrates, ministers, elders, teachers, congregations,

and councils possess acts which another office cannot

perform merely by asserting superior rank. Their

coordination requires communication of needs and

judgments while preserving the particular capacities

through which the responses occur. Hierarchy

consequently gives their dependence an order without

making subordinate agents passive. The wider authority

serves the common end when its exercise sustains the

local operations upon which that common life depends.

Received knowledge governs action through the

disposition and capacities of its recipient. Additional

testimony reaches the fallen mind through its corrupted

reception; the Spirit renews that reception so that the

Word becomes effective in faith and obedience.

Scripture supplies its authority, the promise supplies its

determinate content, and the renewed person receives

and acts. Effective communication joins the disclosed
                         594
truth to the organisation through which it governs

judgment and conduct.

The saving order preserves its ground while

transforming the creature who receives it. Regeneration

changes the inward disposition, sanctification develops

faithful capacities, reform corrects the church’s public

life, and resurrection completes embodied redemption.

The received promise remains fixed through this

movement toward its fulfillment. Preservation and

transformation consequently retain different objects

within one history: the source and confession continue,

while the person and community undergo the changes

through which their appointed end becomes actual.

Sanctification develops capacities through the

correction it includes. A wrongful disposition is

opposed, truthful knowledge is acquired, an act of

obedience is exercised, and a habit becomes stronger


                           595
through its continuing use. The mature person

consequently possesses more than a reduced record of

deviation. His available responses, understanding, and

loves have been formed through the Spirit’s sustaining

work. Development changes the organization from

which subsequent acts proceed, joining the correction

of present conduct to the growth of a life capable of

fuller faithful action.

The church exhibits the same duality. It preserves a

received gospel and sacramental order while

continually confronting new disturbances, political

conditions, controversies, and pastoral needs. Viability

requires invariance of identity together with adaptive

variety in administration. Too little invariance dissolves

confession; too little adaptation renders the institution

incapable of responding to its environment. Calvin's

own reforming project exists inside precisely that

tension.
                           596
The equations identify the invariants, changing states,

essential variables, observing agents, corrective acts,

and jurisdictional boundaries governing Calvin’s

argument. The promise remains settled through

fluctuations of confidence; grace changes reachability;

the Word bounds the offices that minister it; and divine

government orders the actions of subordinate agents.

Each relation fixes the operation through which the

received doctrine governs persons and common life.

The direction of dependence determines the

consequence of a doctrinal relation. The Spirit effects

faith, union communicates the double grace, the Word

gives the sacramental sign its promised object, and

providence governs the secondary causes through

which events occur. Accompaniment alone would

establish none of these offices. Each claim identifies

what supplies, bounds, communicates, or transforms the

other term. Verbs such as “relates,” “works,” or “leads”
                          597
therefore receive their determinate meaning through the

actual act being expounded, and the equation retains

that directed operation in the consequence it expresses.

Open-loop and closed-loop organization specify

whether the resulting state returns information to the act

governing it. Discipline, pastoral exhortation, self-

examination, and prudential government receive

consequences and correct subsequent creaturely action.

Divine government proceeds from complete

knowledge: God’s decree does not require subsequent

events to disclose an unknown result. Creaturely

correction occurs within the providential order whose

source and end God already knows.

Time indexes the progressive manifestation of relations

whose grounds remain fixed. Justification establishes

acceptance; sanctification develops the renewed life of

the accepted person. Election establishes the eternal


                           598
ground; calling communicates its effect within history.

Sacramental institution appoints the ordinance;

administration performs its entrusted act on a particular

occasion. Explicit temporal indices preserve those

dependencies through their changing historical

expression.

Disorder occurs through real acts within the order God

governs. A channel transmits falsely, an observer

misclassifies, an office exceeds its commission, or a

corrective intervention intensifies the deviation it

addresses. Sin reaches the persons and institutions

through whom these operations proceed, so their

fallibility belongs to the conditions of the church’s

continuing life. Providence encompasses that history,

while grace gives the renewal the fallen person cannot

produce. Correction and restoration therefore have an

effective source beyond the disorder they confront, and


                           599
the final consequence remains governed by the God

who creates, reconciles, and sustains.

Calvin’s account joins the preservation, knowledge,

violation, and restoration of order through divine

government and creaturely action. Information received

becomes judgment, judgment governs conduct, and

conduct returns consequences to the person and

community acting. Grace transforms the recipient, the

promise fixes confidence, and the Word regulates every

subordinate authority. The developed relations carry

these claims from their source into their effective

operation.

The Institutes ends by directing obedience to its highest

source. Acts 5:29 gives divine command priority over

conflicting human command, and 1 Corinthians 7:23

binds the redeemed person to Christ. Civil office

remains real and subordinate; its authority cannot


                           600
dispose of the allegiance Christ has purchased. The

closing rule therefore completes the opening movement

from Creator to creature: received existence establishes

dependence, and redemption establishes the obedience

through which the restored creature serves God (Calvin,

Institutes IV.20.32).
