can disturb the believer’s apprehension without altering

the truth or faithfulness of the God whose promise he

receives. A stable external reference and a variable

subjective estimate must be distinguished; disturbance

in confidence does not alter the promise’s truth-value.

Calvin’s rejection of faith made justifying by an added

quality of love fixes the ground and instrument of

acceptance. Faith receives Christ’s righteousness; love

proceeds from the same living union as its fruit.

Turning that fruit into the ground of the verdict would

make reception depend upon the moral quality

produced in the recipient. Calvin preserves love’s

necessity by locating it within renewal while retaining

Christ’s righteousness as the sole ground of justification

(Calvin, Institutes III.2.7; III.11.7–23).

III.2.17–21 — Faith and doubt

Calvin spends substantial space on conflict because

certainty is not psychological invulnerability. Faith can

be attacked and obscured while continuing to cling to

the promise (Calvin, Institutes III.2.17–21).

Augustine places the believer’s continuing conflict

under a grace stronger than the disorder it confronts.

The saints struggle against the flesh and ask for the

strength to fight and conquer. Their need of help

accompanies the reality of their deliverance. Calvin’s

treatment of doubt preserves this account of faith

exercised amid opposition. Conflict exposes

dependence upon Christ; divine preservation carries the

believer through the conflict to the promised end

(Augustine, On Rebuke and Grace 29–30).

The father in Mark 9 asks Christ to help his unbelief

while professing belief. His request turns the weakness


                           271
of his faith into a reason to seek aid from its object.

Romans 4 describes Abraham confronting the apparent

impossibility of the promise while being strengthened

in faith and persuaded of God’s power to perform it.

The contrary evidence is neither absent nor unreal; the

decisive judgment concerns the faithfulness and power

of the One who promises (Mark 9:24; Romans 4:18–21;

Calvin, Institutes III.2.17–21).

Calvin’s certainty survives conflict through this relation

of dependence. The believer’s apprehension changes,

while the divine promise supplies the reference toward

which prayer and renewed hearing direct him.

Measuring certainty solely by undisturbed feeling

would make the disturbance itself decisive and detach

assurance from Christ. The example of faith seeking

help instead makes weakness answerable to the

Savior’s sufficiency. The same order governs

perseverance through temptation and suffering. A
                        272
regulator can remain oriented to a stable reference

while its internal estimate oscillates under disturbance.

The believer’s conflict with doubt occurs within faith’s

continued adherence to the promise. Assault and

weakness change experienced confidence without

changing God’s truthfulness or the object received.

Calvin’s pastoral argument therefore directs the

troubled person back to the promise rather than

requiring an uninterrupted internal sensation as proof of

faith. The corrective reference remains available

through the disturbance that makes renewed attention to

it necessary (Calvin, Institutes III.2.17–21).

Cross-reference: See III.24.5 on Christ as the proper

object of election-assurance.

Calvin’s celebrated definition describes faith as a firm

and certain knowledge of God’s benevolence toward us,

founded upon the truth of the freely given promise in

                           273
Christ and revealed to our minds and sealed upon our

hearts through the Holy Spirit. Every element matters.

Faith has cognitive content; its object is God’s gracious

promise; Christ is the center of that promise; and the

Spirit produces the certainty by which the promise is

personally embraced (Calvin, Institutes III.2.7).

This is why Calvin opposes the scholastic category of

“implicit faith” when it means submitting to

propositions one does not know simply because the

church holds them. He allows that believers know

imperfectly and that genuine faith may contain much

that is undeveloped, but faith cannot be reduced to

ignorance accompanied by institutional deference

(Calvin, Institutes III.2.2–5). Faith rests upon the Word

and therefore seeks knowledge of what God has

promised.




                           274
Calvin’s language of certainty does not mean believers

never experience doubt. Much of Book III, chapter 2 is

devoted to the conflict between faith and unbelief. Faith

may be assaulted, shaken, and obscured while still

clinging to the promise of God. The certainty belongs

ultimately to the truthfulness of the promise and the

work of the Spirit, not to uninterrupted psychological

confidence (Calvin, Institutes III.2.17–21).

This distinction is pastorally important. Assurance is

not generated by staring at the intensity of one’s own

believing. Faith looks outward to Christ. Even when

Calvin later discusses signs of election or the fruits of

grace, he repeatedly directs the believer away from

speculation about the hidden decree and toward Christ

as the mirror in whom election is contemplated (Calvin,

Institutes III.24.5).

Calvin’s definition of faith combines knowledge,

certainty, promise, Christ, and the Spirit. Faith is

described as a stabilized state estimate whose reference

perturbation within the estimating system rather than

The settled promise corrects the believer’s changing

apprehension amid fear and temptation.

The recurrence developed below follows that

Faith receives the determinate promise of reconciliation

transmission, and response belong to the same act of

communication.

Calvin's definition of faith as firm and certain

III.3.5 — Mortification and vivification

Calvin defines repentance as turning to God expressed

in mortification of the flesh and vivification by the

Spirit. The structure is ongoing, not a one-time

precondition paid before access to grace (Calvin,

Institutes III.3.5).

Augustine explains the usefulness of rebuke through

God’s gift of repentance. An admonition reaches the

ear, but its saving profit depends upon the Lord who

turns the heart. Peter’s restoration illustrates the relation

between outward encounter and inward grace. Calvin’s

account of repentance develops precisely this order:

God’s renewing action produces the changed judgment

and affection by which the sinner turns. The act of
                            283
turning belongs to the person, and its saving source

belongs to God (Augustine, On Rebuke and Grace 7–8).

Romans 6 derives the command to resist sin from

participation in Christ’s death and resurrection.

Believers are to reckon themselves dead to sin and alive

to God, then refuse sin’s reign in their bodies and offer

their members as instruments of righteousness.

Colossians likewise joins the putting to death of earthly

vices to seeking the things above and putting on the

renewed person. The commands follow the life received

in Christ and direct its continuing exercise (Romans 6:1

–14; Colossians 3:1–10; Calvin, Institutes III.3.5).

Mortification and vivification act upon different aspects

of the same renewal. The former counters the

dispositions and acts through which sin claims

dominion; the latter forms conduct toward the good

belonging to life in Christ. Repentance consequently


                           284
extends through time without becoming a recurrent

purchase of acceptance. The believer’s status in the

Savior grounds the continuing correction of his

conduct, preserving the distinction between justification

and sanctification. The Christian life contains negative

feedback against old patterns and positive development

of new capacities; correction alone does not exhaust

transformation.

Historical and theological location. Augustinian and

monastic languages of conversion lie behind the

categories, while Calvin integrates them into

justification by faith.

Cross-reference: See III.11–16.

Calvin’s movement from repentance into the penitential

controversy follows the question of what gives the

troubled conscience peace. Contrition belongs to the

sinner’s actual turning: sin is acknowledged, hated, and

                          285
forsaken through the renewal already received. It

cannot become a quantity of sorrow sufficient to buy

the divine verdict. The requirement of adequate

contrition instead sends the sinner back into an

indefinite inspection of his own grief. Faith directs that

same person to Christ’s accomplished satisfaction. The

causal order of repentance therefore determines the

order of assurance: reconciliation grounds the turning

which follows, and the depth of felt sorrow supplies no

alternative ground (Calvin, Institutes III.3.1–5; III.4.1–

3).

Confession gives acknowledged sin its determinate

address. Before God, the person names the offense and

seeks mercy from the one against whom it was

committed. Before an injured neighbour, confession

acknowledges the wrong and seeks restored peace.

Within the church, public confession expresses

common dependence, and personal counsel addresses a
                      286
conscience requiring instruction and comfort. Calvin

preserves these actual acts while rejecting a law that

makes an exhaustive recital of every sin to a priest

indispensable to forgiveness. The rejection concerns the

manufactured necessity and the authority claimed for it.

It leaves truthful acknowledgment, pastoral ministry,

and reconciliation with the neighbour fully operative

(Calvin, Institutes III.4.9–14).

James joins confession to prayer and the church’s care

for its members. Christ’s command to be reconciled to a

brother addresses the injury between persons before the

offender presents his gift. Neither passage turns the

minister into the source of pardon or gives a complete

inventory of remembered sins the power to procure it.

Their practical operations are definite: an offense

becomes acknowledged, an injured relationship

receives correction, and the community prays for the

person. The promise of forgiveness supplies the ground
                         287
upon which those operations proceed. Calvin’s account

of confession follows the scriptural distinction between

acknowledgment of sin and payment for sin (James

5:16; Matthew 5:23–24; Calvin, Institutes III.4.9–14).

An exhaustive-confession requirement also binds

assurance to a task the sinner cannot complete. Psalm

19 asks cleansing for hidden faults because the person’s

present knowledge does not contain every deviation in

his own conduct and desire. If pardon required a

complete enumeration, an unremembered offense

would always remain available to overturn confidence.

The resulting rule makes the finite observer’s inventory

the condition of the divine promise. Calvin removes

that dependence. The sinner confesses what is known,

acknowledges the depth of corruption exceeding that

knowledge, and receives the mercy promised in Christ.

Partial self-knowledge remains a reason for humility


                          288
without becoming a veto upon forgiveness (Psalm

19:12; Calvin, Institutes III.4.15–18).

Let K t be the sins known to the person at time t, C t the

sins then confessed, and S b the person’s actual offenses.

C t ⊆ K t ⊆ S b. The requirement C t = S b makes complete

self-observation necessary before peace is permitted.

Calvin’s order instead places pardon in Christ and

directs confession from that received reference. An

increase from K t to K t ₊₁ creates a new occasion for

repentance; it does not add a missing payment to

Christ’s satisfaction. The same account preserves

responsibility for newly exposed sin while keeping

acceptance grounded in the Savior (Calvin, Institutes

III.4.16–18, 27).

The power of the keys serves that promise. A minister

directs the penitent to the Gospel and declares

forgiveness on its terms. The declaration carries real

                            289
authority because it communicates Christ’s Word to a

person needing that testimony. Its efficacy does not

arise from the minister’s independent control of a

reserve of pardon. The church’s later disciplinary

judgment likewise distinguishes continued impenitence

from repentance and restoration. In each act, the

entrusted office applies a received command and

promise to a manifest condition. The source, content,

recipient, and lawful act must remain connected for the

ministry to retain its identity (Calvin, Institutes III.4.14

–24; IV.11.1–2).

Satisfaction introduces the decisive dispute over the

remaining debt. Calvin confronts the claim that

forgiveness of guilt leaves a penalty which the penitent

must discharge through assigned works. Tears, fasting,

gifts, and acts of mercy then acquire an expiatory

office. His answer returns to the burden Christ actually

bears. Isaiah’s servant receives the chastisement
                           290
through which the people obtain peace; Peter joins the

bearing of sins to Christ’s body upon the tree.

Reconciliation therefore rests upon the punishment

borne by the Mediator. The believer’s subsequent

obedience follows that reconciliation and supplies no

unpaid remainder of its price (Isaiah 53:5–6; 1 Peter

2:24; Calvin, Institutes III.4.25–30).

David’s continued affliction after Nathan announces

pardon tests this relation directly. Calvin distinguishes

judicial vengeance from paternal chastisement by their

governing end and the person’s relation to God. The

Father corrects the child whose sin is pardoned;

correction exposes, restrains, and reforms the disorder

remaining in that child’s conduct. It does not reopen the

account of expiation. Hebrews makes the relation

explicit by treating discipline as the act of a father

toward sons. The same painful event must therefore be

identified through its source, recipient, and purpose.
                           291
Pain by itself supplies no measure of an unpaid saving

debt (2 Samuel 12:13–14; Hebrews 12:5–11; Calvin,

Institutes III.4.31–33).

Indulgences extend the disputed accounting into an

the foundation before the mechanism: Christ’s

that promise. An ecclesiastical instrument cannot sell or

Purgatory preserves the same supposed deficit beyond

death. Calvin’s rejection therefore belongs to the

account of Christ’s cleansing already established. The

claim assigns a further expiatory operation to suffering

after death where the Gospel directs the conscience to

Christ’s sufficient blood. He also answers the appeal to

the fire in 1 Corinthians 3 by attending to the passage’s

object: Paul tests the work built upon the foundation,

and the defective work is exposed and lost. The passage

supplies no independent foundation for a place where

believers pay a remainder of their penal debt. The

judgment upon defective building preserves the

foundation rather than supplementing Christ’s

The whole controversy carries the opening account of

repentance into its institutional consequences.

Confession acknowledges the truth, absolution

communicates the promise, restitution addresses an

injured neighbor, and discipline corrects manifest

disorder. Their appointed effects remain substantial

because they operate within the salvation Christ

accomplishes. The error enters when one of these

subordinate acts assumes the office of completing the

purchase of peace with God. Calvin’s argument

preserves the acts by restoring their causal order: Christ

reconciles, faith receives, repentance follows, and the

church ministers the Word through which the penitent

returns to that settled ground (Calvin, Institutes III.3.1;

III.4.9–14, 25–33; III.5.1–6).

III.6–10 — Self-denial, cross-bearing, future life, use

of goods

The chapters on Christian life move from conformity to

divine holiness through self-denial and cross-bearing to

eschatological revaluation and disciplined use of

created goods. Each step changes the end governing the

believer’s conduct and the means through which that

conduct is formed (Calvin, Institutes III.6–10).

The Epistle to Diognetus describes Christians living

within ordinary households and cities while possessing

their citizenship in heaven. Their life displays fidelity

through marriage, care for children, obedience to lawful

authority, and goodness toward those who persecute

them. Heavenly belonging orders their earthly conduct.

Calvin’s account of the Christian life develops this

pattern of consecrated ordinary existence: the believer’s

dependence upon Christ governs the use of created


                           296
goods and the discharge of daily obligations (Epistle to

Diognetus 5–6).

Romans 12 places bodily self-offering under the mercy

of God and describes the renewing of the mind as the

means of discerning his will. The whole person is

ordered toward service through a transformed

judgment. Luke 9 joins following Christ to self-denial

and daily cross-bearing, making continued discipleship

answerable to the Master’s path. Colossians directs

desire toward the risen Christ and locates the believer’s

life with him, with its public manifestation awaiting his

appearing. Present conduct is governed by a future

whose source is already established (Romans 12:1–2;

Luke 9:23; Colossians 3:1–4; Calvin, Institutes III.6–

10).

This ordering changes the use of earthly goods and the

interpretation of suffering. Goods are received as


                           297
instruments of service; adversity is endured within

dependence upon the Lord; ambition is judged by the

end toward which it directs the person. Self-denial

therefore reorganizes action around belonging to Christ.

The ethical life is the sustained effect of the union

introduced at the beginning of Book Three. The

reference changes the weighting of present goods and

disturbances; a longer time horizon alters what counts

as loss, success, and essential variable.

Calvin rejects both indulgence and contempt for

creation. Earthly goods retain their appointed use, and

their reception requires gratitude, moderation, and

attention to vocation. Self-denial changes the governing

end of possession and enjoyment: the recipient

administers God’s gifts under duties to God and

neighbor. The discipline of use therefore belongs to the

goodness of creation received under its proper order

(Calvin, Institutes III.6–10).

Repentance follows faith. Calvin explicitly rejects the

idea that repentance is a meritorious preparation that

must be completed before the sinner may embrace the

gospel. True repentance flows from faith as fruit from a

tree. Yet this priority does not make repentance

optional. The grace that reconciles also renovates

(Calvin, Institutes III.3.1).

Calvin defines repentance in terms of a genuine turning

of life to God arising from sincere fear of God and

consisting in the mortification of the flesh and the

vivification of the Spirit. Mortification and vivification

describe a continuing pattern rather than a single

emotional episode. The old life is put to death and the

new life is brought forth through the Spirit (Calvin,

Institutes III.3.5).


                                299
The Christian life developed in chapters 6–10 gives this

repentance practical form. Calvin begins with

conformity to God’s holiness and then turns to self-

denial, bearing the cross, meditation on the future life,

and the right use of the present life. Self-denial is not

contempt for creaturely existence; it is the displacement

of the self from the position of ultimate rule (Calvin,

Institutes III.7.1–3).

Cross-bearing trains the believer in dependence and

exposes hidden confidence in personal strength.

Meditation on the future life relativizes the goods and

sufferings of the present without declaring creation evil.

Finally, Calvin’s doctrine of the use of earthly goods

rejects both indulgence and an inhuman austerity.

Created goods may be received with gratitude, but use

must be disciplined by vocation, necessity, love, and

the recognition that we are stewards rather than


                            300
absolute possessors (Calvin, Institutes III.8.1–6; III.9.1–

Calvin’s mortification and vivification are represented

as a paired regulatory process. Mortification suppresses

trajectories that carry conduct away from the norm;

vivification strengthens trajectories ordered toward

God. A purely negative model would be inadequate

because sanctification is not only error removal. It

includes the generation and stabilization of new

patterns of action.

Mortification and vivification coordinate the reduction

of sinful patterns and the strengthening of renewed life.

The Spirit operates through the Word, prayer,

discipline, and trial. Their effect changes the disposition

from which the person acts, so sanctification includes

both corrected conduct and the acquisition of faithful

capacities.

believer’s response; the Word identifies its error; the

Spirit forms renewed obedience through the correction.

Subsequent conduct carries the change into the next

occasion of trial. Providence, disclosure, and renewal

therefore operate together in the believer’s actual

development (Calvin, Institutes III.8.1–6).

Mortification and vivification give sanctification a two-

III.11.1–4 — The hinge and the duplex gratia

Calvin establishes justification as the principal ground

supporting religion and immediately locates it in

participation in Christ. Beveridge’s “twofold benefit”

joins reconciliation through Christ’s righteousness to

sanctification through his Spirit. The common Christ

secures their inseparability, while their distinct
                            308
operations preserve judicial acceptance and renewing

obedience (Calvin, Institutes III.11.1–4).

Clement of Rome excludes self-generated wisdom,

understanding, godliness, and works performed in

holiness of heart from the ground of justification. God

justifies through faith, and the source of the gifts

received is his will. Clement immediately asks whether

this excludes diligence in good works and answers by

urging such diligence. Justification by faith and

energetic obedience stand together because they occupy

different places in the saving order. Calvin’s double

grace gives this early Christian argument its developed

form: acceptance rests upon Christ’s righteousness

received through faith, and renewal produces the good

works belonging to life in him (Clement of Rome, 1

Clement 32–33).




                            309
Romans 3 identifies a righteousness manifested apart

from the law and received through faith in Jesus Christ.

The universality of sin removes the ground of human

boasting, while redemption in Christ explains how God

justifies freely by grace and remains just in doing so.

First Corinthians identifies Christ himself as the

wisdom, righteousness, sanctification, and redemption

given to believers. The saving benefits come together in

the same person, though their functions remain distinct

(Romans 3:21–28; 1 Corinthians 1:30; Calvin, Institutes

III.11.1–4).

Justification concerns acceptance before God through

Christ’s righteousness; sanctification concerns the

renewal of life received in him. Their shared source

excludes separation, while their distinct effects exclude

substitution. Treating progressive moral improvement

as the ground of the verdict would assign to the

changing condition of the believer the function
                          310
Scripture assigns to redemption accomplished in Christ.

Calvin’s double grace retains a stable judicial ground

while accounting for genuine transformation. Common-

source coupling yields distinct outputs: juridical status

and progressive transformation cannot be substituted

for one another.

Osiander makes indwelling divine righteousness the

ground of acceptance. Calvin preserves real union with

Christ while rejecting that transfer of the verdict’s

ground into the believer’s inward condition. Union

communicates distinct benefits: acceptance through

Christ’s righteousness and renewal through the Spirit.

Their common source does not make renewal the basis

of acquittal. The dispute therefore concerns the

operation grounding justification within the union both

acknowledge (Calvin, Institutes III.11.5–12).

Cross-reference: See III.1 and III.16.1.


                           311
At III.11.1–4, let J denote justificatory status and S t

sanctifying state. Calvin's causal ordering requires J≠ S t

and prevents J from being recomputed as J t =f( S t ).

Union supplies both benefits, but the forensic verdict

and progressive renewal occupy different variable

types. That typing is what keeps evidence,

consequence, and ground from migrating into one

another.


III.11.7–23 — Faith as instrument, Christ as

righteousness

Faith does not justify because faith itself is a worthy

moral achievement. It receives Christ. This is why

Calvin can insist on faith alone without making faith

the new work that replaces old works (Calvin, Institutes

III.11.7–23).

Clement’s exclusion reaches works performed in

holiness of heart. The argument therefore extends

                            312
beyond ceremonial observances or obviously corrupt

deeds to the person’s own religious excellence. Faith

receives a justification whose source is God. The

Epistle to Diognetus locates that acceptance in the

righteousness of the one Savior who justifies the many.

Together these texts give Calvin’s instrumental account

its patristic substance: faith receives Christ, and Christ

supplies the righteousness on which the verdict rests

(Clement of Rome, 1 Clement 32; Epistle to Diognetus

9).

Paul relinquishes a righteousness of his own derived

from the law and seeks the righteousness from God

received through faith in Christ. Romans 4 makes the

contrast explicit through the distinction between a wage

owed to work and righteousness reckoned to the one

who believes in the God who justifies the ungodly.

David’s blessing of forgiven sin then identifies

acceptance through God’s gracious non-imputation.
                        313
The receiver’s need is met by a righteousness whose

source is outside his achievements (Philippians 3:8–9;

Romans 4:1–8; Calvin, Institutes III.11.7–23).

Faith’s office is receptive. Its act reaches toward Christ

and receives the righteousness given in him; it does not

turn the believer’s trust into a new meritorious

performance. The distinction preserves the exclusion of

boasting through the whole saving relation. Assurance

consequently follows the sufficiency of Christ’s

righteousness rather than a judgment that the believer

has attained a sufficiently excellent degree of belief.

Instrument and ground occupy different causal roles.

Confusing them moves merit from works into the act of

believing.

Faith’s justifying office is reception of Christ’s

righteousness. Neither the intensity of its act nor the

love produced through union supplies the merit on


                           314
which God accepts the believer. The instrument

receives what its own operation cannot produce. This

preserves assurance at the righteousness of Christ while

giving the renewed life its full place as the effective

fruit of the same union (Calvin, Institutes III.11.7–23).

Cross-reference: See III.13–18 on merit and works.

At III.11.7–23, distinguish the object of faith R from

the believer's variable apprehension at . Calvin's

architecture permits at to fluctuate under disturbance

while R remains fixed; assurance is therefore not

modeled by making the promise itself a function of

momentary confidence. The distinction separates

instability in the observer from instability in the object

observed.


III.16.1 — Christ cannot be divided

Calvin’s anti-antinomian reply is structurally simple:

the Christ who justifies is the Christ who sanctifies.

                           315
Works do not ground the verdict, but a “faith” that

severs sanctification from union is not the relation

Calvin has defined (Calvin, Institutes III.16.1).

Clement follows his statement of justification through

faith with an appeal to God’s own fruitful working in

creation. The justified are summoned to diligent action

by the God whose gifts have already excluded boasting.

The works follow the reception of grace and express its

moral purpose. This is the order Calvin defends when

he refuses to divide Christ: the Savior who grants

acceptance also renews the person accepted. The

distinction of benefits establishes the proper place of

obedience within salvation (Clement of Rome, 1

Clement 32–33).

James rejects a profession of faith that leaves a hungry

or unclothed brother without help. His question

concerns the saving reality of a claim whose outward


                           316
expression contradicts its alleged life. Abraham’s

obedience demonstrates faith’s activity, and the

comparison with a body without spirit identifies the

absence of works as the absence of living faith.

Ephesians supplies the causal order: salvation is God’s

gift through faith, and those saved are created in Christ

for the good works God has prepared (James 2:14–26;

Ephesians 2:8–10; Calvin, Institutes III.16.1).

The works function as the living expression of a benefit

already received. They reveal the character of the faith

professed without acquiring the office of earning

acceptance. Calvin’s reply therefore retains both

conclusions: justification excludes works as its ground,

and union with the justifying Christ produces

obedience. A lifeless profession fails because it lacks

the saving relation whose existence it claims, not

because it has omitted an additional payment for the

verdict. Distinct outputs remain covariant because they
                            317
arise from one union, but covariance does not imply

causal identity.

The inseparability of justification and sanctification

follows from the indivisibility of Christ. The person

received by faith communicates both acceptance and

renewal. Their distinction fixes the operation of each

benefit; their common source secures their conjunction.

Good works therefore accompany living faith without

becoming the ground by which that faith justifies.

Separating renewal from acceptance divides the Christ

received, while grounding acceptance in renewal

reverses the direction of the gift (Calvin, Institutes

III.16.1).

Cross-reference: See II.7.12–13 and III.3.

Calvin calls justification one of the principal hinges on

which religion turns. To justify is not, in this context, to

make a person inherently righteous but to accept the

                            318
person as righteous in judgment through the

righteousness of Christ. Forgiveness of sins and the

imputation of Christ’s righteousness belong together in

this forensic account (Calvin, Institutes III.11.1; III.11.2

–4).

Faith justifies not because faith is a superior work but

because it receives Christ. Calvin is therefore careful to

deny that the dignity or quality of faith becomes a new

ground of merit. The believer is accepted because

righteousness is found outside the believer in Christ and

is received by faith. This is the basis of assurance: the

conscience is directed away from the unstable

measurement of its own moral achievement (Calvin,

Institutes III.11.7, 23).

The polemic against justification by works occupies

many chapters because Calvin thinks the issue reaches

the character of grace itself. Even works performed


                            319
after regeneration cannot become the ground upon

which the believer stands before God. They are real

fruits of grace, pleasing to God because he graciously

receives them, but they do not displace Christ as the

believer’s righteousness (Calvin, Institutes III.14.9–21;

III.17.1–5).

The distinction between justification and sanctification

is therefore a distinction without separation. Calvin

famously argues that Christ cannot be divided: those

whom he justifies he also sanctifies. Good works are

necessary as fruits, signs, and acts of grateful

obedience, but they are not the price of acceptance

(Calvin, Institutes III.16.1).

Justification establishes acceptance through Christ’s

righteousness received by faith; sanctification changes

the believer’s conduct and disposition. These are

distinct variables within one saving union. The verdict


                            320
takes its ground from Christ’s righteousness, while

renewal describes the changing life of the person

accepted. Making the verdict depend upon that

changing life would reverse the relation Calvin

establishes between acceptance and its fruits.

Acceptance rests upon Christ’s righteousness while the

justified person’s life undergoes renewal.

The verdict’s settled ground and the believer’s

changing conduct therefore occupy different offices in

one saving relation. The works testify to renewal

without becoming the price of acceptance.

Justification is where state, status, and performance

III.19.2–8 — Three parts of Christian liberty

Calvin distinguishes freedom from the Law as a ground

of justification, willing obedience rather than servile

compulsion, and liberty in indifferent things. The third

part becomes institutionally explosive in Book Four

(Calvin, Institutes III.19.2–8).

Romans 14 locates disputed practices before the Lord to

whom each servant belongs and will give account. The

person persuaded of a practice’s lawfulness remains

responsible for its effect upon a weaker brother. First

Corinthians applies the same distinction to food

associated with idols: knowledge of the creature’s

proper use does not authorize participation in idolatry

or disregard of another’s conscience. Galatians grounds
                            328
liberty in Christ’s deliverance from bondage and directs

its exercise toward service through love (Romans 14:1–

23; 1 Corinthians 8:1–13; 10:14–33; Galatians 5:1, 13;

Calvin, Institutes III.19.2–8).

Liberty therefore releases the conscience from invented

necessities while retaining the divine commands

governing love and worship. An indifferent act can be

lawful in itself and wrongful in its use because the

circumstances introduce an obligation to another.

Calvin preserves both the freedom of the object and the

responsibility of the agent. This distinction later limits

church legislation: practical order can regulate conduct

without converting an accommodation into an

indispensable condition of salvation. Liberty is a

jurisdictional boundary: lower-order regulators may

coordinate behaviour without reclassifying indifferent

variables as divine necessities.


                            329
Christian liberty concerns release from demands that

claim an authority they do not possess over conscience.

Christ’s righteousness establishes acceptance, willing

obedience replaces servile fear, and indifferent things

cease to become necessities for righteousness. Those

freedoms retain the duties of love and lawful order.

Their institutional consequence is a boundary: church

or civil office cannot convert its contingent

arrangements into the divine ground of acceptance

(Calvin, Institutes III.19.2–8).

Cross-reference: See IV.10.1–8, 27–32 on ecclesiastical

constitutions.

At III.19.2–8, jurisdiction is better represented set-

theoretically. Let J G be the domain of divine command

and J h the domain of human ordinance. Calvin permits

human regulation within J h while denying J h the

authority to rewrite J G or create divine necessity in

                            330
indifferent matters. Christian liberty therefore operates

as a boundary condition on subordinate control.


III.20.1–52 — Prayer as the principal exercise of

faith

Prayer expresses creaturely dependence inside

providence. Calvin does not imagine that petition

transfers missing information to God; rather, ordained

petition is one of the means within divine government

(Calvin, Institutes III.20.1–14).

The Didache directs believers to the prayer Christ

commanded in the Gospel and appoints its regular use.

Prayer is learned from the Lord’s teaching and offered

through the form in which he orders the church’s

approach to the Father. Polycarp likewise joins

supplication to perseverance, asking God to preserve

believers from temptation. Calvin’s extended treatment

develops the same dependence: prayer receives its

                           331
warrant, direction, and hope from God’s promise, and

its exercise belongs to the means through which

believers continue in faith (Didache 8, Polycarp, To the

Philippians 7, 12).

The Lord’s Prayer orders petition from the Father’s

name, kingdom, and will toward the creature’s needs,

forgiveness, and preservation from evil. The form trains

desire before it enumerates requests. Philippians

commands believers to present their needs with

thanksgiving and promises the peace of God guarding

heart and mind in Christ. Prayer places the person’s

apprehension and action under the God whose

providence already encompasses those needs (Matthew

6:9–13; Philippians 4:6–7; Calvin, Institutes III.20.34–

47).

The effect upon the petitioner belongs to the appointed

means, while the answer remains an act of the Father.


                          332
Petition acknowledges dependence, corrects anxious

self-sufficiency, and directs the believer toward

promised help. Divine foreknowledge leaves this action

meaningful because providence includes the means

through which the help is sought and received. The

stable reference is God’s promise; the changing state is

the believer’s judgment and confidence amid the

circumstances for which he prays. Communication can

be causally real without repairing an information deficit

in the higher-order knower.

Historical and theological location. Augustine’s

theology of prayer and desire is a useful genealogy;

Calvin’s immediate concern is pastoral formation in

dependence.

Cross-reference: See I.17.4–5 on creaturely uncertainty

under providence.




                           333
Prayer is the principal exercise of faith by which

believers daily receive God’s benefits. Calvin’s

placement is significant. Prayer follows his long

treatment of justification because dependence upon

grace does not produce passivity; it produces petition.

The believer who knows that every good comes from

God turns to God rather than treating divine grace as a

doctrine with no devotional consequence (Calvin,

Institutes III.20.1).

Calvin gives four principal rules for prayer: reverence,

a genuine sense of need, humility that abandons

confidence in personal worth, and confident hope of

being heard. These rules hold together attitudes that can

otherwise be separated. Boldness without humility

becomes presumption; humility without confidence

becomes despair. Christian prayer approaches God

through the mediator (Calvin, Institutes III.20.4–14).


                           334
Christian liberty likewise concerns the conscience’s

relation to God. Calvin gives it three parts: freedom

from the condemning rigor of the Law when seeking

justification; freedom to obey God willingly rather than

under servile constraint; and freedom regarding

outward things that are morally indifferent in

themselves (Calvin, Institutes III.19.2–8).

The third part is politically and ecclesiologically

explosive if misunderstood. Liberty does not mean that

every use of an indifferent thing is wise or loving.

Believers may voluntarily restrain themselves for the

sake of weaker brethren. But neither church nor

individual may manufacture divine necessity where

God has left the conscience free. This principle

becomes central in Book Four’s account of

ecclesiastical laws (Calvin, Institutes III.19.9–13).




                           335

thanksgiving to God within the government from which

every benefit comes. The promise directs petition,

received help gives occasion for thanksgiving, and

remembrance of that help changes subsequent

confidence and conduct. The return is effective within

the history God appoints: dependence produces action,

action belongs to the appointed means, and reception

renews the believer’s judgment of the source from

which the gift came.

Christian liberty limits the permissible control exercised

by human institutions over the conscience. Church and

state may regulate domains actually committed to them,

but an institution that attempts to bind conscience

where God has left it free is increasing control variety

beyond its legitimate jurisdiction. This is not merely

excessive control effort; it is a boundary violation

between levels of governance.

III.21.1–5 — Why Calvin treats predestination here

In the 1559 Institutes Calvin places election in Book

Three rather than under providence. That location

emphasizes its soteriological function: the question

arises from grace, faith, and the unequal historical

reception of the gospel (Calvin, Institutes III.21.1–5).

Ignatius addresses the Ephesian church as predestined

before the ages and united and elected through Christ’s

true passion by the Father’s will. Election is already
                           352
part of the church’s confession of its identity in the

Savior. Clement similarly places the gifts received by

God’s people in the divine will and describes Christian

calling on that basis. Calvin’s account develops this

confession into a sustained explanation of saving grace:

the church’s life in Christ proceeds from God’s

purpose, and the believer’s confidence rests upon the

mercy from which that purpose is fulfilled (Ignatius, To

the Ephesians, greeting; Clement of Rome, 1 Clement

32).

Ephesians places election in Christ before the

foundation of the world and identifies holiness and

adoption as the ends of that choice. Redemption,

forgiveness, and the Spirit’s sealing then unfold the

purpose within the believer’s received salvation.

Romans 9 excludes antecedent works as the ground of

God’s discriminating mercy; Romans 11 likewise

preserves the distinction between grace and works
                           353
while warning Gentile believers against boasting. The

history of unequal reception discloses dependence upon

divine mercy (Ephesians 1:3–14; Romans 9:10–18;

11:5–6, 17–22; Calvin, Institutes III.21.1–5).

Election explains the source of the saving relation

whose effects Book Three has already described. The

placement prevents the decree from becoming an

isolated cosmological abstraction: it answers why

sinners receive adoption, forgiveness, faith, and

preservation in Christ. Holiness follows the choice as

its appointed end. Reversing that order would make the

purpose depend upon the very effect it establishes and

restore the boasting Paul excludes. The hidden causal

parameter is introduced only after the observable

economy of grace has been described; order of

exposition controls the risk of speculative misuse.

III.24.5 — Christ as the proper “mirror” of election

Calvin directs assurance away from attempts to inspect

the hidden decree and toward Christ. The epistemic rule

is crucial: hidden cause is known through the revealed

relation God authorises, not by bypassing revelation

Ignatius binds the Father’s electing will to Christ’s

actual passion. Election is confessed through the

Savior’s saving work and the church’s union with him.

The eternal purpose reaches the believer in the crucified

Christ. Calvin’s direction to behold election in Christ

                            355
through the Mediator in whom the Father’s mercy is

of those given and raises them at the last day. In John

recognizable relation to Christ and the divine act

sustaining it (John 6:37–40; 10:27–29; Calvin,

III.24.1–11 — Calling and perseverance

Calvin distinguishes outward calling from the inward

efficacy of the Spirit and treats perseverance as divine

preservation through real conflict, not exemption from

conflict (Calvin, Institutes III.24.1–11).

Augustine treats perseverance as a divine gift and reads

Christ’s intercession for Peter as securing the faith that

endures. The will preserved by grace acts willingly, and

the Lord’s effective help brings that willing action to its

appointed end. Augustine also distinguishes this gift

from benefits received by persons who subsequently

fall away. The distinction prevents visible reception and
                           366
temporary participation from becoming the measure of

God’s electing purpose. Calvin’s account gives the

same governing role to divine preservation: the outward

call is heard widely, while the saving efficacy of grace

establishes and sustains the elect in Christ (Augustine,

On Rebuke and Grace 17–18, 20–23, 38).

Romans 8 joins foreknowledge, predestination, calling,

justification, and glorification through the repeated

identification of those upon whom God acts. The

sequence presents an ordered saving purpose reaching

its final end. John 10 locates the security of the sheep in

Christ’s gift and the Father’s power, while their hearing

and following describe the life preserved within that

security. Perseverance includes active discipleship and

depends upon divine preservation (Romans 8:28–30;

John 10:27–29; Calvin, Institutes III.24.1–11).




                           367
The outward summons can be encountered without

producing this whole saving sequence. Calvin

consequently distinguishes the communicated call from

the Spirit’s efficacy through which the person comes to

Christ and continues in him. Preservation does not

remove the occasions of conflict; it sustains the faithful

relation through them. The same distinction guards

sacramental teaching, where receiving an external sign

and receiving the saving reality are related acts with

distinguishable effects. Trajectory preservation does not

require zero perturbation; a system can remain within a

basin of recovery while undergoing severe transient

deviation.

Historical and theological location. Augustinian

perseverance is a major genealogy; later Reformed

scholasticism will formalize distinctions more tightly.

III.25.1–12 — Resurrection as the completion of

salvation

Calvin closes Book Three with bodily resurrection. The

terminal state of redemption is not merely survival of

the soul but restored embodied life before God (Calvin,

Institutes III.25.1–12).

Polycarp places the believer’s future resurrection under

the promise of the God who raised Jesus Christ.

Ignatius defends the risen Lord’s flesh against the

denial of genuine incarnation and bodily victory. The

hope of the church has the same bodily object as the

event on which it rests. Calvin’s account therefore

carries forward the early Christian insistence that

redemption includes the creature’s bodily life: Christ’s

victory reaches the person whom God created and

raises that person into enduring communion with

himself (Polycarp, To the Philippians 2; 5; Ignatius, To

the Smyrnaeans 1–3).
                           369
First Corinthians 15 makes the resurrection of the dead

depend upon Christ’s resurrection and explains the

transformation through continuity of the person raised.

The body sown in weakness and corruption is raised in

power and incorruption; the mortal puts on immortality.

Philippians locates the corresponding agency in the

Savior who transforms the believer’s lowly body to

conform to his glorious body by the power through

which he subjects all things to himself. The final state is

embodied life transformed by Christ’s rule (1

Corinthians 15:35–58; Philippians 3:20–21; Calvin,

Institutes III.25.1–12).

This completion governs the present use of the body.

The same person who labors, suffers, and obeys is the

person God raises, so bodily conduct belongs within the

saving history. Union supplies the relation, the Spirit

sustains its present life, and resurrection establishes its

enduring bodily form. Calvin’s conclusion to Book
                         370
Three fixes the end toward which the church’s external

means in Book Four nourish believers. The terminal

condition is supplied by divine eschatological action;

sanctification is not an autonomous process guaranteed

to converge by its own dynamics.

Book Four: The External Means by Which God

Invites Us Into the Society of Christ

Book Four develops the church’s organized common

life through Word, sacrament, office, discipline,

council, vow, and civil order. These appointed

operations preserve confession, communicate the

promise, correct conduct, distribute care, and bound

authority across time. Their recipients remain embodied

and dependent upon one another. The visible institution

gives those continuing needs their ordered ministry

under Christ.

Ministers regulate teaching under Scripture; councils

correct common judgment under the same reference;

discipline corrects conduct under proportionality,

process, and restorative ends. Sacraments nourish faith

through dominical institution. Magistrates govern civil

conduct within divine law and their entrusted office.

                          377
Each subordinate regulator receives both a commission

and a boundary. Distributed authority preserves the

common order through differentiated operations whose

exercise remains accountable to their governing source.

The church gives continuing creaturely need its

appointed common ministry. Believers require

teaching, nourishment, correction, material care,

coordinated judgment, and the preservation of

confession across generations. Those acts occur through

offices whose recipients and ministers remain finite.

The institutional question therefore follows through

their actual relations: who receives the disturbance, who

possesses the commission to answer it, how the answer

reaches the persons affected, and what source governs

the judgment. Book Four develops this common life

under Christ, carrying the saving dependence of Book

Three into public operations whose authority remains

ministerial.
                          378
Every ecclesial office receives an act together with its

governing boundary. Ministers teach under the Word,

councils judge under the same testimony, and discipline

proceeds under a process directed toward protection,

repentance, and restoration. Sacraments communicate

through divine institution, while civil government

exercises its own commission under God. The person

who corrects remains subject to correction, and the

body which coordinates remains accountable to the

confession it preserves. Authority consequently

operates through nested dependence: its reality consists

in the service entrusted, and its lawfulness continues

through fidelity to the source of that trust.

Congregations, ministers, and councils exercise

distinguishable responsibilities within a common

confession. Local discernment handles the needs of a

particular flock; coordination addresses disputes and

obligations extending beyond it; the received Word
                         379
governs both. Information about local conditions can

therefore travel toward wider deliberation while

doctrinal judgment returns to guide local action. Beer’s

account of organizational viability identifies this

relation between operational autonomy and

metasystemic coordination. Calvin specifies its

ecclesial norm through the authority of Christ and the

ministry of his Word (Calvin, Institutes IV.1.1–4;

IV.3.1–9; IV.9.1–14).

IV.1.1–4 — External means and the church as

mother

Calvin begins Book Four by denying that mature

Christianity outgrows external means. Word, ministry,

sacrament, discipline, and communal formation are


                           380
divine accommodations to finite believers (Calvin,

Institutes IV.1.1–4).

Clement describes the church as a body whose

members serve one another through the gifts God

supplies. The strong and weak, rich and poor, each

occupy a place within the common life preserved in

Christ. The church’s visible relations belong to the

means by which divine generosity reaches its members.

Calvin’s account of the church as mother develops this

corporate order. Faith is nourished within a community

whose teaching, ministry, and mutual care are

instruments of the Lord’s provision (Clement of Rome,

1 Clement 37–38).

Ephesians identifies Christ as the giver of ministers and

makes their service answerable to the equipping of the

saints, the building of the body, and maturity in faith

and knowledge. The body grows through its


                           381
coordinated members under its head. Acts 2 describes

the community’s continuing participation in apostolic

teaching, fellowship, breaking of bread, and prayer. The

received life is sustained through recurring public

practices (Ephesians 4:11–16; Acts 2:42; Calvin,

Institutes IV.1.1–4).

The church’s external means are therefore part of

Christ’s provision for growth. Teaching establishes the

received rule, sacramental participation confirms the

promise, and common life supplies the relations in

which love and correction are exercised. Their necessity

follows the creature’s dependence upon the means

Christ gives. Maturity increases the faithful use of those

means, while institutional authority remains subordinate

to the Head whose gifts give it purpose. The church is a

recursive environment of formation and memory:

individuals are regulated by institutions that are

themselves regulated by Word and confession.

IV.1.7–8 — Visible and invisible church

The distinction solves an observability problem. God

knows the true membership infallibly; human

administration acts through visible profession, ministry,

and discipline (Calvin, Institutes IV.1.7–8).

Second Timothy identifies the Lord’s knowledge of

those who are his and joins that divine certainty to the

public obligation to depart from iniquity. Matthew’s

Calvin’s distinction assigns different scopes to divine

IV.1.9–12 — Word and sacraments as marks

Calvin identifies faithful preaching and hearing of the

Word and lawful administration of the sacraments as

the public marks by which the church is recognised.

Discipline preserves the order of that body through

correction of its members. Recognition and correction

therefore perform distinguishable operations within one

ecclesial life: the marks establish grounds for

communion, and discipline addresses disorder within

the body recognised (Calvin, Institutes IV.1.9–12).


                           404
The Didache requires fidelity to the received teaching

and orders the baptized community’s participation in

the Eucharist. Teaching and sacramental communion

give the gathered body its recognizable form, while

false doctrine and corrupt conduct require discernment.

Calvin’s marks of the church develop this early pattern

into an explicit criterion: the Word governs the

congregation’s confession, and the sacraments enact the

Lord’s appointed fellowship. The visible church is

known through the faithful exercise of the gifts by

which Christ gathers and nourishes it (Didache 9, 11,

14–15).

Acts 2 connects the gathered church’s common life to

apostolic teaching and the breaking of bread. Matthew’s

commission orders the making of disciples through

baptism and instruction in all Christ has commanded.

Word and sacrament consequently have identifiable

content and authorized administration. The
                         405
congregation is gathered through teaching that belongs

to Christ and marked through the sign he appoints (Acts

2:42; Matthew 28:19–20; Calvin, Institutes IV.1.9–12).

These practices supply public criteria for recognizing

the body without claiming exhaustive knowledge of its

members. The judgment concerns whether Christ’s

gathering and nourishing means are faithfully

exercised. Calvin therefore distinguishes substantial

fidelity from every remaining defect in conduct or

administration. Discipline protects that fidelity and

corrects its violations; the mere existence of

imperfections does not erase the church’s identity

where the constitutive means remain present. The

marks are observable outputs coupled strongly to the

organization that reproduces ecclesial identity.




                           406
Later Reformed confessions frequently list discipline

explicitly as a mark; that is a development worth

distinguishing from Calvin’s exact wording here.

Cross-reference: See IV.12 on discipline.

The principal marks by which the visible church is

recognised are the pure preaching and hearing of the

Word and the lawful administration of the sacraments.

Where these are present, Calvin urges extraordinary

caution before abandoning a church because of

secondary defects. The doctrine of the marks therefore

serves both Reformation separation from Rome and an

argument against endless schism (Calvin, Institutes

IV.1.9–10).

The marks also reveal Calvin’s understanding of

ecclesial authority. The church is recognized by fidelity

to gifts and commands received from Christ, not by an

autonomous institutional pedigree. Antiquity,

                          407
succession, learning, and size may possess evidential

value, but none can compensate for the corruption of

the gospel.

The marks of Word and sacrament function as

diagnostic variables by which an observer identifies the

visible church. They are not exhaustive descriptions of

every good ecclesial property. A diagnostic variable

compresses a large state space into discriminations

relevant to a decision. Calvin’s marks therefore act as

high-value observables: where the Word is faithfully

preached and heard and the sacraments rightly

administered, the church is to be recognized despite

many remaining defects.

The constitutive ministry of Word and sacrament

supplies the public grounds for recognizing a church.

Recognition concerns these instituted operations in the

visible body. It neither requires complete purity of

                           408
every member nor authorises the dismissal of a

constitutive mark as incidental.

The marks function as observables by which an

invisible theological reality is provisionally recognised.

Calvin knows God alone perfectly knows the elect, yet

Christians must make practical judgments about visible

churches. Word and sacraments therefore serve as

public indicators. This is an observability problem:

internal state cannot be directly inspected, so judgment

proceeds from outputs that are causally related to the

identity in question.

Visible recognition proceeds through the public marks

Christ appoints. Outward forms admit hypocrisy, and

genuine churches contain serious defects; human

judgment therefore lacks exhaustive access to each

member’s inward condition. Word and sacrament

nevertheless supply sufficient grounds for recognition


                           409
and communion. Calvin’s rule fixes the evidence

required for that public judgment without demanding

knowledge of every heart.

The marks also limit schismatic sensitivity. If every

local defect were treated as evidence that the entire

system had ceased to be church, the recognition

threshold would be so strict that stable communion

would become impossible. Calvin tolerates secondary

error while identifying corruption of Word and

sacrament as a more fundamental failure. In signal-

detection terms, he is trying to avoid both false

positives and false negatives in ecclesial judgment,

though his theological criteria, not statistical

optimization, determine the threshold.

sacraments are administered. The criterion is therefore

condition of every member remains hidden, but the

Word preached and the sacraments administered are

publicly encountered. These acts testify to identity

because they also preserve it: the congregation receives

the confession through teaching and is nourished

through the appointed signs. Recognition therefore

proceeds through more than an outward resemblance.

The observer identifies ministries whose actual

operation gives the visible body its ecclesial character

and carries that character forward through time.

IV.3.1–9 — Ministry as divine accommodation

Calvin argues that God could govern the church without

human ministers but chooses to use them. Office is

therefore genuinely causal yet radically derivative

(Calvin, Institutes IV.3.1–9).

Clement traces ministerial appointment from God to

Christ, from Christ to the apostles, and from the

apostles to the bishops and deacons established among

their converts. Appointment follows proclamation, and

the candidates are tested. Ministry belongs to the

ordered transmission of the Gospel. The Didache

likewise instructs the congregation to appoint worthy

bishops and deacons who perform the service of

teachers. Calvin’s account develops this received order
                            415
of calling, doctrinal service, and accountable office

(Clement of Rome, 1 Clement 42; Didache 15).

Ephesians locates ministerial office in Christ’s gift and

orders it toward the saints’ maturity. Romans 10 traces

a communicative sequence: sending makes preaching

possible, preaching supplies hearing, and hearing gives

the occasion for believing and calling upon the Lord.

The minister occupies a real place within that sequence

while the saving message and authority come from

Christ (Ephesians 4:11–13; Romans 10:14–17; Calvin,

Institutes IV.3.1–9).

The derivative office is genuinely instrumental. Its

dependence upon the Sender establishes the limits of its

speech and gives its faithful exercise efficacy as an

appointed means. The hearer receives the Gospel

through human ministry without making the minister

the source of salvation. This distinction later governs


                           416
councils, the keys, and sacraments: ecclesial action has

an authorised function, and that function remains

answerable to the divine institution that gives it

purpose. Distributed regulation increases local capacity

while preserving a higher-order norm that no office

owns.

Martin Bucer’s Strasbourg ecclesiology and pastoral

practice are significant for Calvin’s development of

ministry and discipline.

Cross-reference: See IV.8 on doctrinal authority and

IV.12 on discipline.


IV.4.1–4 — Bishops and ancient polity

Calvin does not argue that the bare term bishop is

unbiblical. He can acknowledge ancient presidency

among presbyters while denying that this history

establishes universal papal monarchy (Calvin, Institutes

IV.4.1–4).

                           417
Jerome identifies presbyters and bishops through the

apostolic usage of Philippians, Acts, and Titus. He then

explains the presidency of one presbyter over the others

as an arrangement directed against schism. His

argument distinguishes the scriptural identity of the

ministry from the subsequent ordering of its presidency.

Calvin’s reception of ancient episcopacy follows this

distinction. An office serving concord has a legitimate

historical function; the history of that presidency

supplies no divine institution of universal papal

jurisdiction (Jerome, Letter 146.1–2).

Titus moves from the appointment of elders to the

qualifications of an overseer, identifying the office

through blameless conduct, household governance, and

the ability to hold and teach the faithful Word. In Acts

20, Paul summons the Ephesian elders and addresses

them as overseers made such by the Holy Spirit to

shepherd the church God purchased. The charge directs
                        418
their attention to the flock and to threats from false

teaching, including threats arising within their own

company (Titus 1:5–9; Acts 20:17–31; Calvin,

Institutes IV.4.1–4).

The office’s authority is specified by its pastoral

responsibility and fidelity to the received doctrine.

Those conditions govern any later arrangement of

presidency or coordination among ministers. Calvin can

recognize practical organization because it serves

common oversight; the organization cannot change the

source or scope of the office. A presiding minister

remains accountable to the Word and to the care of the

particular flock entrusted to him. Historical

differentiation of office does not by itself justify

unlimited expansion of jurisdiction.




                            419
Historical and theological location. Chrysostom,

Jerome, Cyprian, and other fathers are deployed

selectively in Calvin’s historical case.

Cross-reference: See IV.6–7.

Calvin treats ministry as a divine provision for human

weakness. God could govern the church without human

ministers, but chooses to use them as instruments,

thereby testing obedience and binding the community

together. The dignity of ministry therefore comes from

the office and commission, not from an independent

sacerdotal power possessed by the minister (Calvin,

Institutes IV.3.1–3).

His discussion of offices distinguishes extraordinary

ministries associated with the church’s foundation or

unusual need from ordinary offices continuing in the

church. Pastors and teachers have particular



                           420
prominence, while elders and deacons belong to the

church’s ordered life (Calvin, Institutes IV.3.4–9).

Calvin’s historical discussion of bishops is more

nuanced than a simple assertion that the title is

illegitimate. He can acknowledge ancient arrangements

in which one presbyter presided among others, while

rejecting the later concentration of universal jurisdiction

in the Roman see. The issue is the structure and limits

of authority, not merely vocabulary (Calvin, Institutes

IV.4.1–4; IV.6–7).

Calvin’s ministry distributes teaching, oversight,

service, and discipline among differentiated offices.

Ashby’s law explains why such differentiation can be

structurally necessary in any complex community: the

variety of pastoral, doctrinal, material, and disciplinary

disturbances cannot be absorbed by an undifferentiated

regulator with one response. Distributed offices


                           421
increase available regulatory variety while allowing

specialized competence.

Differentiated offices provide distinct and coordinated

responses to the church’s needs.

The relevant capacity is the repertoire actually

available, not a sum of office titles. The union of

repertoires counts overlapping responses once and

includes the joint actions made possible by

coordination.

Differentiated ministry increases the repertoire of

relevant responses. Teaching, pastoral oversight,

material care, discipline, and administration answer

distinct demands. Their distribution prevents one

undifferentiated office from becoming the sole channel

of response, and coordination joins their acts where a

disturbance crosses their competences. The church

thereby preserves its received identity through the

                           422
active service of its differentiated members (Calvin,

Institutes IV.3–5).

Yet differentiation creates coordination costs. Offices

can conflict, duplicate work, or become isolated. This is

where Beer's distinction between operational units and

coordinating functions is illuminating. Viability

requires neither total centralisation nor atomized

autonomy. Calvin's councils of ministers and elders,

common doctrine, and shared discipline provide

coordinating structures that preserve plurality without

surrendering unity.

IV.6.1–17 — Peter, Matthew 16, and universal

jurisdiction

Calvin reads the Petrine texts against the claim that

Christ instituted a transferable universal monarchy. The

exegetical dispute is therefore not whether Peter has

prominence but what sort of jurisdiction, if any, follows

from the text and whether it descends to Roman bishops

(Calvin, Institutes IV.6.1–17).

Jerome expressly places the bishop of Rome within the

common dignity of bishops. Rome, Engubium,

Constantinople, Rhegium, Alexandria, and Zoan share

the same priesthood, and their bishops alike succeed the

apostles. The size and wealth of a city create no

additional degree of episcopal authority. Calvin’s
                           426
rejection of universal Roman monarchy carries this

patristic principle into the question of jurisdiction.

Apostolic succession identifies an accountable ministry

within the church; it does not concentrate the authority

of the whole church in one metropolitan see (Jerome,

Letter 146.1).

Matthew 16 locates Peter’s confession in revelation

from the Father and connects the keys to the church

Christ builds. The passage establishes Christ’s authority

to gather and govern his people; Peter’s reception of the

confession leaves that authority with its divine source.

John 21 restores Peter to the responsibility of feeding

Christ’s sheep. The repeated possessive identifies the

flock’s owner, and the command defines the apostle’s

service toward it (Matthew 16:16–19; John 21:15–17;

Calvin, Institutes IV.6.1–17).




                            427
A pastoral commission establishes a duty toward

Christ’s people; it does not itself establish an

indefinitely transferable jurisdiction over every other

pastor. Calvin tests the Roman claim at that additional

inference. The office given to Peter remains real, while

the asserted monopoly requires an institution that these

commands do not supply. The distinction preserves the

keys and pastoral authority within the church without

converting a minister’s prominence into ownership of

the body he serves. The dispute concerns whether a

bounded office can validly map its jurisdiction onto the

entire ecclesial domain.

Historical and theological location. Late-medieval papal

monarchy, canon-law claims, and conciliar criticism

form the historical field. Calvin can appropriate

conciliar and patristic material against Rome while

rejecting conciliar infallibility.

Calvin’s notorious language is the conclusion of an

argument about usurped headship and corruption of the

particularly angry (Calvin, Institutes IV.7.25).

Second Thessalonians describes the man of lawlessness

disorder concerns the usurpation of an authority that

the description. The claim operates within a professedly

IV.8.1–9 — Ministerial doctrinal authority

Calvin gives ministers real teaching authority while

denying them power to create revelation. Authority is

therefore functional, bounded, and norm-governed

(Calvin, Institutes IV.8.1–9).

Cyril requires scriptural demonstration for the articles

of faith even while instructing catechumens through the
                           439
church’s received teaching. His own authority as

teacher stands within that requirement. The Didache

similarly refuses a teacher who departs from the

doctrine delivered. Calvin’s account of bounded

teaching authority therefore continues an established

patristic discipline: the minister is received as a servant

of divine truth and judged by fidelity to that truth. The

office carries a duty to teach what God has given (Cyril

of Jerusalem, Catechetical Lectures IV.17; Didache 11).

Paul describes ministers as servants of Christ and

stewards of God’s mysteries, then identifies faithfulness

as the governing requirement of stewardship. The

steward’s charge concerns goods entrusted to him; his

responsibility is measured by fidelity in their

administration. First Peter requires the speaker to speak

as the oracles of God and the servant to minister

through the strength God supplies, so that glory returns

to God through Christ. Authority, message, and end are
                         440
all received (1 Corinthians 4:1–2; 1 Peter 4:11; Calvin,

Institutes IV.8.1–9).

Calvin’s limits upon teaching authority follow from the

office’s constitution. Ministers can expound, defend,

and apply revelation because their commission requires

those acts. They cannot manufacture a new revelation

or make their judgment the final reference governing

the Word. Fidelity remains assessable precisely because

the entrusted message precedes its administration. The

same rule governs a council’s collective judgment:

gathering more stewards changes the procedure of

deliberation while leaving the source of their authority

unchanged. The regulator is regulated by an external

reference; legitimacy depends upon fidelity, not

autonomous institutional status.

The church teaches with real authority because its

ministry communicates the Word entrusted to it. That


                          441
authority remains bounded by the source it serves. A

minister’s declaration neither creates revelation nor

exempts his judgment from examination. Calvin

consequently preserves the church’s public teaching

office while denying that its exercise supplies an

independent norm above Scripture (Calvin, Institutes

IV.8.1–9).

Cross-reference: See I.7 and III.19.


IV.9.1–14 — Councils

Calvin values councils and the ancient ecumenical

settlements while denying automatic infallibility to

assemblies. Collective judgment can correct local error,

but collective agreement can also stabilise shared error

(Calvin, Institutes IV.9.1–14).

Acts 15 presents deliberation concerning Gentile

believers through testimony of God’s work,

examination of the disputed demand, and appeal to the

                           442
prophetic promise. Peter rejects placing a yoke upon the

disciples and identifies salvation through the grace of

the Lord Jesus. James brings the admission of the

Gentiles under the scriptural account of God’s restored

people. The resulting letter orders conduct necessary for

the community’s faithful life while refusing

circumcision as a condition of salvation (Acts 15:6–29;

Calvin, Institutes IV.9.1–14).

The council’s authority appears through its fidelity to

the Gospel and its application of that Gospel to a

concrete dispute. Deliberation distinguishes saving

necessity from practical order and tests the proposed

obligation against the revelation already received.

Calvin’s account retains that relation when evaluating

later councils. An assembly has no power to reverse the

distinction upon which the apostolic judgment

depended by turning an ecclesial accommodation into a

new ground of acceptance before God. Pooling
                        443
observers increases available variety but does not

guarantee a correct reference model.

Historical and theological location. Nicaea, Chalcedon,

and later medieval councils are part of the historical

evidence. Late-medieval conciliarism also supplies a

background to the question of whether councils can

limit papal authority.

Cross-reference: See I.13 and IV.6–7.


IV.10.1–8, 27–32 — Church laws and conscience

Calvin permits rules necessary for decency and order

while resisting ceremonies imposed as intrinsically

necessary to salvation or worship. The distinction

operationalizes Christian liberty (Calvin, Institutes

IV.10.1–8, 27–32).

The Didache distinguishes fixed fidelity to the Lord’s

command from practical ordering within the

community. It directs prayer by the Gospel while
                           444
allowing baptism in different available waters and,

where necessary, by pouring. The divine institution is

maintained through accommodations serving its

administration. Calvin’s treatment of church laws

develops this distinction between the command that

binds the conscience and arrangements that enable

orderly obedience under varying conditions. The

church’s regulation serves the Lord’s ordinance

(Didache 7–8).

First Corinthians requires the gathered church’s activity

to occur decently and in order. The command

authorizes arrangements directed toward intelligible

participation and common edification. Colossians

exposes the contrary use of regulation: human decrees

concerning touching, tasting, and handling acquire an

appearance of wisdom through self-imposed worship

and severity while lacking power over the flesh’s

indulgence. Outward strictness supplies no guarantee of
                          445
spiritual efficacy (1 Corinthians 14:26–40; Colossians

2:20–23; Calvin, Institutes IV.10.1–8, 27–32).

Calvin distinguishes the function of a rule from the

sacred necessity claimed for it. A schedule or procedure

can serve the command for orderly worship under

changing circumstances. Its utility gives no warrant to

identify compliance with saving righteousness or to

make the arrangement irreversible. The governing end

remains edification through God’s appointed worship,

and the conscience remains subject to divine command

rather than the institution’s elevation of its own

expedients. A local regulator may coordinate contingent

variables without rewriting them as higher-order

invariants.

Church laws regulate the circumstances of common life

through their appointed purpose. Time, place, and

orderly practice require determinate arrangements, and


                           446
those arrangements remain revisable as circumstances

change. Their practical necessity does not establish a

divine condition of righteousness. Calvin’s objection

begins where an administrative ordinance claims to

bind conscience as though God himself had imposed its

particular form (Calvin, Institutes IV.10.1–8, 27–32).

Cross-reference: See III.19.2–8.

Calvin grants the church genuine authority in doctrine,

legislation, and discipline, but repeatedly describes that

authority as ministerial. Ministers are stewards of

mysteries given by another; councils are to be heard

insofar as they faithfully interpret Scripture; and no

ecclesiastical body possesses the right to create new

articles of faith at will (Calvin, Institutes IV.8.1–9;

IV.9.1–14).

His treatment of councils is therefore neither simple

conciliarism nor simple individualism. Calvin respects

                            447

infallible merely by virtue of assembling bishops. Their

judgments remain subject to the Word (Calvin,

Institutes IV.9.8–13).

Ecclesiastical laws receive a similar treatment. Order

and decorum require common rules, and Calvin is no

anarchist about church practice. But laws become

tyrannical when human ceremonies are imposed upon

consciences as though necessary to salvation or divine

worship. Here the Christian liberty of Book Three

becomes an institutional principle (Calvin, Institutes

IV.10.1–8, 27–32).

Councils and church laws remain subject to Scripture

The church orders teaching and practice through offices

conformity to the Word, so the same regulating act

Beer repeatedly warns that metasystemic intervention

IV.11.1–2 — Binding and loosing

Calvin roots the keys in gospel ministry and church

judgment rather than a free-standing sacerdotal power

(Calvin, Institutes IV.11.1–2).

Matthew assigns binding and loosing within Christ’s

institution of the keys. John joins the declaration

concerning remission and retention of sins to the risen

Lord’s sending of the disciples and gift of the Holy

Spirit. The authority is received through a commission,

so its exercise remains bound to the message and

mission of the Sender. The minister addresses

forgiveness through the Gospel entrusted to him

(Matthew 16:19; John 20:21–23; Calvin, Institutes

IV.11.1–2).
                           459
The keys therefore operate as an authorized application

of Christ’s saving judgment. Their efficacy does not

arise from the minister’s independent power to create

guilt or forgiveness. He declares and applies the terms

supplied by the Lord, while the hearer’s response is

judged under those terms. This preserves the reality of

ecclesial judgment and its accountability: a sentence

opposed to the Gospel exceeds the authority through

which a valid sentence has force. Declarative authority

depends on accurate application of a reference it does

not create.

Binding and loosing exercise the Word’s judgment

through the church’s entrusted ministry. The minister

declares the promise and applies the discipline Christ

appoints; his office does not create an independent

source of pardon. The authority of the keys therefore

depends upon fidelity to the commission exercised.

Forgiveness belongs to God’s promise in Christ, and its
                         460
ministerial declaration remains governed by that

promise (Calvin, Institutes IV.11.1–2).

on discipline.


IV.12.5–10 — Ends and proportionality of discipline

Calvin identifies several ends: honour of Christ,

protection of the community, warning, repentance, and

restoration. Severity therefore cannot be the sole

success metric (Calvin, Institutes IV.12.5–10).

Polycarp’s treatment of the fallen presbyter Valens

joins the gravity of wrongdoing to the duty of

restoration. He condemns covetousness, prays for true

repentance, and instructs the church to recall the

offenders as straying members. Presbyters must

themselves act with compassion and avoid rash

judgment because they too stand under a debt of sin.

Calvin’s discipline develops this pastoral order:

                           461
correction protects the body by seeking the recovery of

the member, and the minister’s judgment remains

The power of the keys is fundamentally connected to

Discipline is an application of that authority to the

Calvin gives discipline several purposes: preventing the

IV.13.1–7 — Lawful self-binding

Calvin permits vows but subjects them to God’s Word,

vocation, and prudence. A promise cannot create moral

goodness in an act already forbidden or foolish (Calvin,

Institutes IV.13.1–7).

Psalm 50 joins payment of vows to thanksgiving and

calling upon God in trouble. The vow belongs within

dependence upon the Lord who delivers and receives

the resulting honour. Ecclesiastes requires the promised


                           476
act to be performed and warns that a promise made to

God cannot be treated as disposable speech. Voluntary

undertaking creates a genuine obligation, and the

gravity of that obligation supplies a reason for

deliberate restraint before undertaking it (Psalm 50:14–

15; Ecclesiastes 5:4–5; Calvin, Institutes IV.13.1–7).

Calvin consequently examines the lawful scope of the

act promised, the person’s capacity, and the end served

by the undertaking. A vow cannot authorize conduct

God forbids, nor can a promise create capacities God

has not granted. Its force belongs to faithful self-

binding within an antecedent divine rule. The

distinction prevents religious solemnity from

converting an impossible or unlawful undertaking into a

superior form of obedience. Self-constraint is legitimate

only inside the admissible state-space established by the

higher-order norm.


                           477
Monastic vows become unlawful when their

undertaking contradicts vocation, exceeds the

promisor’s capacity, or claims a superior righteousness

through self-imposed difficulty. The act of promising

cannot create the power to perform, and an

ecclesiastical designation cannot authorize an

undertaking God forbids. Calvin tests the vow through

its subject, object, and end. Lawful self-binding

requires those conditions together (Calvin, Institutes

IV.13.1–7).

Cross-reference: See III.19 and IV.10.

His extended attack on monastic vows is therefore not

merely an attack on ascetic seriousness. Calvin argues

that perpetual vows, especially when attached to a

conception of meritorious religious perfection, can bind

consciences beyond Scripture and distort Christian

vocation. The controversy again returns to jurisdiction:


                           478
who may bind the conscience, and on what authority?

(Calvin, Institutes IV.13.8–21).

Monastic vows become controversial because Calvin

believes they institutionalize constraints as though those

constraints constituted a higher perfection binding the

conscience before God. His objection is therefore not

merely psychological severity. It is that a lower-order

rule set claims normative status at the level of the

divine reference. The local rule overwrites the divine

reference under which it receives its authority.

A perpetual vow removes future options from an agent

whose knowledge of future circumstances is

incomplete. Changes in vocation, need, and capacity

expose the danger of an undertaking that forecloses acts

later required by duty. The vow remains accountable to

God’s commands through those changes. Its binding

force cannot authorize neglect of a superior obligation


                           479
or manufacture a capacity the promisor lacks (Calvin,

Institutes IV.13.1–7).

IV.14.1–6 — Sign, seal, promise

Divine institution joins an external sign to the promise

it confirms. The element receives its sacramental office

through that appointment, and the believer receives its

saving object through faith under the Spirit’s operation.

The relation fixes sign, source, and reception together.

Material administration cannot supply an independent

power to confer grace, while the reality of the promise

prevents the sign from becoming an empty ceremony.

Calvin’s definition follows the gift through the

embodied means appointed to seal and confirm it

(Calvin, Institutes IV.14.1–6).

Augustine explains sacramental constitution through

the Word joined to the element. Without the Word,

water remains water; joined to the Word, it becomes the
                           481
sacrament. He then locates its cleansing efficacy in the

Word believed, distinguishing the passing sound from

its abiding spiritual operation. Calvin’s definition

receives this Augustinian order directly. The external

sign bears God’s promise, and faith receives the reality

communicated through that promise. The element’s

sacramental use is established by divine address

(Augustine, Tractates on John 80.3).

Genesis 17 joins the covenant promise to circumcision

as the appointed sign borne by Abraham and his

household. God establishes the relation and commands

its visible marking. Romans 4 identifies Abraham’s

circumcision as a sign and seal of the righteousness of

faith already possessed before circumcision. The

temporal order fixes the sign’s office: it confirms the

promise and the received righteousness without

generating either through its physical administration


                           482
(Genesis 17:7–14; Romans 4:9–12; Calvin, Institutes

IV.14.1–6).

The sign confirms the same divine gift proclaimed in

the Word. Its embodied presentation answers the

weakness of the recipient through another appointed

mode of reception, keeping the promise available to

sight, action, and remembrance as well as hearing. The

communication remains one in its object while

differentiated in its instituted means. Its efficacy

therefore follows the whole relation: God appoints, the

promise identifies Christ and his benefits, the Spirit

makes reception effective, and the believer’s faith is

strengthened. The material element acquires no separate

source of saving power through its confirming office.

Historical and theological location. Augustine’s

IV.14.9–17 — Spirit and efficacy

Calvin avoids both automatic efficacy detached from

faith and an empty symbolism in which the sign does

nothing. The Spirit gives the sacrament its true efficacy

according to divine promise (Calvin, Institutes IV.14.9–

17).

Augustine distinguishes receiving the sacrament visibly

from receiving Christ spiritually. A person can press the

sacramental sign with his teeth while lacking the mutual

indwelling through which Christ’s flesh and blood give

life. Such reception brings judgment rather than

nourishment. Calvin’s account of sacramental efficacy

develops this distinction: the sign is truly administered,

                           484
and saving participation requires the Spirit’s work

uniting the recipient to Christ. The difference lies in the

reception of the promised reality (Augustine, Tractates

on John 26.18).

First Corinthians identifies the one Spirit as the agent

through whom believers are baptized into one body and

made to drink of one Spirit. The unity includes different

social and ethnic conditions, so the church’s common

life arises from a divine action that reaches across those

distinctions. The sacramental language belongs within

incorporation into Christ’s body and participation in the

Spirit’s gift (1 Corinthians 12:12–13; Calvin, Institutes

IV.14.9–17).

The external administration and the inward

communication must therefore be distinguished without

separating their appointed relation. The sign has its

public, instituted use; the Spirit effects the communion


                           485
that gives its saving reception life. Calvin excludes both

the transfer of divine efficacy into an independently

operating object and the removal of the sign from the

Spirit’s chosen means. The sacrament truly serves

incorporation and nourishment because its operation

remains dependent upon the divine agent who

communicates Christ. The sign is a dependent

instrument: causal efficacy is real but not autonomous.

Calvin’s sacramental account preserves both the

appointed sign and the Spirit’s effective communication

of the promised good. Institution gives the sign its

office; the promise fixes its content; and the Spirit

makes its reception fruitful in faith. An account that

assigns independent efficacy to the element reverses

that order. An account that empties the sign of its

appointed communication destroys the operation Christ

instituted for the nourishment of his people (Calvin,

Institutes IV.14.9–17).
                           486
Cross-reference: See IV.17.

Calvin defines a sacrament as an external sign by which

the Lord seals on our consciences the promises of his

good will toward us in order to sustain the weakness of

our faith, while we in turn attest our piety toward him.

Word and sacrament therefore belong together. The

sacrament does not invent a promise; it visibly confirms

the promise already given in the Word (Calvin,

Institutes IV.14.1).

Calvin rejects both magical efficacy and bare

symbolism. The sign must not be confused with the

thing signified, yet God truly uses the sacrament as an

instrument. The Holy Spirit alone gives inward

efficacy, but this does not make the external sign

empty. This pattern repeats an organisation already

present elsewhere in Calvin: a secondary instrument is

real and effective precisely because its efficacy depends


                           487
upon divine action rather than autonomous power

(Calvin, Institutes IV.14.9–17).

Calvin recognizes baptism and the Lord’s Supper as the

two sacraments instituted by Christ for the Christian

church. His dispute with Rome over the number of

sacraments is consequently a dispute over definition,

institution, promise, and ecclesiastical authority, not an

arbitrary preference for the number two.

Calvin’s definition of sacrament as sign and seal

establishes coordinated confirmation through

differentiated channels. The Word gives the promise

Word and sacramental sign communicate the same

The Spirit makes the promise operative in the believer

belongs to the actual saving communication of Christ.

The promise reaches the embodied believer through

verbal proclamation and instituted sign. The two

differentiated acts of reception; their coordination

IV.15.1–6 — Baptism’s continuing use

Calvin treats baptism as a once-given sign whose

promise can be recalled throughout life. Its meaning is
                          495
not exhausted by the psychological state of the moment

of administration (Calvin, Institutes IV.15.1–6).

The Didache places baptism under the triune name and

directs its administration through water, with practical

accommodations for the water available. The identity of

the ordinance remains stable across those

accommodations. Augustine explains its efficacy

through the Word joined to the element. Together these

witnesses supply the patristic order Calvin develops:

baptism is Christ’s instituted sign, its administration

obeys his command, and its benefit rests upon the

divine promise rather than a particular physical

circumstance of washing (Didache 7, Augustine,

Tractates on John 80.3).

Romans 6 describes baptism through participation in

Christ’s death and burial, then gives that participation

its ethical consequence in walking in newness of life.


                           496
The sign’s meaning reaches beyond the moment of

washing into the continuing life of the person marked.

Titus places salvation under divine mercy and identifies

the washing of regeneration and renewal with the Holy

Spirit poured out through Jesus Christ. The saving

source is therefore the mercy of God acting through the

Mediator and Spirit (Romans 6:3–4; Titus 3:4–7;

Calvin, Institutes IV.15.1–6).

Baptism supplies an enduring reference to that

promised relation. Its institution can be recalled amid

later repentance and weakness because its divine

meaning is not recomputed from every subsequent

emotion. The recollection directs the believer toward

Christ’s death and risen life, whose benefits the Spirit

communicates. This enduring use coordinates

sacramental memory with continuing renewal while

leaving regeneration’s efficacy with God. A historical

initiating event can remain an active reference in later
                           497
corrective cycles through institutional and personal

memory.

Baptism’s continuing use rests upon the durability of

the promise it seals. Its administration occurs once, and

the believer returns repeatedly to its testimony in

repentance and confidence. That return does not require

a new initiation because the promise already given

remains directed toward Christ. Calvin’s account joins

the completed sacramental act to its recurrent reception

across the believer’s life (Calvin, Institutes IV.15.1–6).

Cross-reference: See III.2 on faith and IV.14 on

sacramental efficacy.

At IV.15.1–6, baptism functions as a nonrepeatable

initiating mark B 0 whose promise remains available to

later memory M t . Recurrent appropriation therefore

takes the form Recall( B 0,t), not repeated initialization

B t . This distinguishes the once-given sign from the

                            498
believer's repeated return to what the sign signifies and

seals.


IV.16.3–6 — Covenant continuity and infant

baptism

The infant-baptism argument turns first on the

continuity of the Abrahamic covenant and its inclusion

of believers’ children. Calvin then relates circumcision

and baptism as covenant signs across differing

administrations (Calvin, Institutes IV.16.3–6).

Augustine expressly includes infants in his account of

the Word’s sacramental efficacy. Their inability to

articulate faith does not exclude them from baptism’s

administration within the believing church. The child’s

reception rests upon the divine Word ministered in that

ecclesial setting. Calvin’s defense of infant baptism

develops the covenantal ground of this inherited

practice: the promise governs the inclusion of children,

                           499
and the sign is administered on the authority of God’s

commitment to his people (Augustine, Tractates on

John 80.3).

Genesis 17 expressly extends the covenant promise to

Abraham’s seed and commands the sign for the male

children of his household. Their inclusion is established

by the promise’s scope before their ability to articulate

faith. Romans 4 then distinguishes the sign from the

righteousness it seals, preventing physical reception

from becoming the cause of acceptance. Colossians

places circumcision made without hands and burial

with Christ in baptism within the believer’s

participation in his death and resurrection. The saving

substance is located in Christ across the change of sign

(Genesis 17:7–14; Romans 4:11; Colossians 2:11–12;

Calvin answers objections concerning the absence of an

IV.17.1–5 — More than memorial

Calvin insists that the Supper truly communicates

Christ to believers. The sign does not merely trigger

recollection of an absent benefit (Calvin, Institutes

IV.17.1–5).

The Didache gives thanks for spiritual food and drink

and eternal life freely bestowed through Jesus Christ.

Its Eucharistic prayer asks that the scattered church be

gathered into God’s kingdom. Communion is thus a
                         510
divine gift nourishing a gathered people toward their

promised end. Augustine identifies the reception of

Christ’s flesh and blood with abiding in Christ and

having Christ abide in the believer. Calvin’s account of

real participation develops these affirmations: the

Supper communicates the Savior’s life and strengthens

the communion of those who receive him in faith

(Didache 9–10, Augustine, Tractates on John 26.18–

19).

Paul identifies the cup and bread as communion in

Christ’s blood and body and immediately draws the

corporate consequence: the many are one body because

they partake of one bread. Participation in Christ

establishes a shared life whose effects include mutual

belonging. His account of the institution in First

Corinthians 11 joins remembrance and proclamation of

the Lord’s death to discerning reception, with judgment

falling upon abuse of the holy meal. The meal carries a
                           511
promise and a responsibility that bare recollection

cannot exhaust (1 Corinthians 10:16–17; 11:23–29;

Calvin, Institutes IV.17.1–5).

Calvin’s real participation accounts for both effects.

Christ nourishes the faithful through the appointed

signs, and the communion received binds them to the

body he gathers. The same promise can be encountered

through an unworthy act whose outcome is judgment

rather than nourishment. The difference concerns the

receiver’s relation to Christ, while the institution’s

meaning remains fixed by the Lord who gives it. The

sacrament is an actual coupling relation whose source

remains Christ rather than the recipient’s act of

recollection.

Calvin rejects a Supper reduced to remembrance

detached from real communion. The ordinance

nourishes believers through participation in Christ,


                            512
whose body remains in heaven. The Spirit establishes

the effective relation between the ascended Savior and

the gathered recipients. Remembrance, instituted signs,

sacramental act whose source remains Christ (Calvin,

IV.17.10–12, 31–33 — The Spirit and the ascended

body

Calvin preserves the true locality of Christ’s human

body in heaven while insisting upon real participation

through the Spirit. This is where Book II Christology

directly governs Book IV sacramentology (Calvin,

Institutes IV.17.10–12, 31–33).

Augustine’s distinction between visible sacramental

reception and spiritual eating establishes the rule for

understanding the participation promised. The life-

                           513
giving reception is mutual indwelling in Christ.

Ignatius’s insistence that the Eucharist confesses the

flesh that suffered and was raised identifies the Savior

received: communion belongs to the genuinely

incarnate and risen Lord. Calvin holds these

affirmations together through the Spirit’s agency. The

believer receives the true Christ, and the saving manner

of reception is spiritual communion with him

(Augustine, Tractates on John 26.18–19; Ignatius, To

the Smyrnaeans 6–7).

Acts 3 identifies heaven as the place receiving Christ

until the appointed restoration. Colossians directs

believers toward the Christ seated at God’s right hand.

First Corinthians nevertheless describes present

communion in his body and blood. These affirmations

                            514
3:1; 1 Corinthians 10:16; Calvin, Institutes IV.17.10–

The Spirit communicates the ascended Christ to

believers whose earthly location remains distinct from

his bodily place in heaven. Communion therefore

preserves the humanity confessed in Book Two

together with the effective divine power through which

participation occurs. The bread’s dimensions do not

become the dimensions of Christ’s body, and the

recipient’s true participation does not require their local

identification. The same personal Savior is received

through spiritual union. Distinction of place and

identity remains within the relation whose efficacy

joins the believer to him.

The ascended body’s heavenly location and the

believer’s real reception remain jointly necessary in

Calvin’s account. The Spirit communicates Christ


                             515
without dispersing his body through the elements.

Reception occurs through faith, so outward eating does

The Lord’s Supper is the sacrament in which Calvin’s

Bread and wine are signs, but the Supper is not merely

believers, and they truly participate in his body and

Calvin locates the mode of communion in the Holy

The Eucharistic controversy also displays Calvin’s

what it communicates is Christ himself.

The Supper communicates Christ through the Spirit

IV.18.1–7 — Once-for-all sacrifice

Calvin’s central objection is that a propitiatory Mass

assigns a recurrent sacrificial function to the church that
                           523
compromises the finality of Christ’s unique priestly

offering (Calvin, Institutes IV.18.1–7).

The Didache calls the Lord’s Day assembly to break

bread, give thanks, confess transgressions, and seek

reconciliation so that its sacrifice is pure. The sacrificial

language belongs to the worship of a reconciled people

receiving and thanking God for his gifts. Its Eucharistic

prayers celebrate the spiritual nourishment freely given

through Christ. Calvin’s distinction between Christ’s

completed saving sacrifice and the church’s sacrifice of

thanksgiving develops this liturgical order. The

congregation’s offering responds to the Savior’s gift

and expresses the communion that gift establishes

(Didache 9–10, 14).

Hebrews distinguishes Christ’s priesthood from

repeated offerings through the completion of his own

sacrifice. He offers himself once, enters by his own


                            524
blood having obtained eternal redemption, and appears

to put away sin through that offering. The recurring

sacrifices of the earlier order testify to their inability to

perfect the worshippers; Christ’s one offering supplies

the decisive reversal. The continuing work described is

intercession by the living Priest (Hebrews 7:25–27;

9:12, 26–28; 10:10–14; Calvin, Institutes IV.18.1–7).

Calvin’s objection to a propitiatory Mass follows this

distribution of functions. The completed offering

establishes reconciliation, intercession sustains the

people’s access, and the Supper communicates and

confirms the benefit. Assigning a renewed propitiatory

act to the congregation transfers the function of the

accomplished sacrifice into a recurrent ecclesial

operation. Thanksgiving retains its proper place as the

church’s response to the gift whose saving ground

remains Christ’s finished work. A completed causal

ground can be repeatedly communicated without being
                         525
repeatedly produced. Recurrent access is not recurrent

origination.

The Council of Trent’s Eucharistic and sacrificial

definitions belong to the immediate mid-sixteenth-

century context, although Calvin’s polemic also

responds to older medieval theology and practice.

Cross-reference: See II.15.6 and II.16.5–7.

Calvin’s attack on the Mass centers upon sacrifice. He

believes the New Testament presents Christ’s self-

offering as unique and sufficient and therefore rejects

the idea that the eucharistic rite is a propitiatory

sacrifice offered by a priest for the living and the dead.

The objection is consequently not first about

ceremonial style but about the finality of Christ’s

priestly work (Calvin, Institutes IV.18.1–7).

Calvin can speak positively of the church’s sacrifice of

praise and thanksgiving and of believers offering

                            526
themselves to God, but he sharply distinguishes such

sacrifices from a repetition or continuation of the

atoning sacrifice. The argument returns to the priestly

office established in Book Two: Christ’s sacrifice is

sufficient because the mediator is sufficient (Calvin,

Institutes IV.18.13–18).

The sacrificial controversy is represented as a question

of whether a completed event is being treated as

requiring recurrent causal supplementation. Calvin

reads Hebrews and the New Testament witness as

establishing a terminal sufficiency condition for Christ's

propitiatory sacrifice: S C is complete such that no

additional propitiatory input ΔS is required. His

objection to the Mass is that a propitiatory construal

introduces recurrent additions to what Scripture

declares once-for-all.




                           527
This does not make the Supper causally empty. Calvin

distinguishes repetition of sacrifice from repeated

communication of the benefits of the sacrifice. The

source event is nonrepeatable; participation is recurrent.

The completed sacrifice supplies benefits

communicated repeatedly through the instituted Supper.

That distinction allows Calvin to affirm frequent

Eucharist while rejecting repeated propitiation.

The difference also explains his positive language of

thanksgiving. The church's response is output generated

by received grace, not an input that completes Christ's

deficient work. Praise, self-offering, and charity are

therefore feedback in the creaturely life of gratitude,

while atonement remains the accomplished ground

from which that life proceeds.

Repeated celebration communicates the benefits of

Christ’s completed offering. Calvin’s objection reaches


                           528
the further claim that the recurrent rite possesses a

propitiatory office through which the saving sacrifice is

reproduced or supplemented. The consequence would

change the ground of reconciliation by assigning an

accomplished mediatorial act to the church’s continuing

administration. The Supper instead returns believers to

the same finished work and nourishes their participation

in its benefits. Repetition belongs to reception and

confirmation, while the expiatory source retains its

once-for-all completion (Calvin, Institutes IV.18.1–7).

Christ and E t a eucharistic celebration at time t.

Calvin's position requires that E t point to,

communicate, and participate in benefits grounded in C

without satisfying C t =C repeated at every celebration.

The once-for-all act functions as a fixed causal and

covenantal reference whose effects are communicated


                            529
repeatedly; the rite does not regenerate the sacrificial

source.

This distinction specifies a temporal organisation: a

nonrepeatable initiating event can govern a series of

later recurrent operations. Constitutions, calibrations,

covenants, and initialization events often have this

structure. Calvin's theological claim specifies the

unique content: the initiating event is Christ's self-

offering, and its sufficiency forbids the church from

treating its recurrent rite as a new propitiatory

production.

His positive language of sacrifice—praise,

thanksgiving, self-offering—shows that he is not

eliminating sacrificial vocabulary from Christian life.

He is partitioning the category. Let S p denote

propitiatory sacrifice and S t the sacrifice of

thanksgiving. Calvin's restriction is S p={Christ's once-

                            530

This also explains why the controversy is inseparable

from priesthood. If the eucharistic minister performs a

new propitiatory sacrifice, the minister's office acquires

a causal function Calvin believes belongs uniquely to

Christ's priesthood. The dispute therefore concerns the

distribution of efficacy within the ecclesial system.

Calvin relocates the minister from producer of

propitiation to minister of a promise and sacrament

whose efficacy depends upon Christ and the Spirit.

The argument again reveals the larger architecture:

Christ's unique office establishes a boundary condition

for church office. The church may administer what

Christ institutes; it may not reproduce the mediatorial

act that constitutes the ground of its own existence. The

                           531
regulator is regulated by the event and Word from

which its authority derives.

IV.19.1–37 — Classification by institution and

promise

Calvin examines confirmation, penance, extreme

unction, clerical orders, and marriage by their


                             532
institution, promise, sign, and appointed recipients.

Baptism and the Supper belong to the whole church.

Legitimate ordination has its particular sacramental

office in the appointment of ministers; the multiplied

orders and expiatory priesthood under dispute do not

possess that office. Marriage remains a divine

ordinance without acquiring a sacramental sign of

saving grace. Each judgment follows the rite’s actual

commission (Calvin, Institutes IV.14.20; IV.19.1–37).

James directs the sick to summon the elders, who pray

and anoint in the Lord’s name, and attributes recovery

to the Lord’s action in response to prayer. The passage

establishes pastoral care through petition; it does not

identify the rite as a universal seal of saving

righteousness administered to every believer. Ephesians

describes marriage through the union of husband and

wife and then identifies the great mystery in relation to

Christ and the church. The comparison explains
                          533
marriage’s significance through the saving union; the

word “mystery” by itself supplies no institution of a

sacramental sign and promise (James 5:14–15;

Ephesians 5:31–32; Calvin, Institutes IV.19.1–37).

Calvin’s classification follows the function Christ

appoints rather than the solemnity of a practice or the

breadth of a translated term. A lawful ceremony, useful

ministry, or sacred relation can retain its proper good

without receiving the same office as baptism and the

Supper. The distinction preserves the specific promise-

bearing function through which those two ordinances

confirm faith. A classification system fails when

surface similarity substitutes for necessary predicates.

Calvin’s dispute is partly a dispute over type definition

and institutional authority to create types.

Peter Lombard’s sevenfold sacramental scheme and its

later scholastic reception are central historical


                            534
background; Trent formalized the seven-sacrament

position against Protestant reduction.

Confirmation first tests the difference between

accompanying their confession. Those acts give the

accountable expression. His objection reaches the claim

that episcopal anointing confers an additional grace

indispensable to becoming a complete Christian. That

claim assigns to a newly asserted sacramental operation

what the baptismal promise already gives in Christ. The

rite’s solemnity cannot supply the missing divine

and tests the later ceremony against that operation.

Repeating an outward gesture without the appointed

gift does not reproduce the act’s original office. The

narrative must establish the enduring institution claimed

for the later rite, including its recipient, promise, and

effect. Where those elements are changed, the asserted

continuity has preserved appearance while altering the

operation. Calvin’s positive account of catechesis

and prayer continue through their own proper ends

Penance returns the sacramental classification to the

indispensable instrument of forgiveness (Calvin,

The claim also fails when the sorrow or confession of

the penitent is treated as though it were the external

sign of a newly instituted sacrament. Repentance

belongs to the recipient’s turning, while the instituted

sign belongs to the means God appoints to

communicate and seal his promise. Moving the

recipient’s response into the place of that sign changes

the relation under examination. The ministry of

absolution remains a declaration of the Gospel to the

penitent; the penitent’s changed conduct remains the

fruit of the grace received. Their reality does not

authorize their reassembly into a new sacramental class

(Calvin, Institutes IV.19.15–17; III.3.1–5).

Extreme unction requires a separate judgment because

its supporting passage concerns the sick and the

ministry of prayer. James directs elders to pray over the

sick person and anoint in the Lord’s name. Calvin

identifies the healing gift exercised in the apostolic

setting and rejects the conversion of its sign into a

perpetual instrument for those at the point of death. The

later rite changes both the timing of administration and

the effect claimed for it. James addresses the sick who

seek the Lord’s raising; the disputed administration

waits upon extremity and attaches a different

sacramental office to the oil. The citation therefore

must carry the actual operation attributed to it (James

5:14–15; Calvin, Institutes IV.19.18–21).




                           539
Oil receives its significance through the Word and the

appointed act, not through a power resident in the

material independently of that appointment. The Lord’s

care for the sick continues through prayer and

providential help. The continuation of divine care

supplies no inference that every outward sign formerly

accompanying a particular gift remains a universal

sacrament. Institution fixes the sign’s commission, the

promise fixes its object, and the appointed operation

fixes the effect being confessed. Calvin’s rejection

therefore preserves the care of the sick while denying

the manufactured sacramental account of its material

presentation (Calvin, Institutes IV.19.19–21; IV.14.9–

17).

Orders must be examined through the office conferred.

Calvin rejects the multiplication of clerical degrees

justified by a catalogue of gifts or an asserted heavenly

hierarchy. Distinct names acquire ecclesial legitimacy
                          540
through an actual commission and useful ministry; their

enumeration cannot generate those functions. The

deacon’s care, the teacher’s instruction, and the pastor’s

ministry must be judged by the work entrusted to them.

A hierarchy whose ranks cease to correspond to

commissioned acts has converted offices into positions

detached from their ends. Book Four’s earlier account

of differentiated ministry supplies the criteria by which

that multiplication is judged (Calvin, Institutes IV.19.22

–27; IV.3.1–9).

The priestly office claimed for the sacrifice of the Mass

introduces a further violation. Christ’s expiatory

priesthood accomplishes reconciliation through his own

once-for-all offering. An ordination which confers

power to offer another propitiatory sacrifice assigns a

minister the office belonging to that completed work.

Calvin instead locates the presbyter’s commission in

preaching the Gospel, administering the appointed
                         541
mysteries, and feeding the flock. The entrusted act

communicates Christ and his benefits; it does not create

a successor to Christ’s unique expiatory priesthood. The

error concerns the ministry’s causal office before it

concerns the ceremony used to confer it (Hebrews 7:23

–28; 10:10–14; Calvin, Institutes IV.19.28–30; IV.18.1

–7).

Calvin expressly accepts the laying on of hands in true

and lawful ordination as sacramental. That

acknowledgment follows the same criteria which

condemn the Roman construction: Christ appoints a

real ministry, the sign is directed to that ministry, and

the promised gift concerns its faithful exercise. The

saving promise is not converted into power to repeat

propitiation. The recognition consequently preserves

the difference between baptism and the Supper as

sacraments belonging to the whole church and the sign

marking the appointment of ministers to a particular
                        542
office. A sacramental description of lawful ordination

supplies no validation of the sacrifice and clerical

powers Calvin rejects (Calvin, Institutes IV.14.20;

IV.19.28, 31).
