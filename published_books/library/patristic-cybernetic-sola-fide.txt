A Cybernetic Analysis of Source, Reception, and the Causal Order of

                                     SOLA FIDE IN THE APOSTOLIC FATHERS



Abstract
The doctrine of sola fide is often treated either as a sixteenth-century slogan or as one item
within a larger catalogue of Protestant distinctives. This paper argues that it is more
fundamentally a claim about the causal and operational order of justification. The righteousness
by which the sinner stands before God originates wholly outside the sinner, is grounded in Jesus
Christ, and is received through faith; good works arise within the justified person as fruits and
manifestations of that prior gift, but never enter into its ground. The Apostolic Fathers do not
supply the full vocabulary of later Reformed dogmatics, yet Clement of Rome, Polycarp of
Smyrna, the Epistle to Diognetus, the Epistle of Barnabas, and Ignatius of Antioch repeatedly
preserve this underlying asymmetry between divine source and human reception. Cybernetic
analysis identifies that asymmetry as an invariant causal architecture. Because cybernetics
analyzes relations of source, communication, control, reception, feedback, and output across
materially distinct systems, it can reconstruct the organization of the theological claims without
reducing their substance to mechanics. A system may receive perturbations, process
communications, generate responses, and manifest transformed behavior, but it cannot convert
its own downstream operations into the external source from which its governing condition
arose. The resulting formal distinction among source, instrument, governing condition, and
output exposes the precise disagreement between the Reformed doctrine of justification and the
Tridentine account of cooperation, inherent righteousness, and merit. The argument is formalized
by distinguishing the ground of justification, the instrument of reception, the renewed state of the
recipient, and the works produced within that state. The question is not whether grace precedes
works, nor whether saving faith bears fruit, but whether anything produced within the justified
person becomes co-causal in the divine judgment by which that person is accepted as righteous.
Keywords: sola fide; justification; Apostolic Fathers; cybernetics; systems theory; Clement of Rome; Niklas
Luhmann; Council of Trent

I. Introduction
The Christian confession that salvation is accomplished by grace through faith rests upon a
determinate order: God acts, Christ accomplishes, the gospel announces, faith receives, and the
justified person thereafter lives and works from a standing that he did not create. Scripture places
the righteousness of justification outside the sinner, in Jesus Christ, and presents faith not as a
meritorious achievement but as the means by which Christ and his benefits are received. 1 The
Reformed confessions therefore describe faith as the “alone instrument” of justification while
insisting, with equal force, that such faith never remains barren in the person justified. 2 This
double assertion is not an unstable compromise between grace and moral seriousness. It
distinguishes two orders that must never be confused: the ground upon which God justifies and
the life that proceeds from the justified state.
     That distinction is the subject of this paper. The central claim is that sola fide identifies the
causal grammar of justification. The ground of the sinner’s acceptance before God is wholly
external to the sinner, for it consists in the obedience and satisfaction of Christ; faith is receptive
and instrumental, for it lays hold of a righteousness it does not manufacture; and works are
consequent, for they arise from the renewed life that justification neither confuses with itself nor
leaves fruitless. If works, dispositions, infused qualities, or movements of the will are admitted
into the ground of justification, even as subordinate co-causes enabled by grace, the governing
asymmetry is altered. The receiver becomes in part the producer of the standing he receives.
     This claim will be developed by reading the Apostolic Fathers cybernetically, not by placing
a historical argument beside a separate systems-theoretical argument. Clement of Rome,
Polycarp of Smyrna, the Epistle to Diognetus, the Epistle of Barnabas, and Ignatius of Antioch
do not merely provide historical data to which a foreign model is later attached. Their own
distinctions among divine will, Christ, faith, salvation, obedience, and good works already
present relations of source and reception, communication and response, condition and output.
Cybernetics reconstructs and tests those relations as a single causal architecture, preventing them
from being flattened into an undifferentiated appeal to grace.
    The analysis therefore moves through the Apostolic-Father texts themselves. When Clement
excludes deeds performed in holiness of heart from justification while commanding good works,
the question is how a received condition becomes operative without becoming internally
grounded. When Polycarp moves from divine grace to rooted faith and consequent obedience,
the question is how an initiating communication generates continued operations. When
Diognetus locates the righteousness of the many in the righteous one, the question is how

It concerns the operational relation among them.

II. Scripture, Formal Causality, and Apostolic-Father Exegesis
Two methodological clarifications are necessary. First, a case from the Apostolic Fathers for sola
fide need not depend upon the implausible claim that every early Christian author possessed a
complete Protestant doctrine of justification, expressed it with later precision, or maintained it
consistently in every passage. Historical theology asks what conceptual distinctions a text makes,
what causal order its rhetoric presupposes, and how apparently competing statements function
within the author’s argument. Clement provides the clearest and most sustained witness; the
other authors show that the same asymmetrical pattern was neither isolated nor unintelligible
within early Christian discourse.
     Second, cybernetics is not introduced as a second argument laid beside the Apostolic
Fathers, nor as a metaphorical substitution for theology. It is the formal mode of analysis by
which their distinctions are reconstructed and tested. Cybernetics studies organization across
materially different systems; therefore, identity of causal relation does not require identity of
substance. A human person need not be a thermostat, compiler, or communications network for
the same formal distinctions among external source, internal operation, governing condition,
feedback, and output to remain valid. Luhmann’s distinction between psychic and social systems
reinforces rather than restricts this method, because it shows that cybernetic analysis types
systems and operations without confusing their material bearers. 3 The governing relations are
exact: an operation generated within a system is not identical with an operation originating

A. The Scriptural Causal Sequence
The governing order is first supplied by Scripture, for the Apostolic Fathers are witnesses to the
reception and continuation of an apostolic gospel rather than independent sources of revelation.
Romans 3:21-28 places the righteousness of God in relation to Jesus Christ, his blood, and the
divine act of justifying. Faith receives this righteousness, while boasting is excluded and works
of the law are denied any place in the sinner’s justification. Romans 4 then makes the causal
distinction still more severe. The worker receives wages as debt; the one who does not work but
believes in the God who justifies the ungodly receives righteousness by imputation. David’s
blessed person is the one to whom God counts righteousness apart from works. 4 The opposition
is not merely between ceremonial and moral works, nor between graceless works and works
assisted by grace. Paul opposes a wage relation grounded in the subject’s performance to a gift
relation grounded in God’s justifying act.
     Philippians 3:8-9 identifies the content of the contrast. Paul refuses a righteousness of his
own derived from the law and seeks instead the righteousness that comes through faith in Christ,
the righteousness from God on the basis of faith. Second Corinthians 5:21 locates that
righteousness in union with the sinless Christ who was made sin for his people, so that in him
they become the righteousness of God.5 These texts establish both source and content. The
righteousness is not an improved moral state offered upward by the renewed subject. It comes
from God, is found in Christ, and becomes the believer’s standing through a receptive relation to
him.
    Ephesians 2:8-10 supplies the temporal and operational sequence in its most compact form.
Salvation is by grace through faith, not from the recipients and not from works, because it is
God’s gift. The same recipients are then described as God’s workmanship, created in Christ
Jesus for good works prepared beforehand for them to walk in. The text excludes works from the
acquisition of salvation and includes them in the purpose of the new creation. It therefore
establishes an ordered transition:

B. Causal Offices and the Governing Constraint
Four terms will recur within the Apostolic-Father readings. The source is that from which
justifying righteousness originates. The ground is that on account of which God declares the
sinner righteous. The instrument is the means by which the sinner receives and rests upon that
righteousness. The output is the renewed obedience that follows from the received relation. In
Reformed theology, God is the source, Christ’s obedience and satisfaction are the ground, faith is
the instrument, and good works are the output. The Westminster Confession accordingly
excludes not only works but “faith itself, the act of believing” from the righteousness imputed to
the sinner.7 These cybernetic terms formally map the relations among grace, imputation, union
with Christ, sanctification, and perseverance. They make the causal office of each doctrine
explicit and prevent one office from being verbally transferred into another.
     The decisive point is that causal offices are typed rather than interchangeable. Faith is
necessary as instrument without becoming any part of the ground. Works are necessary as
consequent operations without becoming either ground or instrument. Sanctification is necessary
as the Spirit’s renewal of those united to Christ without becoming the formal content of the
justifying verdict. To say that each element is necessary in some respect does not place all of
them within one undifferentiated causal function. A steering wheel, fuel, engine, destination, and
driver may all be necessary to a journey, but they are not therefore causes in the same sense.
     The governing constraint can now be stated precisely. Let denote the obedience and
satisfaction of Christ, faith as exercised by the subject at time , the subject’s justified standing,
the subject’s sanctified condition, and the works generated within the renewed life. Let denote

C. Operational Closure, Communication, and Feedback
Luhmann’s account of operational closure supplies the first governing relation. A system exists
by reproducing its own operations and by maintaining the difference between itself and its
environment. In social systems, communications reproduce communications. 8 Closure, however,
does not mean isolation. External events can affect a system, but they become operative only as
the system processes them according to its own structures. 9 This is precisely why an externally
grounded condition may become internally active without becoming internally produced. The
point will not be established in abstraction and then applied to Clement. It will appear within
Clement’s own movement from God’s will, to faith, to righteous activity.
     Communication and structural coupling supply the second relation. Luhmann treats
communication as a synthesis of information, utterance, and understanding, with acceptance or
rejection following as a connected selection. 10 He likewise uses structural coupling to describe

backward into the ground of justification.
    Every Apostolic-Father passage will therefore be read through the same integrated question.
Where is the source located? What is communicated or bestowed? By what mode is it received?
What operations follow within the believer or the church? Do those operations manifest and
continue the given relation, or are they transferred backward into the condition of acceptance?
This is not a cybernetic chapter followed by an historical chapter. It is a cybernetic reading of the
causal order asserted by the Apostolic Fathers themselves.

III. Clement of Rome: Source, Reception, and Visible Output

addresses a concrete ecclesial crisis in Corinth, where rivalry and pride had led to the unjust

an antinomian tract would fail before reaching its central chapters. Yet the very density of

attainment as source, piety as source, and even deeds performed “in holiness of heart” as source.
His antithesis is not between wicked works and sufficiently holy works, nor between works done
without grace and works elevated by grace. The works he excludes are already described in their
strongest religious form. The positive source is God’s will, and the stated mode is faith.
Whatever further precision later theology must supply, Clement has drawn a boundary around
the justificatory ground and placed the subject’s noblest internal operations outside it.
     Operational closure clarifies the force of the exclusion from within Clement’s own
sequence. The Corinthians can produce speeches, decisions, factions, acts of humility, acts of
repentance, and deeds performed in holiness of heart. Those are genuinely their operations. They
cannot, by reproducing any of them, produce the divine will by which the saints are glorified or
the righteousness on account of which God justifies. Clement does not deny that righteousness
becomes operative within the believer. Abraham “wrought righteousness and truth through
faith,” and the Corinthian church is summoned to the works appropriate to its calling. He denies

B. The Grammar of 1 Clement 32.4
The Greek syntax of 1 Clement 32.4 makes the cumulative nature of the exclusion unmistakable.
The sentence begins with a participial clause: dia thelematos autou en Christo Iesou klethentes,
“having been called through his will in Christ Jesus.” Before Clement names either faith or
works, he locates the calling in God’s will and in Christ. He then establishes an antithesis by
means of ou … oude … alla: not through ourselves, nor through our wisdom, understanding,
piety, or works, but through faith. The preposition dia with the genitive is repeated across both
sides of the contrast, so Clement is not merely listing bad attitudes and recommending a better
one. He is distinguishing incompatible channels within a single account of justification.17
     The sequence is cumulative. Ou di’ heauton excludes the self. Oude dia tes hemeteras
sophias e syneseos e eusebeias excludes intellectual and religious qualities belonging to the
subject. E ergon hon kateirgasametha en hosioteti kardias then excludes works actually
performed “in holiness of heart.” Finally, alla dia tes pisteos supplies the positive term. The
relative clause does not qualify the excluded works as hypocritical, merely external, or
performed in open rebellion against grace. Clement chooses language that gives those works
their strongest conceivable moral description and nevertheless places them on the negative side
of the antithesis.
     Two verbal features deepen the contrast. The present passive dikaioumetha, “we are
justified,” makes the recipients the objects rather than the producers of the justifying act. The
closing relative clause then names God as the active agent who “justified” all from the
beginning, using edikaiosen. The grammar therefore moves from divine will, to the calling in
Christ, to the exclusion of the recipient’s internal resources, to faith, and finally to Almighty God
as the one who justifies. Clement does not present faith as a rival achievement that succeeds
where piety and holy works failed. Faith is the receptive means ordered toward the divine agent
and the Christic location already named.
     The claim should not be inflated beyond what the syntax bears. The repeated dia does not,
by itself, furnish a complete scholastic taxonomy of efficient, formal, meritorious, and
instrumental causes. Nor does Clement explicitly define imputation in the vocabulary of the later
Reformed confessions. It does, however, establish a severe negative boundary. Whatever
theological account is built from this sentence must explain why the called Christian’s own

C. Faith as Reception Within Clement’s Order
Clement’s repeated phrase “through faith” identifies the point at which the external source
becomes operative within the human subject without becoming a human production. The gospel
announces what God has done in Christ, and the Spirit-wrought act of faith understands, accepts,
and relies upon that announcement. In Luhmann’s terms, communication becomes operative
through understanding, after which acceptance or rejection can occur. This supplies the formal
structure of reception required by Clement’s grammar. Human understanding does not create the
divine verdict; it receives or rejects a communication whose source and content remain external
to the recipient. Faith is therefore the event of reception, not a supplement added to the
communicated righteousness.
     Structural coupling sharpens the same relation. A system may remain dependent upon an
external condition while producing its own internal response to that condition. Network
authentication instantiates this formal structure with particular clarity: a device presents
credentials issued by an authority beyond itself. Its act of presentation is real and necessary for
participation, but it does not issue the credential, define the authority, or validate itself. The
material difference between a device and a believer does not alter the organizational invariant.
Clement excludes precisely those forms of self-authorization that religious man is most likely to

     The distinction between sensor and actuator makes the point at the level of causal office. A

by contrast, are active operations within the renewed life. Clement can therefore attribute
righteousness and truth to faith, command moral action, and still deny that either faith as an
internal act or works as internal outputs constitute the righteousness by which God justifies.

D. “Justified by Works, Not by Words”
The obvious objection comes from 1 Clement 30.3, where believers are urged to join those to
whom grace is given, “being justified by works and not by words.” 18 A paper that ignored this
statement would not offer an Apostolic-Father argument but a collection of selected quotations.
The phrase must be read within both its immediate exhortative setting and Clement’s own later
exclusion of works from justification.
    In chapter 30 Clement rebukes boasting, impurity, backbiting, and empty speech. The
contrast is between verbal profession and visible conduct within the community. Works justify in
the sense that they demonstrate, vindicate, or display the reality of the believer’s confession
before others. In chapter 32, by contrast, Clement asks how the saints are glorified and how
human beings are justified in relation to God. There he excludes works, including holy works,
from the ground. The difference is not an artificial Protestant rescue. It is demanded by
Clement’s use of the same vocabulary in distinct rhetorical settings. One passage concerns the
observable integrity of profession; the other concerns the source of the sinner’s righteous
standing.
     Cybernetically, the two uses concern different causal locations. Works in chapter 30 are
observable outputs within the ecclesial system. They permit the community to distinguish a
living confession from empty speech because an accepted communication normally continues
through congruent operations. Justification in chapter 32 concerns the source and ground of the
relation itself. The output can disclose whether the relation is operative, but it cannot travel
backward in the causal order and become the source that established the relation. Clement’s
vocabulary remains consistent once source and manifestation are not collapsed into one category.
    Modern scholarship has rightly emphasized that 1 Clement 32.4 must be read together with

    The transition into chapter 33 is logically important. Clement asks whether believers should
become idle or cease from doing good because justification is through faith rather than holy
deeds. His answer, me genoito, rejects the inference with the same kind of emphatic denial Paul
employs in Romans 6. The denial presupposes the distinction it guards. If chapter 32 had merely
excluded works done before grace while admitting later works into justification, chapter 33
would not need to answer the charge of idleness in this form. The objection arises precisely
because Clement has made the exclusion appear absolute. His response is not to qualify the
exclusion, but to locate good works in the calling and pleasure of God.20
     Clement’s appeal to creation further strengthens the causal sequence. God rejoices in his
works; the ordered creation displays his will; human beings, made by him, are therefore to set
themselves zealously to every good work. Creation does not earn the Creator’s identity, nor does
the creature’s imitation establish the divine command to which it responds. In the same way,
Christian works belong to the fitting activity of those created and called by God. Chapter 33
assigns them necessity, beauty, and purpose while leaving the source of justification where
chapter 32 placed it.

E. Feedback, Continuation, and Holy Works
The relation between chapters 30, 32, and 33 can therefore be stated as a relation among source,
continuation, feedback, and output. God’s will calls and justifies. Faith receives. The believer’s
life continues through operations consonant with the call, and those operations become visible
within the ecclesial system as humility, obedience, charity, and peace. Those visible operations
also feed back into the life of the church by correcting scandal, strengthening communion, and
displaying the credibility of its confession. Empty speech without such output is exposed as false
because a claimed relation that never becomes operative is not credible. Yet the diagnostic and
regulatory value of the output does not convert it into the source.

create the reference merely by responding to it. Clement’s moral exhortation serves that
regulatory function. It identifies deviation, calls the Corinthians back to the divine order, and
demands operations consistent with their calling. The exhortation would lose its force if
Christian conduct were irrelevant. It would lose its gospel if the corrective operations became the
ground of divine acceptance.
     Clement’s argument is especially damaging to any account that attempts to preserve an
exclusive divine ground merely by placing grace before cooperation in time. He does not say that
works performed before grace fail while works performed after grace enter the ground. He
excludes deeds accomplished in holiness of heart. In computational terms, the runtime may
display the structure under which it operates, but runtime activity does not compile the condition
of its own possibility. This is the same organizational constraint Clement states theologically.
Holy works execute within the justified life; they do not generate the justification from which
that life proceeds. The issue is not chronological priority but causal category.
     Clement’s language of reward in chapters 34 and 35 does not alter that category. The
faithful are summoned to ordered service, promised the good things prepared for those who wait
upon God, and warned that disobedience incurs judgment. These declarations make conduct
eschatologically serious. They do not identify conduct as the righteousness through which God
justifies. Within the cybernetic analysis, promises and threats operate as governing signals within
a relation already constituted by divine calling. They direct, correct, and stabilize conduct toward
the end God appoints. A reward can be genuinely consequent upon an operation without that
operation becoming the source of the status under which the reward is bestowed.

IV. Convergence and Counterevidence in the Apostolic Fathers
A. Polycarp: Grace, Divine Will, and Consequent Obedience
Polycarp of Smyrna reproduces the same order with striking concision. Addressing the
Philippians, he speaks of the faith that has taken root among them and bears fruit unto Christ,
then reminds them that “by grace you have been saved, not by works, but the will of God
through Jesus Christ.”21 The sentence is saturated with external causality. Salvation is by grace,
not works; it proceeds from God’s will; it comes through Jesus Christ. Faith is already present as
the rooted relation that bears fruit.

    Polycarp proceeds immediately to exhortation. Believers must serve God, keep his
commandments, pursue righteousness, and avoid error. As in Clement, the movement from grace
to works is neither embarrassment nor contradiction. It is the expected continuation of an
externally initiated relation. The rooted faith bears fruit because it lives from Christ; the fruit is
not substituted for the root. Polycarp’s sequence therefore conforms to the same causal
architecture: grace establishes, faith participates, and obedience follows.
     This sequence is best understood as initiation followed by recursive continuation. The divine
will through Christ supplies the originating condition that the believer does not produce. Faith
marks the living relation to that source. Once established, the relation reproduces conduct
appropriate to itself through repeated acts of obedience, repentance, endurance, and love. Those
acts can strengthen habits, correct failures, and shape future action, so they are causal within the
Christian life. They remain downstream of salvation’s source. The feedback by which fruit
encourages further fruit does not cause the root from which the whole process lives.

B. The Epistle to Diognetus: Alien Righteousness and the Sweet Exchange
The Epistle to Diognetus supplies the clearest early statement of the external content of
justification. Its ninth chapter describes God giving his Son as ransom, “the Holy for the lawless,
the innocent for the guilty, the righteous for the unrighteous.” 22 The author then asks what could
cover sin except Christ’s righteousness, and in whom the lawless could be justified except the
Son of God alone. The culmination is the famous exchange in which the righteousness of the one
justifies the many lawless.
     Here the distinction concerns not only the source of salvation but the righteousness by which
sinners are justified. The many do not generate a collective righteousness and offer it upward.
Their lawlessness is covered by the righteousness of another. The justifying content is therefore
exogenous to the sinner and complete in the Son. Faith and trust arise as the fitting response to
the revelation of God’s kindness, but the response does not produce the exchange. God gives the
Son; the righteous one supplies the righteousness; the lawless receive the benefit.
     Cybernetically, the passage establishes an invariant distinction between participation and
origination. The righteousness of the one becomes determinative for the standing of the many
without becoming an achievement produced by the many. A one-to-many relation is established
from the righteous source toward the lawless recipients. Each recipient may thereafter produce
new operations, confessing, worshiping, obeying, and loving, but none of those operations
travels backward to become the righteousness of the one.

prevents a crude account of this exchange. Christ’s historical obedience is not inserted into the

C. Barnabas: Belief, Hope, and Immediate Reception
The Epistle of Barnabas repeatedly places life and salvation in trust directed toward Christ.
Those who hear and believe live forever; those who do not set their hope upon him cannot be
saved.23 In the author’s interpretation of the bronze serpent, the wounded person comes, believes
that what appears dead can give life, and is saved immediately. 24 The typology is not a systematic
treatise on justification, but its operational order is plain. Life resides in the divinely appointed
object; belief does not create that life but receives it.
    The immediacy of the sequence matters. No accumulation of internal merit is inserted
between belief and deliverance. The decisive distinction is between hope placed in the given
means of life and refusal of that means. In cybernetic terms, the difference is not the quantity of
output generated by the receiver but the presence or absence of a living relation to the source.
The healed person will thereafter live and act, but the act of healing proceeds from what God has
appointed, not from the patient’s production of curative power.
     Barnabas thus makes acceptance and rejection, rather than greater and lesser internal
production, the operative distinction. A communication can be understood and either received or
refused. Belief does not create the life offered in Christ any more than rejection destroys Christ
as its source. The selection determines participation. Once life is received, it produces
consequences within the recipient, but those consequences remain effects of the accepted
relation. The immediacy of salvation in the bronze-serpent typology excludes any feedback
interval in which subsequent conduct could become a co-cause of the initial deliverance.

D. Ignatius: Life in Christ and the Reward of Works
Ignatius of Antioch also locates life and resurrection in Christ as received by belief. He warns
that judgment belongs to those who do not believe in the blood of Christ, and he declares that the
Father will raise those who believe in Christ Jesus, apart from whom there is no true life. 25 Christ
is the locus of life; belief identifies participation in him. This language again places the decisive
condition outside the human subject.

have meaning. The soldier’s deposits matter because he already serves a sovereign whose
authority, commission, and promised reward he did not create. Reward belongs to the
governance of the relation, not to the generation of its source.

E. A Convergent Rather Than Merely Verbal Case
The force of these witnesses is cumulative because they approach the same structure from
different directions. Clement supplies the direct exclusion: not through ourselves, piety, or holy
deeds, but through faith. Polycarp supplies the initiating source: grace and the will of God
through Jesus Christ, not works. Diognetus supplies the alien content: the righteousness of the
one justifies the many lawless. Barnabas supplies the receptive event: the wounded person
believes in the divinely appointed source of life and is immediately saved. Ignatius supplies the
domain of participation: true life exists in Christ, and those who believe in him are raised.

F. Difficult Apostolic-Father Countertexts
A serious argument from the Apostolic Fathers must also acknowledge passages that resist easy
assimilation to sola fide. The collection is not a single treatise, its authors do not share a later
technical vocabulary, and its paraenetic texts sometimes assign striking remedial or
eschatological force to human action. The Didache commands the believer who possesses goods
to give through his hands “a ransom” for sins, and it warns that the whole time of one’s faith will
not profit unless one is perfected at the end. 28 Second Clement declares almsgiving good as
repentance from sin, says that almsgiving lifts the burden of sin, and describes the returning
Christ as rendering to each person according to his works.29 These are not trivial phrases, and
suppressing them would produce an apologetic catalogue rather than an historical argument.
     The Didache nevertheless does not offer a causal definition of justification parallel to 1
Clement 32.4. Its ransom language occurs inside the Two Ways catechesis as an urgent
command to generosity, while its eucharistic prayers thank God for faith, immortality, and
eternal life freely made known and given through Jesus. Its final warning concerns perseverance
under eschatological deception. The text therefore establishes that faith which does not endure is
unprofitable and that almsgiving has real penitential significance. It does not identify almsgiving
as the righteousness on account of which God justifies the ungodly. Cybernetically, the warning
tests whether the received relation continues under pressure; it does not specify that the
operations produced under pressure constitute the external source of the relation.

therefore answer the positive texts rather than merely pointing to the moral earnestness of the
collection as though moral earnestness itself settled the causal question.

V. Trent and Causal Reentry
A. The Strongest Roman Catholic Form of the Argument
The Roman Catholic doctrine defined at Trent should not be caricatured as the crude proposition
that unaided human works purchase salvation from an otherwise unwilling God. Trent condemns
the claim that fallen man can be justified by works without grace, affirms the necessity of
prevenient divine action, and teaches that even meritorious works are performed in Christ
through the assistance of the Holy Spirit. 31 The modern Catechism of the Catholic Church
similarly declares that divine initiative precedes, prepares, and elicits the human response, and
that all human merit is itself due to God. 32 These qualifications are real. They establish that the
disagreement is not between grace and the total absence of grace.

justification itself. Trent anathematizes the denial that the will, moved by God, cooperates in
preparing and disposing itself for justification. It rejects justification by faith alone when that

B. Prevenient Grace and the Reentry of Output
From the standpoint of the governing constraint, the appeal to prevenient grace does not resolve
the dispute. An externally supplied initial condition can enable a system to generate operations
that later feed back into the maintenance or alteration of that condition. If those operations
become co-causes of the standing itself, the system is no longer merely receiving and
manifesting what was given. It participates in producing the condition under which it will finally
be accepted.
     This is the precise point at which the Tridentine and Reformed architectures diverge. In the
Reformed order, sanctification and works necessarily follow justification and may affect
countless relations within the Christian life, but the forensic ground remains fixed in Christ. In
the Tridentine order, inherent righteousness and works performed in grace belong to the increase
and final possession of justifying righteousness. The believer’s internal state becomes materially
relevant to the verdict. The output is fed backward into the ground.
      To call such operations “grace-enabled” does not restore the lost distinction. A cause does
not cease to be causal because a prior cause enabled it. If God enables the will to cooperate and
then treats that cooperation as a constituent factor in justification, the human operation remains a
co-cause, however subordinate and derivative. The controversy is not over whether God is the
first cause of salvation in some general sense. It is whether Christ remains the entire meritorious
ground of the specific judgment called justification.

C. Merit as Feedback into the Ground
The contrast becomes sharpest in Trent’s canons 24 and 32. Canon 24 denies that good works are
merely fruits and signs of justification and insists that they preserve and increase it. Canon 32
teaches that the justified truly merit eternal life through works performed by grace in Christ. 36

holiness of heart. Clement’s holy deeds remain outside the ground of justification; Trent’s grace-
enabled works enter into the increase and final attainment of the justified state.
     The Reformed response does not deny that works are more than inert signs. They are real
acts of obedience produced by the Holy Spirit, and they genuinely serve God and neighbor. Nor
does it deny reward. It denies that the value and causality of works extend into the ground of
justification or establish a meritorious title to eternal life. The Belgic Confession captures the
distinction by affirming that God rewards good works because grace crowns his own gifts, while
insisting that believers do not found their salvation upon them.37
     Cybernetic language exposes why the difference cannot be dissolved by a verbal agreement
that “everything is grace.” If the same term includes both the external act by which God accepts
the ungodly and an internal quality by which the renewed person becomes worthy of further
grace and final life, then the decisive question remains unanswered. What operation grounds the
verdict? Where is that operation produced? Does the receiver’s output modify the condition of

VI. Objections and Replies
A. Does Cybernetic Analysis Reduce Persons to Machines?
The first objection is that the systems-theoretical analysis reduces salvation to engineering and
the human person to a machine. The objection commits a category error by confusing
organizational identity with material identity. Cybernetics does not require two systems to
possess the same substance before their causal relations can be formally compared. Logic applies
to stones, angels, contracts, and propositions without declaring them materially identical;
likewise, cybernetics can identify the same relation between source and receiver, or between
governing condition and regulated response, wherever that relation occurs. Personal agency,
divine freedom, and spiritual communion determine the nature of the operations involved. They
do not abolish their causal order.
    The theological realities specify the concrete operations being analyzed. God freely creates
and sustains both person and relation; Christ’s righteousness is personally and covenantally his;
the Spirit regenerates a responsible human being. Cybernetics then determines how those
operations are organized: which operation originates beyond the recipient, which is received,
which is internally produced, which functions as feedback, and which supplies the governing
condition. Its formal authority is precisely the capacity to expose a transfer of causal office,
especially when an effect is moved into the place of its cause or an internally generated output is
made constitutive of the external ground.

B. If Faith Is an Internal Operation, Why Is It Not a Work?
The second objection argues that faith, since it occurs within the believer, must itself violate the
governing constraint. The answer requires the established distinction between instrument and
ground. Faith is internal as an act of the renewed person, but external in its object and non-
meritorious in its office. It does not constitute the righteousness imputed, and it does not
persuade God to regard an otherwise incomplete Christ as sufficient. It receives and rests upon
Christ precisely by renouncing every claim grounded in itself.
     Moreover, Reformed theology does not treat faith as an autonomous operation produced
from the resources of fallen nature. Faith is the gift of God, wrought by the Holy Spirit through
the Word. The cybernetic account therefore includes divine agency at the level of the receiver
itself. God not only provides the external ground but creates the receptive capacity. Monergism
does not eliminate the believer’s act of faith; it explains why that act can be real without
becoming self-originating merit.


                                                                                                 23
                                   SOLA FIDE IN THE APOSTOLIC FATHERS



     The distinction can be tested by substitution. If the act of believing were the righteousness
upon which God justified, then stronger faith would supply a stronger ground and imperfect faith
would imply an imperfect Christ. The confessional doctrine denies the inference. Weak faith and
strong faith lay hold of the same complete Savior. Their subjective effects differ, especially in
assurance and stability, but the ground they receive does not vary with their internal intensity.

C. Do Apostolic-Father Commands and Reward Passages Defeat Sola Fide?
The third objection appeals to the abundance of Apostolic-Father exhortations, warnings,
conditional statements, and promises of reward. These texts defeat only a doctrine that equates
sola fide with moral indifference. The Reformed claim has never been that works are
unnecessary in every sense. It is that they are unnecessary and inadmissible as the ground or
instrument of justification. They remain necessary as fruit, vocation, obedience, evidence, and
the ordained path of the regenerate life.
    This is why Clement can deny justification through holy works and immediately command
every good work. It is why Polycarp can say salvation is by grace rather than works and proceed
to demand obedience. It is why Ignatius can locate true life in Christ received by belief while
promising reward for faithful labor. The apparent contradiction arises only when every statement
about salvation, reward, holiness, and final judgment is compressed into a single undifferentiated
causal category.

D. Is the Argument Anachronistic?
The fourth objection is historical. The Apostolic Fathers did not confront Trent, distinguish
imputed from infused righteousness with later precision, or formulate sola fide as the Reformers
did. Therefore, it may be argued, their texts cannot support the doctrine.
     The premise is true and the conclusion does not follow. A later doctrine may render explicit
a distinction already operative in earlier texts. The doctrine of the Trinity employs terminology
whose conciliar precision developed through controversy, yet that development does not prevent
earlier texts from bearing genuine trinitarian structures. Likewise, a first-century author need not
anticipate every scholastic category in order to distinguish divine source from human work,
Christ’s righteousness from human lawlessness, and faith from deeds performed in holiness of
heart.
     The argument of this paper is deliberately structural. It does not claim that the Apostolic
Fathers were confessional Protestants born fifteen centuries early. It claims that several of the
earliest post-apostolic texts preserve the causal asymmetry that the Reformation later defended
against a system in which internal righteousness and grace-enabled works entered the

justificatory ground. That narrower claim is historically defensible and theologically
consequential.

E. Does Clement Exclude Only Works Performed Before Grace?
The fifth objection supplies the most direct Roman answer to 1 Clement 32.4. Clement may be
excluding only works arising from unaided nature or works performed before the divine calling.
If so, the passage would remain compatible with the Tridentine claim that no one merits initial
justification, while leaving grace-enabled cooperation, inherent righteousness, and subsequent
merit untouched.
     The wording does not support the proposed restriction. Clement’s subject is “we” who have
been called through God’s will in Christ Jesus. The excluded list includes piety and deeds
actually performed in holiness of heart. The text does not say “works before grace,” “works of
nature,” or “works apart from Christ.” Nor does it move from an initial stage in which faith alone
receives justification to a later stage in which holy works preserve and increase it. Instead, it
extends the same divine pattern from the patriarchs to Christians and attributes God’s
justification of all from the beginning to faith.
     The counterreading remains possible only by importing a later initial-versus-increased
distinction into the sentence. That may show how Clement can be accommodated to Trent, but
accommodation is not derivation. The historical question is which architecture Clement’s own
antithesis naturally supplies. Its explicit terms locate divine will, Christ, faith, and God’s
justifying act on the positive side, while placing the called subject’s wisdom, understanding,
piety, and holy works on the negative side. That architecture is closer to the Reformed exclusion
than to Trent’s formal and progressive account of inherent justice.

F. Does Grace-Enabled Causality Cease to Be Human Causality?
The sixth objection argues that because every meritorious work is elicited and sustained by
grace, no human contribution competes with God. The objection is correct if “competition”
means an independent natural power acting without divine assistance. It fails if the question is
whether an internal human operation enters the causal function at all. A secondary cause does not
cease to be a cause because a primary cause enables it. Trent itself insists that the works are not
only God’s gifts but also the genuine merits of the justified person.
     The formal test avoids a dispute over rhetoric. If, with divine grace and Christ’s merit held
constant, a variation in the subject’s works changes the increase of justice or the merited
attainment of eternal life, then those works possess causal relevance to that outcome. Calling
them gifts of grace explains how the operations become possible. It does not relocate them
outside the subject or convert them into Christ’s historical obedience. The whole point of the

                                    SOLA FIDE IN THE APOSTOLIC FATHERS



Tridentine doctrine is that the renewed person truly cooperates and truly merits. The Reformed
objection takes that claim seriously.
     Consequently, the issue cannot be resolved by saying that God crowns his own gifts.
Reformed theology can use the same Augustinian language for gracious reward while denying
merit as a title to justification or eternal life. The disputed question remains the office assigned to
the crowned work. If it is rewarded within the order of a salvation wholly grounded in Christ, the
source-output distinction remains intact. If it preserves or increases justifying righteousness or
merits the attainment of eternal life, output has entered the acceptance function.

G. Does Counterevidence Elsewhere in the Collection Defeat the Thesis?
The final objection appeals to the Didache, Second Clement, and other morally severe writings in
the collection. Their language of ransom, repentance, perseverance, almsgiving, and judgment
according to works can sound closer to later penitential theology than to a Reformed confession.
This evidence defeats a claim of uniform dogmatic precision across the Apostolic Fathers. The
present argument does not require that claim.
     Historical presence and historical unanimity are different propositions. To refute the
assertion that sola fide is a sheer sixteenth-century invention, one need not prove that every post-
apostolic author articulated it consistently. One must demonstrate that its constitutive causal
distinction appears in genuinely early witnesses and explain those witnesses without evacuating
their language. Clement’s explicit exclusion, Polycarp’s grace-not-works formula, and
Diognetus’s righteousness of the one cannot be cancelled merely because another early sermon
speaks imprecisely or differently about almsgiving.
     The countertexts also clarify what the positive case can and cannot establish. They show that
the collection contains diverse paraenetic idioms and that later theological development cannot
be reconstructed as a straight line from a unanimous first-century formula. They do not show that
Clement meant the opposite of what his cumulative antithesis says. The proper conclusion is
therefore neither universal consensus nor historical absence, but the demonstrable early presence
of the architecture later defended as sola fide.

VII. Conclusion
The doctrine of sola fide is not exhausted by the statement that faith is important, that grace
comes first, or that Christ makes salvation possible. It protects a definite order in which the
righteousness of justification originates outside the sinner, is complete in Christ, and is received
through a faith that contributes nothing to its ground. Good works follow with necessity because
the faith that receives Christ is living and the Spirit who grants it renews the person. Yet no

vigor, holiness, or necessity belonging to those works permits them to migrate backward into the
basis of the divine verdict.
     The Apostolic-Father evidence considered here does not merely furnish isolated phrases that
resemble Protestant slogans. Clement of Rome supplies the controlling distinction by excluding
the self, wisdom, understanding, piety, and deeds performed in holiness of heart from
justification, then ordering believers toward vigorous good works. Polycarp assigns salvation to
grace and divine will through Christ while describing faith as a root that bears fruit. The Epistle
to Diognetus locates justifying righteousness in the righteous one whose righteousness covers the
lawless. Barnabas places life in the divinely appointed object of faith, and Ignatius places true
life in Christ while treating works as the faithful operations of those already enlisted in his
service.
     Read cybernetically, these Apostolic-Father claims disclose a single causal order rather than
a collection of isolated Protestant-sounding phrases. Clement distinguishes source, reception, and
visible output. Polycarp shows externally initiated life continuing through recursive obedience.
Diognetus distinguishes participation in another’s righteousness from origination of one’s own.
Barnabas frames salvation through acceptance rather than internal production, while Ignatius
locates reward and endurance within a relation already constituted in Christ. Across the texts, an
externally grounded condition becomes internally operative without becoming internally
produced; reception generates response without producing the received object; feedback
regulates conduct without creating its reference; and output evidences and continues a relation
without becoming its source. Cybernetics formally reconstructs this order, renders its causal
offices explicit, and subjects competing theological accounts to the same counterfactual test.
Once source, ground, reception, state, feedback, and output are distinguished, the Apostolic-
Father order and the Tridentine order cannot be treated as verbal variations within one
architecture. They assign internal operation to incompatible causal offices.
      Trent affirms grace, rejects unaided human merit, and assigns divine initiative to the
beginning of salvation. Nevertheless, by making cooperation necessary to preparation for
justification, inherent charity part of justifying righteousness, and grace-enabled works causes of
its increase and the merit of eternal life, it crosses the boundary that sola fide exists to preserve.
The difference is not whether grace assists human action. It is whether human action, however
assisted, becomes part of the reason for which God accepts the sinner as righteous.
     The formal result is therefore direct. In the Apostolic-Father architecture developed from
Clement and the convergent witnesses, variation in the recipient’s internal holiness or works
does not vary the ground of justification, because that ground remains Christ. In Trent, variation
in grace-enabled works varies the increase of inherent justice and the merited attainment of
eternal life. The systems are not distinguished merely by terminology or by different emphases

                                  SOLA FIDE IN THE APOSTOLIC FATHERS



within one shared causal order. They differ over whether output generated within the renewed
subject enters the constitutive and final function of justification.
     The Christian answer must remain as uncompromising as the order of the gospel itself. God
is the source. Christ is the righteousness. Faith receives. Works follow. To reverse that order at
any point is not to complete grace, but to relocate its ground.
