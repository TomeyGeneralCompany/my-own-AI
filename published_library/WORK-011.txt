Salvation is accomplished by Grace, through Faith. Jesus Christ, the living Son of God, was
given by God as a sacrifice for the sins of all men. Christ died on the cross to mend the
relationships between God and man which has been broken by man's sin of disobedience. Belief

origin of justification lies outside the human subject, the subject does not generate it, and
participation in it occurs through a specific mode of reception. Now, I will admit that this is one

separates us from all the other religions and cults of the Buddha, Osiris, Jupiter, Vishnu, and all
the other man made religions which have sprung up since the flood. So I suppose I should put
together a defence of the doctrine of justification, specifically by faith in Jesus Christ, by

of the pretenders, who, by the conclusion of my work, will be, proven to be nothing more than

In order to defend it, rather than appealing solely to Scripture, I am going to be building a
patristic, and cybernetic defense. But why? One of the common objections made by the followers
of the Papal Religion, to many Christian ideas, is that their teaching is the one which is in line
with the Church Fathers who were the generations of men who lived roughly from just after the
Apostles through the 8th Century or so. This clearly means, then, that the Reformation was
creating something new; the theology of Luther, Calvin, and others was innovation, and therefore
out of the bounds of orthodoxy. Specifically, Roman Catholic apologists love to claim this about
the five Solae (editor or Solas) of the Reformation. Yet, couldn’t we simply claim Sola Scriptura
and be done with their objection? Well, yes and no. Yes in the sense that we don’t need to
ultimately address their claims, because the Solae are definitively found in the Scriptures, but no,
in the sense that, if we are trying to meet them where they are at, it is best to try and use the
sources which they would be familiar with. Now, this is not going to be a simple list of Church
Fathers and their quotations, plenty of those already exist, but I intend to use their works to help
in the goal of justifying the aforementioned doctrinal statement.

Why cybernetics? Well, because the field of Cybernetics provides a unique and robust
vocabulary which is more than capable of describing such differentiation. At its core, cybernetics
is the study of systems, their structures, and the flow of information within them. Systems
maintain equilibrium through feedback loops and adaptive regulatory mechanisms. So instead of
using the scholastic categories which are very popular in Occidental apologetics, it is better to
frame the matter in terms of source and system, communication and production, reception and
generation, for these terms provide a formal vocabulary for asymmetry, dependence, and
reception.

From this follows a governing constraint. Any model that introduces human co-causality into
justification necessarily collapses the distinction between source and system, converting

reception into production, and thereby eliminating justification as externally grounded. This is
not a later objection, but a condition which the entire argument must satisfy at every point, and to
which each subsequent claim must continually return.

Accordingly, at every stage of the argument, the following must be preserved without exception.
That which grounds justification must originate outside the human system. That which occurs
within the system may receive, respond, manifest, and continue what has been given, but it may
not contribute to the ground itself. If at any point an internal operation is made co-causal, the
distinction between source and system is not merely blurred, but destroyed, and the entire
structure fails.

The human subject, if he or she is considered as a system, cannot generate the ground of its own
justification. If justification is real, then its origin must lie beyond the system’s internal
operations. The field of cybernetics provides a precise language for that claim without collapsing
into older metaphysical debates about substance or habit.

\

So how does Sola Fide work in this lens then? In Luhmannian terms, the distinction is between
system and source: justification does not arise from the internal operations of the human system,
but from what is communicated to it from beyond itself.

Luhmann defines a system not by substance, but by operation. A system is constituted by the
ongoing reproduction of its own operations, and these operations are self-referential. As he
states, “systems are operationally closed in the sense that they produce and reproduce the
elements of which they consist by means of the elements of which they consist.” This means that
nothing originating outside the system can be produced by the system itself. If justification were
an internal product, it would have to arise from the system’s own operations. But if it is
communicated from beyond, then it belongs not to the system’s production, but to its relation to
an environment.

Luhmann further clarifies that “operational closure does not mean isolation.” A system, in spite
of any boundaries which it nessicarily constructs and opperates within, remains dependent upon
its environment, but this dependence takes the form of reception, which is structured by the
system itself. The system cannot generate what comes from outside, but it can be structurally
coupled to it. Faith, in these terms, is not an internal productive act that creates our justification,
but instead is the mode of coupling by which the human system receives what it cannot, due to
the aforementioned system boundaries, internally produce. This may be likened to network
authentication. The system does not generate its own authorization credentials as a valid ground
of access, but receives and presents what has been issued by an external authority. Access is
granted not because the system produces its own legitimacy, but because it is recognized as
properly coupled to a source beyond itself. If one were to assert that the system contributes to its
own authorization as a co-cause, the analogy collapses, for the system would then be both the
issuer and validator of its own credentials, destroying the distinction between authority and
participant.

Clement of Rome states this directly and without qualification. So for a very basic run down,
Clement of Rome writes from the church in Rome to the church in Corinth, the same Corinth that
had received the Apostle Paul. His letter addresses a real crisis, rampant disorder, pride, and the
unjust removal of presbyters, and he answers it by grounding the church again in the doctrine of
God’s work, not man’s. In his first epistle, he writes that the saints, quote, “were glorified and
magnified, not through themselves or their deeds or righteous actions which they accomplished,
but through his will.” (1 Clement 32:3). He then removes every possible human ground of
justification: “we are not justified through ourselves, or through our wisdom or understanding or
piety or deeds which we accomplished in holiness of heart, but through the faith by which all
those since the beginning the Almighty God has justified.”(1 Clement 32:4), a claim which
stands in direct opposition to Roman Catholic dogma surrounding Salvation, which was
established at the Council of Trent, which declares, “If any one saith, that by faith alone the
impious is justified…in such wise as to mean, that nothing else is required to co-operate in order
to the obtaining the grace of Justification, and that it is not in any way necessary, that he be
prepared and disposed by the movement of his own will; let him be anathema” (Session 6, Canon
9); Protestants affirm that a justifying faith works through love, so that is not where the debate
lies, but rather whether, after receiving justification through faith, such works allow us to “merit
for ourselves…all the graces needed to attain eternal life” (CCC 2027), and Clement teaches that
justification and glorification are by faith apart from our own piety or holy deeds, such that the
Council of Trent, 1450+ years later, anathematizes him for doing so. The exclusion is total. Not
only sinful works, but even “deeds…accomplished in holiness of heart” are denied as the basis of
justification.

\

This is the decisive structural claim which my defense is built around, that the human system
does not generate the condition of its own justification. In Luhmannian terms, justification is not
an internally produced communication arising from the system’s own operations. It is received.
Faith, therefore, is not a co-causal input alongside works, but the mode by which the system is
opened to those things ( or thing ) which lie beyond the scope of its operational closure. A
thermostat clarifies this at the level of operation, it does not generate heat, nor does it contribute
energy to the system it participates in. Its function is to register and respond to a condition which
is sourced elsewhere. If it is not coupled to that source, no internal activity produces heat. This
may be further clarified through the distinction between source voltage and signal: the signal
may carry information, modulate behavior, and regulate response, but it does not itself generate
the power it conveys. Likewise, sensors and actuators must be distinguished. A sensor registers a
condition external to itself, while an actuator produces an effect within the system. Faith
functions as a sensor rather than an actuator. It receives and registers what is given, but does not
generate the underlying reality. If faith were made co-causal, it would cease to function as a
sensor and become an actuator, thereby transforming reception into production and collapsing
the distinction between what is given and what is generated. Likewise, Clement’s exclusion of all
works—even those “accomplished in holiness of heart”—establishes that no internal operation of
the human system becomes the generative ground of justification.

For Luhmann, this follows directly from the distinction between system and environment, and
this distinction must be maintained without exception if the governing constraint is to hold. “The
unity of the system is constituted by the difference between system and environment.” The

system exists only by maintaining this distinction. If the ground of justification were internal, the
distinction would collapse, because the system would then be the source of what is supposed to
come from beyond itself. The claim that justification is by faith alone preserves this distinction:
the system remains a receiver, not a generator, of its own ground.

Luhmann defines communication as a synthesis of three selections: information, utterance, and
understanding. Crucially, communication is not transmitted as a substance but occurs only when
it is received and understood within a system. As he writes, “communication comes about only
through understanding.” This means that what is communicated does not become operative
within the system unless it is received. Faith, therefore, is not an additional contribution to
justification, but the event of reception in which the communicated righteousness becomes
effective within the system. If faith is treated as contributing to the ground itself, then it ceases to
be reception and becomes production, and the governing constraint is violated.

This corresponds to Luhmann’s concept of structural coupling. Systems cannot import operations
from their environment, but they can be coupled to external conditions in a way that allows
environmental events to trigger internal operations. “Structural coupling restricts the range of
possible structures of a system by selecting those that are compatible with the environment.”
Faith functions in this precise way: it does not produce the content of justification, but it is the
structure through which the system is made receptive to it.

The same epistle attributes righteousness to faith in continuity with Scripture: “Was it not
because he brought about righteousness and truth through faith?” (1 Clement 31:1–2).
Righteousness is not produced as a justificatory ground by works; it is brought about “through
faith.” In systemic terms, this means that righteousness enters the system through
communication, not production. It is not an output that the system generates and then presents as
its ground, but something effected through the relation established by faith. The distinction is not
between more or less output, but between whether the system is actually receiving from its
source.

Luhmann explicitly distinguishes between production and reception in communicative systems.
A system cannot produce information that originates outside of it; it can only process what it
receives according to its own structures. “Information is a difference that makes a difference,”
but that difference must be selected and processed within the system to become operative. Thus,
righteousness is not generated as an internal output and then presented as a ground; it is
introduced as a communicated difference that reconfigures the system upon reception.

Where Clement speaks of works, the context is specific to the Corinthian situation. He is
rebuking empty speech, envy, and hypocrisy within the community. The issue is not justification
before God, but justification before men. He says, “being justified by works not by words.” (1
Clement 30:3). The surrounding exhortation concerns humility, self-control, and avoiding evil
speech. The contrast is between empty profession and visible righteousness. This is not a
statement about the ground of justification before God, which he has clearly defined later in
32:4, but about the visibility of communication within the system. Works here function as
observable outputs of an already established relation, and must remain so if the governing
constraint is to be preserved. They do not generate the source, any more than a thermostat’s
signals generate the heat they correspond to.

Within Luhmann’s framework, outputs are the result of internal operations that follow from prior
communications, and therefore cannot be elevated to the level of causal ground without violating
the governing constraint. Once a system has received a communication, it reproduces operations
in accordance with that reception. Works, then, belong to what Luhmann would call the
continuation of communication within the system. They are not inputs that establish the system’s
relation to its source, but outputs that manifest the system’s already established coupling to it.
This is analogous to the distinction between compiler and runtime. The condition which allows
execution is not generated during runtime itself, but is established prior to it. Runtime behavior
manifests and expresses what has already been structured. Works, therefore, do not compile
justification, but execute in accordance with what has already been received. If they were said to
contribute to the compilation itself, then the system would participate in generating the very
condition under which it runs, rendering the distinction between prior condition and subsequent
operation incoherent.

Polycarp of Smyrna speaks in the same terms. He writes, “knowing that by grace you have been
saved, not by works, but the will of God through Jesus Christ.” (Philippians 1:3). Salvation is
attributed to grace, explicitly denied to works, and located in the will of God through Christ,
which must remain entirely external if the governing constraint is to be preserved. In systemic
terms, grace is the initiating communication. It does not arise from the human system but enters
it. The subject does not co-produce salvation; it receives what is given.

Luhmann’s theory requires that every system be triggered by events it does not produce. “No
system can begin with itself; it must presuppose its own operation as already ongoing.” Applied
here, the initiating act cannot arise from within the human system. Grace functions as that
initiating communication which the system does not originate but upon which its further
operations depend. The asymmetry is absolute: initiation belongs to the source, not to the
receiver.

\

When Polycarp exhorts believers, he does so in a conditional form that presupposes faith as the
determining factor: “if indeed we have faith.” (Philippians 5:2). The condition that governs
participation is faith, not as a productive work, but as the presence or absence of reception. It
marks whether the system is actually coupled to the communication that constitutes its life. The
call to “do his will” (Philippians 2:2) describes the operations that follow from that reception, not
the basis upon which salvation is granted.

The Epistle to Diognetus makes the ground of justification entirely external to the sinner. It asks,
“For what else than one’s righteousness could cover up our sin? In who else than in the Son of
God alone could our lawlessness and ungodliness possibly be justified?” (Diognetus 9:3–4). The
answer is exclusive: justification is in “the Son of God alone.” It then defines the mechanism:
“the righteousness of the one should justify many lawless.” (Diognetus 9:5). And again, “he
himself gave his own son, a ransom on our behalf, the Holy for the lawless, the innocent for the
guilty, the righteous for the unrighteous.” (Diognetus 9:2).

Here the structure becomes explicit. The content of justification is not generated within the
system; it is communicated from the one to the many. “The righteousness of the one” justifies

the lawless. The system does not produce its own righteousness as an internal output; it receives
righteousness from outside itself. This corresponds directly to the distinction between source and
receiver: the furnace produces heat, the thermostat participates in it. The righteousness that
justifies is not endogenous to the subject; it is exogenous and communicated.

This also aligns with Luhmann’s insistence that systems cannot transfer operations across their
boundary. What crosses the boundary is not the operation itself, but a perturbation that the
system then processes internally. The righteousness of “the one” does not become an internal
product of the many; rather, it is communicated and then received in such a way that it
constitutes their standing. The distinction between source and receiver remains intact even in
participation.

The Epistle of Barnabas presents the same structure under the language of faith, hope, and belief.
“Those who hope on him will live forever.” (Barnabas 8:5). Conversely, “unless they place their
hope upon him, they will be warred upon forever.” (Barnabas 12:2). Salvation is tied to hope in
him. The decisive act is described explicitly: “he may hope, believing that it, being dead, is able
to give life, and immediately he will be saved.” (Barnabas 12:7). The man looks, believes, and is
saved.

This immediacy shows that no internal accumulation functions as the ground. The determining
factor is whether the system receives the communication of life. Belief does not generate life; it
marks the point at which life is received. The difference is not between greater or lesser internal
production, but between reception and non-reception, for to introduce production at this point
would violate the governing constraint and relocate the ground within the system.

\

Luhmann emphasizes that reception is selective. Not every communication is accepted; systems
differentiate between acceptance and rejection. “Understanding includes the possibility of
rejection.” Faith, therefore, is not the creation of life but the acceptance of the communication in
which life is given. The difference between belief and unbelief is not productive capacity, but
whether the communication is received or rejected.

Ignatius of Antioch locates life and judgment in relation to belief in Christ. “Unless they believe
on the blood of Christ, judgment is also theirs.” (Smyrnaeans 6:1). The dividing line is belief. He
also writes that God “will likewise raise up us who believe in him in Christ Jesus, without whom
we do not have true life.” (Trallians 9:2). Life is in Christ, and it belongs to those who believe in
him.

In these terms, Christ is the locus of life, and faith is the mode by which the system participates
in that locus. Without that relation, the system remains closed to the communication that
constitutes life, and any attempt to make the system co-causal would invert this dependence and
violate the governing constraint. With it, life is present, not as an internally generated property,
but as something received and operative.

\

In Luhmannian language, this can be described as inclusion within a communicative domain. A
system participates in a given domain only insofar as it is integrated into the communications
that constitute it. Christ, as the locus of life, is not internal to the human system; participation in
him occurs through incorporation into the communication that proceeds from him. Faith is the
condition of that incorporation, not a productive contribution to its content.

Ignatius does speak of works, endurance, and obedience, as in the exhortation, “Let your works
be your war-time deposits, so that you may receive your deserved savings.” (Polycarp 6:2). But
these exhortations operate within the same framework already established: life is in Christ,
received by faith, and works belong to the operations that follow from that reception. They are
outputs within the system, not the source that grounds it, and must not be treated as such if the
governing constraint is to remain intact.

This preserves what Luhmann calls the asymmetry between system and environment. The
environment provides conditions that the system cannot produce, while the system generates its
own operations in response. To confuse outputs with sources is to collapse this asymmetry. This
may also be expressed in terms of closed-loop regulation. A system may regulate itself through
feedback, correcting deviations and stabilizing its operations, yet such regulation presupposes an
initial reference condition which the system does not generate. PID controllers, for example,
adjust behavior based on proportional, integral, and derivative terms, yet they do not create the
target state itself. In like manner, the human system may exhibit regulation, growth, and
transformation, but these remain responses to a condition externally given. If such regulation
were treated as co-causal in establishing that condition, then the feedback loop would be made
the origin of its own reference point, which is structurally impossible. The loop presupposes the
reference; it does not create it. Your argument maintains it: works are operations of the system,
while justification belongs to what the system receives from beyond itself.
