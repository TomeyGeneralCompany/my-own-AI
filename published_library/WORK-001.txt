    A MATHEMATICAL, PATRISTIC, AND LOGICAL WALKTHROUGH
   AND EXPLANATION OF THE SEVEN PETITIONS OF THE LORD’S
                         PRAYER


                                               By Isaiz James Tomey




PREAMBLE
     [1] The prayer which Christ taught His disciples is neither an inventory of private wishes nor a

ceremony by which man informs God of necessities otherwise unknown to Him. It is the divinely

prescribed ordering of the creature before his Creator. Christ, whose wisdom neither errs nor suffers

deficiency, has furnished in these few petitions the entire grammar of Christian dependence: the honour of

God, the advent of His kingdom, the obedience of His creatures, the provision of their necessities, the

remission of their sins, their preservation in trial, and their final liberation from evil. There is neither a

redundant request nor an omitted necessity. What appears brief is not therefore meagre. The Lord's Prayer

is sufficient precisely because its petitions ascend to the causes under which the innumerable particulars

of human life stand. The man who believes that he has outgrown it has merely mistaken the multiplication

of his desires for the enlargement of his wisdom.

     [2] Prayer is an act of the whole person, not the mechanical motion of lips. A mouth may confess

dependence while a heart remains enthroned in self-sufficiency, and the apparent unity of the act then

contains a profound contradiction. Scripture speaks of David crying to God with his whole heart, and of

Moses crying although the outward voice was silent; it also condemns those who honour God with their

mouths while their hearts remain far removed from Him. The distinction is not between audible and

inaudible words, but between a truthful communion of understanding, will, and speech and a counterfeit

operation in which these powers have been separated. Let the act of prayer be written P = Φ(F, I, W, S),

where faith F, intention I, will W, and spoken or inward expression S ought to converge toward God. The

symbols are not a measure of Divine favour, as though grace could be computed; they expose the

absurdity of a performance in which S continues while F and I are deliberately withheld. What is offered

in that case is the sound of prayer deprived of its creaturely answer.

      Terms. P denotes the actual act of prayer; W its spoken confession; H the inward intention of

      Consequence. The verbal act alone cannot establish the devotion which the petition requires.

Where the lips profess one end and the heart pursues another, the petition is divided within its own

operation.

     [3] The petitioner must therefore examine his intent, discipline the wandering attention, and ask

according to the grace and will of Him to whom the request is directed. Trust in God is not presumption

that every appetite shall receive its object. It is the conviction that the Father knows the fitting end of the

child better than the child knows the immediate object of his appetite. Devotion is accordingly not a fever

of emotional intensity but an intelligent and earnest conformity of the heart to the prayer uttered. The

same reasoning explains why ordinary Christians ought to pray in the language which they understand.

Words comprehended can instruct the judgment, engage the affections, and direct the will; unintelligible

syllables may be repeated while the inner man remains absent. The mother tongue is no enemy of

reverence. It serves reverence by making the petition genuinely the petitioner's own. Prayer thus begins

where the whole creature answers the God who first addressed him.


THE FIRST PETITION — OUR FATHER, WHICH ART IN HEAVEN, HALLOWED
BE THY NAME
     [4] The first words overthrow both servile despair and impious self-exaltation. Christ commands us

to call Almighty God Father. Were an earthly king to bid one of his subjects address him as son, the

subject would rightly regard the word as an extraordinary advancement; how much greater then the

adoption by which the Lord of kings receives the faithful into His household. Yet the word Father does

not make the creature divine by nature. It signifies a received relation, established through the goodness

of God and the mediation of Jesus Christ, under which the creature has grounds for confidence

concerning the inheritance prepared for him. The natural son looks to the father for those goods fitting his

formation and advancement; the adopted child of God may more securely trust that nothing profitable to

his eternal end will be withheld. The promise is not that he shall possess whatever the flesh covets, but

that paternal providence does not forget the true good of the child.

     [5] Fatherhood simultaneously reveals Divine benevolence and imposes filial obligation. Creation

and redemption manifest a goodness which precedes the creature's reciprocation; obedience, love,

humility, and readiness to keep the commandments are the fitting answers to that goodness. These

operations are neither an equal repayment nor the purchase price of adoption. The order is asymmetric:

God gives what man could never originate, and man returns the obedience of a life made possible by that

gift. The relation therefore has a determinate moral structure. To speak Father while intending to remain a

rebel is not a harmless imperfection in diction; it is the contradiction of a sonship confessed in words and

repudiated in conduct. Judas kissed the Master while betraying Him. The same disorder appears wherever

a petitioner offers filial language as a mask for settled disobedience. The word itself summons an

examination of the life that speaks it.

     [6] A man conscious of grievous sin ought not therefore to abandon prayer on the ground of his

unworthiness, nor ought he to conceal the offense beneath religious fluency. He must accuse himself

before God, acknowledge with the prodigal son that he has sinned and is unworthy to be called a son,

repent, purpose amendment, and seek the grace of reconciliation. Repentance is not the claim that the

fallen will can restore itself to innocence by an autonomous effort. It is the creature's penitent response to

the Father whose mercy alone can receive and renew him. Neither despair nor hypocrisy has any standing

here. Despair refuses the offered goodness; hypocrisy invokes it without intending any return. True

confession does neither. It calls the Father Father precisely as the sinner asks to be restored to the

obligations and privileges of the Father's house.

     [7] The pronoun Our is as consequential as the name Father. Christ has not instructed isolated

monads to say My Father, give me my bread, and forgive me my sins, as though the other members of His

household were accidental ornaments of a private salvation. The faithful are brethren by grace and

adoption, made fellow heirs in Christ and thereby bound not merely to an identical superior but to one

another. Their communion requires mutual love, aid, exhortation, and intercession. Each petitions for

others when he asks for bread, remission, preservation, and deliverance. The other is thus present within

the act by which the self addresses God. This does not abolish the singular soul, for each must answer in

his own person; it forbids the pretense that the Christian can discharge his filial obligation while

remaining indifferent to his brother's good. The common Father gives a common field of responsibility.

     [7a] The testimony of Ignatius of Antioch places this filial communion within the antecedent

counsel of God. Writing in the opening salutation of his Epistle to the Ephesians, Ignatius

describes the Church as “predestinated before the ages of time,” and as “united and elected

through the true passion by the will of the Father.” Election is therefore not presented as a

distinction subsequently manufactured by the creature, nor as a title acquired through the

perfection of his own obedience. It belongs to the prior Divine purpose through which the

Church is constituted in Christ. The communion of the elect consequently possesses both a

vertical and a horizontal order: its source is the will of the Father, its mediation is Christ, and its

creaturely actuality appears in the common life of those whom God has called. (Ignatius of

Antioch, Epistle to the Ephesians, salutation; Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene

Fathers, vol. 1.)

     [8] The words Which art in heaven do not confine the omnipresent God to a location, as a prince

might be confined to his palace. They direct the creature's desire to the place and condition of perfected

communion with Him. A loving child wishes to be where his father dwells; the Christian ought

accordingly to desire the sight of God and to order his conversation toward heavenly things rather than

surrender it to the world, the flesh, and the Devil. This longing is not contempt for created life as though

creation were evil. It is the refusal to make transient goods the final end of a creature whose inheritance

lies beyond them. The pilgrim knows the difference between the road and its destination. With the

Psalmist he laments the prolongation of exile, not because the earth has escaped Divine government, but

because the creature has not yet entered the fullness of the Father's presence.

     [9] The name of God signifies more than the letters by which He is addressed. It denotes God

Himself as manifested through power, majesty, wisdom, providence, mercy, goodness, and glory. To

hallow that name is to honour the reality signified by it in confession and conduct. Yet one must not

suppose that God's holiness is capable of increase or decrease at the hands of His creatures. He is not

made more holy when men worship Him, nor less holy when they blaspheme Him. The change belongs to

the created reception and public manifestation of a holiness eternally perfect in itself. We pray, therefore,

not for an improvement in God but for the rectification of our treatment of God. Hallowing is the proper

ordering of our knowledge, speech, worship, and action toward the One whose majesty is in no wise

dependent upon our acknowledgment.

     [9a] Theophilus of Antioch, in the third chapter of the first book of his address To

Autolycus, gives the necessary distinction an almost irresistible clearness. Of the several Divine

names he writes, “if Providence, I but mention His goodness,” and again, “if I call Him

Kingdom, I but mention His glory.” Theophilus does not imagine that the Divine essence is

exhausted by the catalogue of predicates which finite reason is able to pronounce. Rather, the

names refer the understanding to Him whose operations make His providence, goodness,

sovereignty, and glory known. To speak rightly of God is therefore not to contain Him within our

words, but to acknowledge the source disclosed through His works. The creature neither

contributes holiness to its Creator nor produces the reality which it names. It receives the

manifestation, judges its own speech and conduct by that manifestation, and returns praise to the

One whose glory existed before the praise was offered. Such is the actual force of the petition

that His name be hallowed. (Theophilus of Antioch, To Autolycus I.3; Marcus Dods translation,

Ante-Nicene Fathers, vol. 2.)

     [10] The petition consequently condemns every false object of ultimate confidence. When men trust

their salvation to charms, conjurations, witchcraft, or any creature elevated above the Creator, the disorder

is not simply an erroneous selection among equivalent religious techniques. It is the displacement of the

true source by something which possesses no independent capacity to bestow the promised good. False

doctrines and heresies likewise corrupt the reception of Divine truth and may conceal deceit beneath the

appearance of devotion. We pray for their overthrow, for the true exposition and pure proclamation of

God's word, and for those who do not believe to receive the Christian faith. Nor does doctrinal profession

exhaust the petition. Swearing falsely, deceiving a neighbour, pride, ambition, vanity, envy, malice,

avarice, adultery, gluttony, sloth, backbiting, and slander bring dishonour upon the name confessed by the

offender. A corrupted life is a public counterargument against its own confession.

     [11] In danger we must flee to the name of God as our refuge; in prosperity we must refuse the

ingratitude that credits the creature with an autonomous power of preservation. Good works and an

upright conversation ought to move observers toward the praise of the Lord, while the sins of professing

Christians must not furnish occasions for His name to be blasphemed. Confession and life thus constitute

one field of witness. It is impossible to maintain indefinitely a distinction between the holiness

proclaimed in worship and the dishonour enacted through ordinary conduct. The first petition is therefore

no ornamental preface to the requests which follow. It governs them all, for bread, forgiveness,

protection, and deliverance must ultimately be sought in a manner that returns every received good to the

glory of the Giver.


THE SECOND PETITION — THY KINGDOM COME
     [12] The second petition concerns dominion. The Devil seeks continually to draw men beneath his

tyranny through pride, wrath, envy, covetousness, lust, gluttony, disobedience, and the innumerable

deformations of human appetite. A man who calls himself free merely because he does what he wishes

has not yet asked whether his wishes themselves have become his masters. When anger governs

judgment, when avarice commands labour, or when lust determines the movement of the will, the

passions have acquired an authority to which the person submits. This is the miserable inversion at the

centre of sinful bondage: powers given for the good of man are elevated above the good they were

appointed to serve. The rule of the Devil is therefore visible not only in explicit rebellion against God but

in the inward disorder by which sin reigns over the faculties of the soul.

     [13] No sinner can emancipate himself from this tyranny by making the enslaved will the sufficient

principle of its own liberation. The prophet Hosea teaches that man's destruction proceeds from himself,

while his help is in God. We therefore petition the Father for the reign of His grace: for faith in Himself,

His Son Jesus Christ, and the Holy Ghost; for charity toward God and neighbour; and for deliverance

from infidelity, despair, malice, dissension, covetousness, and illicit desire. The kingdom comes within us

when understanding, affections, judgments, and bodily powers are drawn into the service of Divine

command rather than divided among rival claimants. It is an actual government of life, not a flattering

name placed over an unchanged allegiance. The prayer asks that God's dominion become the operative

order under which the whole man acts.

     [13a] Clement of Rome establishes the same priority of Divine operation when, in the thirty-

second chapter of his First Epistle to the Corinthians, he declares that those called by God's will

in Christ Jesus are “not justified by ourselves, nor by our own wisdom, or understanding, or

godliness, or works which we have wrought in holiness of heart; but by that faith through which,

from the beginning, Almighty God has justified all men.” The distinction, and I do not use that

term lightly, is, if I may be so bold as to put it in words, nothing short of decisive. Clement does

not assign to the creature an autonomous power by which he first makes himself acceptable to

God and afterward receives the confirmation of grace. The calling proceeds from the Divine will,

while justification is explicitly distinguished from the works of the recipient. Yet Clement

immediately exhorts the faithful to diligence in good works. Here grace and obedience are

neither rival causes nor interchangeable operations. The former establishes the order within

which the latter becomes the fitting exercise of the Christian life. The kingdom of God therefore

does not come because man has generated righteousness from his own resources; rather, the

creature is summoned to act within the government of the grace which precedes him. (Clement

of Rome, 1 Clement 32.4–33.1; Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers, vol. 1.)

     [14] The movement from sin's dominion to the kingdom of grace may be considered as an alteration

of governing orientation rather than the annihilation of natural capacities. Let x denote the creature's

presently exercised powers and let T(x) denote their direction toward an end. Sin does not manufacture a

new nature ex nihilo; it disorders the end toward which existing powers are directed. Grace does not make

sight cease to be sight or reason cease to be reason, but orders the faculties toward faithful action. Thus

the question is not merely whether x possesses activity, but whether T(x) conforms to the good for which

x was given. The mathematical distinction is philosophical, not a pretense to calculate sanctification. A

kingdom is known by what rules, what is obeyed, and to what end its subjects are ordered. The regenerate

man must accordingly ask whose command his activity actually fulfills.

     [15] The kingdom once begun must grow. Secret hatred of goodness, negligence, sloth, and the

temptation to look backward threaten the stability of the Christian life. We ask not merely for an

inaugural moment of innocence but for perseverance and increase: a firm purpose, fruitful works, fuller

knowledge of God, and advancement in charity. Saint Paul prays that believers may walk worthily, please

God, bear fruit, and increase in knowledge; he likewise bids them speak and work the truth in love. A

beginning which repudiates its own continuation has not reached the end for which it began. Christian

growth is therefore a directed and repeated operation in which grace sustains the believer's continuing

answer to God, corrects deviation, and strengthens him against renewed bondage. One cannot use the

reality of an initial gift as an excuse to neglect its cultivation.

     [15a] Polycarp of Smyrna likewise joins the priority of grace to the necessity of a life

directed toward its proper end. In the first chapter of his Epistle to the Philippians he declares,

“by grace you are saved, not of works,” and places salvation under the will of God through Jesus

Christ. Nevertheless, the same epistle exhorts the faithful toward obedience, righteousness,

endurance, and perseverance. His closing petition asks God to establish believers in faith, truth,

meekness, patience, and purity. The order is not accidental. Grace is not an isolated occurrence

whose effect expires when the recipient begins to act, but the Divine beneficence upon which the

Christian continues to depend. Neither does the continuing necessity of obedience convert

obedience into the originating cause of salvation. The gift establishes the creature's new

condition, and its continued operation directs the creature toward the fulfillment of that

condition. (Polycarp of Smyrna, Epistle to the Philippians 1.3, 12.2; Roberts–Donaldson

translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers, vol. 1.)

     [16] This petition looks beyond the present government of grace toward the kingdom of glory. The

believer asks to remain within God's mercy until the passing of mortal life brings him to the inheritance

which does not perish. Saint Paul's desire to depart and be with Christ, and his groaning for release from

the mortality of the body, disclose an end superior to the comforts of the present order. The kingdom of

grace and the kingdom of glory are not competing objectives. The first is the present rule under which the

pilgrim is prepared for the second; the second is the fulfillment toward which the first directs him. To

pray Thy kingdom come is consequently to ask both that sin cease to reign now and that the final

communion for which man was created be brought to completion.

THE THIRD PETITION — THY WILL BE DONE IN EARTH AS IT IS IN HEAVEN
      [17] The third petition reaches the question of the will itself. Adam's disobedience has left mankind

corrupted, inclined toward self-love, and unable, apart from the grace of God, to render the perfect

obedience which Divine law requires. The fallen man possesses desires and powers, yet neither the mere

presence of desire nor its inward intensity establishes its moral authority. Indeed, the most dangerous

error of the will is to mistake its own inclination for the measure of goodness. Christ therefore commands

the creature to pray for the conformity of earthly obedience to the obedience of heaven. The angels and

saints serve God gladly, without grudging, rebellion, or rivalry, because their wills accord with the

goodness of Him whom they worship. The petition asks that man likewise come to love what God

commands, not merely submit outwardly while maintaining a private kingdom of resentment within.

      [18] The will of God is not one preference among the preferences of created persons. It is perfectly

wise, just, holy, and ordered toward the true good of those whom He governs. Our task is not to reconcile

God to the fluctuating appetite of the creature, but to mortify the appetite where it opposes Divine

goodness and conform ourselves to His command and providence. Such conformity must extend through

the ordinary acts of life. Worship which does not become obedience, and obedience which is offered only

while it coincides with private inclination, both remain deficient. The creature does not become less

himself by obeying his Creator; he becomes more perfectly actual according to the end for which his

powers were given. Grace perfects the creaturely answer by correcting the disorder that sin has introduced

into it.

      [19] Patience is among the most difficult expressions of this conformity. When another man

obstructs our wishes, contradicts us, injures our reputation, or interrupts our chosen course, the immediate

response of the flesh is often anger, complaint, cursing, or revenge. Yet the frustration of my will does not

establish that God's will has been frustrated. I must therefore ask for the grace to bear contradiction

without hatred, to speak and do good even to adversaries, and to refrain from making my private

displeasure the court of final judgment. This does not deny the existence of injustice or abolish its proper

remedy. It denies to personal rancour the authority to determine the moral nature of an act. An adversary

may have truly offended, while the Christian remains bound to oppose the offense without enthroning

vengeance as his end.

     [20] Poverty, sickness, contempt, persecution, and bodily affliction can likewise become occasions

of obedience. Such sufferings are not intrinsically desirable as though pain were the final good of man;

they are conditions through which Divine providence may exercise the faithful, expose disordered loves,

and correct those who wander. The same adversity may strengthen one creature in virtue and chastise

another for vice, according to the wisdom of the Governor and the condition of the recipient. Its

theological meaning therefore cannot be reduced to a crude equation in which every affliction proves

special guilt. God orders the trials of His creatures for their good, even where the immediate experience

of that ordering is grievous. The believer must be ready to endure what is appointed without surrendering

trust in the Father whose understanding exceeds his own.

     [21] Our request reaches even to death. We pray to receive the hour of our departure without a

rebellion produced by fear, and to accept the Divine summons when mortal life is concluded. Such

willingness is not a command to seek death or disregard life; it is the final acknowledgment that life is

received and remains under God's lordship. The eyes, tongue, heart, hands, and feet must likewise be

governed in their several operations. The eye must not be the servant of envy, the tongue of malicious

rejoicing, the hand of revenge, or the heart of resentment when an adversary prospers. The question is not

whether the faculties are active, but whether their activity answers the end given to them. Thy will be

done brings the whole embodied creature beneath one obligation, from the smallest private movement to

the final surrender of mortal breath.


THE FOURTH PETITION — GIVE US THIS DAY OUR DAILY BREAD
     [22] The fourth petition descends from the government of the soul into the provisions necessary for

embodied life, without abandoning the same Divine order. Christ commands us to ask for bread, not that

man might despise material goods, but that he might distinguish necessity from the insatiable appetite for

excess. Bread signifies sufficient provision according to a person's state, vocation, and condition. It does

not authorize the claim that wealth is inherently sinful, nor does it justify the fantasy that a creature can

accumulate himself into independence of God. Saint Paul reminds us that we brought nothing into the

world and carry nothing out; food and clothing ought therefore to furnish grounds for contentment. The

man who makes superfluous possession the governing end of life exposes himself to temptations, snares,

and destructive desires. He has exchanged the means of his livelihood for an idol which no accumulation

can finally satisfy.

     [23] The wise petition asks neither destitution nor excess, lest abundance become an occasion for

forgetting God and poverty an occasion for theft and the profanation of His name. This is a judgment

about the moral ordering of possession, not a mathematical decree that every man must possess an

identical quantity. Sufficiency is relative to real duties and conditions: what is necessary to perform one

vocation may not be necessary to another. Nevertheless, necessity must not be stretched until every

appetite can represent itself as an obligation. The creature is called to steward the goods he has received

and to employ them toward fitting ends. Riches may provide means for legitimate work and service, but

they cannot supply the final ground of the person, and the desire for them becomes corrupt when it

displaces justice, gratitude, and the worship of God.

     [24] The words our daily bread also condemn those who consume the fruits of others by theft,

extortion, fraudulent craft, deceit, or predation. Bread taken by injustice cannot become righteous merely

because the thief invokes God's generosity over it. Nor does the condemnation end with obvious robbery.

Those who abandon every useful labour, industry, study, or service while presuming to live indefinitely

upon the burden imposed upon others likewise refuse the ordinary duties of their condition. A man may

work with his hands, with his mind, through instruction, administration, judgment, or another office

beneficial to the commonwealth; the form differs, but the obligation to employ received powers toward

genuine goods remains. Those invested with authority are not exempt. Office becomes a species of

robbery when its holder appropriates the advantages of rank while refusing the work for which rank was

granted.

     [25] The husbandman offers a proper image of the relation between labour and providence. He tills

the soil, sows the seed, weeds the ground, and guards the field, yet he neither creates the nature of the

seed nor commands the rain by the force of his will. He works as a genuine secondary cause while

depending upon an order which precedes, contains, and sustains his operation. So every craftsman,

labourer, merchant, scholar, and magistrate ought to exercise diligence, skill, foresight, and judgment,

while praying that God grant sufficient fruit. The error lies equally in indolence which refuses to sow and

presumption which imagines that sowing alone is the sovereign cause of harvest. Providence establishes

rather than cancels the reality of work. Prayer is not a substitute for faithful industry; it is the

acknowledgment of the source on which industry itself depends.

barns as men do, yet the Father feeds them; lilies do not spin, yet their beauty exceeds the apparel of

uncertainties as though the Father had ceased to govern His creation. Life is greater than food, and the

body greater than clothing. Seek first the kingdom and righteousness of God, and receive the necessities

of life within that superior ordering. Care becomes disordered when it ceases to be prudent stewardship

and becomes practical disbelief in providence.

     [27] There is dignity in the honest calling of the ordinary labourer. The craftsman who performs his

office faithfully may please God within that condition no less truly than one who occupies a position of

public distinction or religious prestige. What determines the goodness of the work is not worldly

splendour, but the truthful exercise of a received vocation. The hierarchy of communion does not make

every office identical; it binds each office to the good for which it exists. Honest labour therefore has its

own proper honour, and the man who despises it because its outward appearance lacks magnificence has

confused rank with worthiness. Bread is received in the commonwealth through countless differentiated

services, and the prayer's plural pronoun instructs every participant to recognize that his provision is

entangled with the lawful work of others.

     [28] The wealthy man must pray for daily bread no less than the poor man. Possession is not

preservation. A substantial estate may be destroyed by fire, water, theft, disaster, or innumerable causes

that its owner does not control. Job, enriched beyond his contemporaries, was stripped of his possessions

in a moment. It is therefore not sufficient to acknowledge God as the original giver and then attribute to

autonomous human possession the continuation of the gift. What He grants must also be sustained. The

petition covers both acquisition and preservation, the obtaining of needed goods and their continuance

toward their proper use. It rebukes the poor man's temptation to despair and the rich man's temptation to

self-sufficiency with the same truth: all finite provision remains contingent, and all creaturely powers

depend upon the sustaining providence of God.

     [29] Bread also signifies the sacrament of the altar, the flesh and blood of our Saviour Jesus Christ,

whom the Gospel of John proclaims the bread of life descended from heaven. The original exposition

therefore asks not merely that bodily hunger be satisfied, but that this holy sacrament be purely ministered

and distributed for the benefit and consolation of God's children, and that believers receive it with right

faith and charity. It would be an act of editorial dishonesty to erase this sacramental claim in order to

make the passage conform more conveniently to some later doctrinal vocabulary. The petition's own

argument treats the sacramental provision as real nourishment ordered toward salvation. Its reception is

not a detached ceremony standing beside the believer's life, but belongs to the communion by which

Christ nourishes His people toward everlasting life.

     [30] A third sense of bread is the word of God, by which the soul is instructed, sustained, corrected,

and comforted. As the body fails without repeated sustenance, so the soul grows feeble when it ceases to

receive the truth by which its judgment and affections are ordered. Christ declares that man lives not by

bread alone but by every word proceeding from God's mouth. Where shall the sick and imprisoned seek

lasting consolation, where shall the self-righteous see the true measure of their offenses, and where shall

the despairing discover the mercy which their sins have obscured, if not in that word? It provides a mirror

for the guilty, medicine for the wounded, and armour against the world, the flesh, and the Devil. When

Christ resisted temptation He answered with Scripture; so must His disciples learn to resist through the

same truth.

     [31] The three senses of bread are not three disconnected ornaments gathered beneath an ambiguous

noun. Bodily provision sustains man's earthly operation; the sacrament nourishes his communion with

Christ; the word supplies instruction, correction, and comfort to the soul. These operations are distinct,

and one must not pretend that eating ordinary food is identical with receiving sacramental nourishment or

hearing the Gospel. Yet the whole creature is addressed through them, for human life is neither an

isolated body nor an abstract intellect nor an autonomous religious sentiment. The petition orders all

necessary sustenance beneath the Father whose gifts enable creaturely life and direct it toward its end.

Give us this day is thus simultaneously a confession of dependence, a summons to faithful work, and a

prayer for the preservation of body and soul.

THE FIFTH PETITION — FORGIVE US OUR TRESPASSES, AS WE FORGIVE
THEM THAT TRESPASS AGAINST US
     [32] The fifth petition strips man of the illusion of innocence. No Christian should glory as though

he had passed through mortal life without offense. Saint John declares that those who say they have no sin

deceive themselves and do not possess the truth. The command to ask forgiveness daily is itself a witness

against the proud supposition that the need for mercy has been left behind. Sin is not merely an

unfavorable social judgment attached to otherwise self-sufficient acts. It is a real disorder of the creature's

relation to the Divine command, and therefore the sinner cannot pronounce himself reconciled by

unilateral decree. He must ask remission from the God against whom he has offended. Confession is a

truthful act because it acknowledges a condition that self-flattery would conceal.

     [32a] Clement of Rome, in the seventh chapter of his First Epistle to the Corinthians, directs

the penitent to the blood of Christ, which, he says, “has set the grace of repentance before the

whole world.” There is more theology contained in that brief expression than in a great many

elaborate attempts to make man the author of his own reconciliation. Repentance is set before the

sinner by a grace whose ground is the blood of Christ, not by a power which the sinner discovers

in the secluded depths of his supposedly self-sufficient nature. The command to return is real, the

obligation to confess is real, and the sin to be renounced is real; nevertheless, the mercy to which

the creature returns was not brought into existence by his return. To suppose otherwise is to

mistake the response for its source. The petitioner therefore asks to be forgiven as one who

acknowledges both the guilt of his offense and the priority of that Divine beneficence through

which forgiveness is offered. (Clement of Rome, 1 Clement 7.4–8.1; Roberts–Donaldson

translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers, vol. 1.)

     [33] Yet the petition joins forgiveness received to forgiveness given. Christ does not permit a man to

beg mercy from the Father while declaring that the injuries committed by his brother shall be preserved

forever in the private treasury of resentment. He expressly teaches that if men forgive the trespasses of

others, their heavenly Father forgives them; if they refuse forgiveness, they must not presume upon

pardon while remaining fixed in hatred. When Peter asks whether seven acts of forgiveness are sufficient,

Christ answers seventy times seven. The number is not a tariff after which vengeance becomes righteous.

It destroys the attempt to transform mercy into a limited account whose balance can be exhausted by

repeated injury. The forgiving heart must remain disposed toward the brother's good even when the

offense has been grievous or recurrent.

     [33a] Polycarp of Smyrna repeats this very obligation in his instruction to the Philippians,

recalling the command of Christ: “forgive, and it shall be forgiven unto you; be merciful, that

you may obtain mercy.” The proposition is not an incidental exhortation to agreeable conduct. It

concerns the integrity of the Christian's participation in the mercy which he confesses. The man

who asks that his own offenses be remitted while preserving malice against his brother divides

his confession from his operation, invoking the goodness of God while refusing to communicate

that goodness according to his own finite capacity. Divine pardon and human forgiveness are

neither equal acts nor interchangeable powers; nevertheless, the obligation of the latter proceeds

from the reception of the former. The command therefore exposes the contradiction of a creature

who desires mercy as a private possession while denying its proper expression toward his

neighbour. (Polycarp of Smyrna, Epistle to the Philippians 2.3; Roberts–Donaldson translation,

Ante-Nicene Fathers, vol. 1.)

     [34] Consider Christ's parable of the king and the debtors. A servant owes ten thousand talents and

cannot pay; the king commands the sale of his household and property, but upon the servant's petition of

mercy releases him from the debt. That same servant then finds a fellow servant who owes a hundred

pence, seizes him violently, demands immediate payment, and consigns him to prison despite his plea for

patience. The other servants report the contradiction, and the king calls the unforgiving debtor to account.

The debt once remitted becomes the measure of his wickedness, because he had received compassion

while refusing a comparatively small compassion to another. He is handed over to tormentors. The

parable is not principally an economic lesson about efficient debt collection. It discloses the moral

monstrosity of receiving mercy as a private entitlement while refusing to let mercy govern one's treatment

of a neighbour.

     [35] The relation between mercy received and mercy rendered is asymmetric, yet inseparable. Man's

forgiveness does not equal the Divine forgiveness in magnitude, authority, or origin. The pardon of the

Father is the prior good to which the creature is called to answer. Nevertheless, refusal to forgive is not a

harmless omission external to the petition; it repudiates the very character of the mercy invoked. Let M

denote mercy received and R the disposition to render mercy to another. The moral contradiction is

displayed when a petitioner demands M while deliberately asserting R = 0. This formalism does not turn

God's pardon into a commercial exchange, nor claim that man purchases grace by an adequate quantity of

mercy. It makes visible the incoherence of asking to participate in a good while withholding every

creaturely expression of that good from one's brother.

     [36] For this reason rancour, malicious delight in punishment, and the will to private revenge must

be cast out. Punishment for an offense against God's law or the lawful civil order belongs to the proper

administration of justice, not to a man's appetites when they have been inflamed by injury. This

distinction is indispensable. The Christian may acknowledge a genuine offense, may require that justice

be done, and may seek restoration of what has been wrongfully taken without wishing evil for its own

sake upon the offender. The law may judge a wrong which charity has already forgiven in the heart.

Mercy is not the repudiation of moral order; it is the repudiation of personal hatred as the principle of

action. A community which confused forgiveness with immunity from consequence would turn the

language of grace into the accomplice of injustice.

     [37] The measure of our obligation appears in the suffering of Christ. He endured mockery, binding,

scourging, the crown of thorns, and the shameful death of the cross for those who were sinners and His

enemies. If the Son of God so acted toward the guilty, by what principle shall a forgiven man elevate his

own grievances above the duty of charity? The objection that forgiveness is difficult proves only that

fallen human nature cannot accomplish every good through its unaided inclination. The grace which

commands such forgiveness must also be sought for its performance. One does not conquer hatred by

calling it justice, nor excuse vengeance by listing the injuries which first provoked it. Christ's example

supplies both the rebuke of self-righteousness and the pattern toward which the petitioner must be drawn.

     [38] Forgiveness positively includes prayer for the offender. We ask that God not impute the trespass

to him, that he may receive grace, and that he may arrive at the glory which we desire for ourselves.

Where an occasion arises, we must help him as a Christian brother rather than secretly wish for his ruin.

Such charity need not falsely pronounce an unrepentant man innocent. It seeks his repentance,

amendment, and salvation, and precisely for that reason it refuses to identify his person wholly with the

injury he has committed. The other remains a creature before God, capable of receiving correction and

mercy. To forgive in the heart is therefore more than to refrain temporarily from retaliation. It is to desire

a good for one whose conduct has furnished strong temptation to desire the contrary.

     [39] But a theological account confined to the duties of the injured party would be incomplete and

dangerous. Every command concerning one's brother must also disclose the obligations of the brother

who receives the benefit. Otherwise the wicked would hear the doctrine of forgiveness as permission to

continue in injury while requiring their victims to remain silent. The offender is obliged to recognize his

wrong, ask forgiveness both of God and of the person offended, sincerely purpose not to repeat the

offense, and make fitting restitution according to his means and the gravity of the injury. Repentance

which refuses amendment is not repentance; a request for pardon employed as a device for avoiding

restitution is another abuse of the neighbour. Charity must be interpreted on both sides of the relation, or

its reciprocal order becomes the tool of the one who refuses to reciprocate.

     [40] The laws of princes and the ordinary administration of justice are not suspended by the Gospel's

command to forgive. If the offender remains obstinate and refuses recognition, amendment, and

restitution, the injured party may seek lawful recompense through the tribunals and remedies established

for that purpose. Such recourse is legitimate when it is undertaken in charity, for the preservation of

justice, the correction of vice, and the possible reformation of the offender, rather than for rancour or

sinister satisfaction. The claimant must still obey the rule of Christ: do to others as one would have others

do to oneself. Civil judgment and personal forgiveness occupy different offices within the same moral

order. To abolish justice in the name of mercy would injure the innocent; to reject mercy in the name of

justice would corrupt the intention of judgment. The Christian must preserve both, not by dissolving their

distinction but by governing each according to its proper end.

THE SIXTH PETITION — AND LET US NOT BE LED INTO TEMPTATION
     [41] The sixth petition requires a distinction between two forms of temptation. God permits and

appoints trials through which His servants are proved, exercised, and strengthened. The wise man

compares the testing of the righteous to the furnace by which a vessel is tried. Abraham was tested in

various manners, and Job endured the loss of his children, his goods, and his health. Such probation is not

the Divine solicitation to evil, as though God became the author of sin. It is the providential ordering of

adversity toward a good end, under which the faithful are supplied with aid and the trial may be made

profitable to them. An outward event cannot be understood merely by naming the pain it occasions. One

must ask by whose operation it is governed, toward what end it is directed, and how the creature answers

within it.


     [42] A different temptation proceeds from the Devil and from man's own concupiscence. Saint James

teaches that a man is drawn and enticed through his own disordered desire. This inclination belongs to the

corruption of human nature after Adam's fall. The original exposition holds that baptism removes original

sin and reconciles man to God, while the contest between flesh and spirit persists until bodily death. The

remaining concupiscence assaults even the devout and contemplative; its mere intrusion does not have the

same moral status as consent to its solicitation. Grace restrains its dominion, but does not make vigilance

needless. The distinction between involuntary suggestion and willing surrender must be maintained, lest a

believer despair at every temptation or, conversely, justify the deliberate indulgence which makes

temptation fruitful in actual sin.

     [43] The Devil makes use of the creature's disordered appetite, and the appetite seeks consent. Saint

James describes the progression: desire conceives and brings forth sin, and sin matures into death. The

sequence extends beyond a single act. Repetition becomes custom; custom obscures judgment; judgment

corrupted by long practice comes to excuse the very offense it ought to condemn. At the extreme the

sinner no longer recognizes evil, or recognizes it while regarding it as unworthy of concern. He may

presume upon the mercy of God as though mercy constituted a license to remain in rebellion; or he may

postpone repentance until an imagined future hour. Both are errors of the same kind, for each treats the

possibility of repentance as a possession under the sinner's command. Sudden death discloses the vanity

of that calculation.

     [44] The progression admits a formal representation without pretending that moral action can

be reduced to a physical machine. Let s(t) denote a sinful suggestion, c(t) the consent freely yielded

to it, a(t) the consequent act, and h(t) the habituation produced by repetition. A recurrent sequence s

→ c → a → h alters the conditions under which the next suggestion is received; habit can weaken

resistance, distort judgment, and make renewed consent easier. This is a feedback relation, and its

moral importance lies precisely in the difference between a passing solicitation and an inclination

cultivated by repeated surrender. The earliest resistance therefore has extraordinary significance. If

temptation is opposed at the point of suggestion, the later operations may be denied their occasion.

The model explains the gravity of habit; it does not excuse the agent as though a feedback process

abolished responsibility.


                            sₜ → cₜ → aₜ → hₜ,              cₜ = 0 ⇒ ¬(sₜ → aₜ).        (6)


      Terms. s denotes a sinful suggestion, c the consent yielded to it, a the resulting sinful act, and

      Consequence. Suggestion is not identical with consent, and temptation need not mature into

a deed. Resistance at the point of consent interrupts the progression described by Saint James;

persistent surrender, by contrast, strengthens the habitual disorder.

     [45] The wisest resistance is to reject the first invitation to evil rather than entertain it as an object of

prolonged inward conversation. A temptation given lodging in the imagination may gather force until

consent and action follow. The believer must therefore exercise discernment, turn the mind toward what

is good, and call upon Divine assistance before the appetite has established its own momentum. Neither

seclusion nor the appearance of exceptional devotion guarantees immunity from this struggle. The flesh

may assault the most disciplined man, and the Devil continues to seek an occasion of destruction. The

petition presupposes conflict as a reality of earthly life. It asks that the Christian be strengthened to fight

rather than lulled into the fantasy that there is no longer an adversary.

     [46] Christ does not teach us to pray for a world entirely without trial. He teaches us to ask that we

not be led into temptation in the sense of being abandoned to its dominion. Saint Paul declares that God is

faithful and will not permit His people to be tempted beyond what they may bear, but will provide the

way to endure. Saint James bids Christians count their varied trials occasions for joy because tested faith

produces patience and mature work. This is no celebration of wickedness, but the recognition that

resistance can bring the received powers of the creature into stronger conformity with their end. One must

distinguish the trial through which virtue is exercised from the evil toward which temptation solicits

consent. To confuse these is to confuse the furnace with the corruption it may expose.

     [46a] Polycarp's seventh chapter is more directly pertinent still, for he actually repeats the

petition under consideration. He instructs Christians to persevere in prayer and fasting,

“beseeching in our supplications the all-seeing God not to lead us into temptation,” and

immediately recalls the Lord's warning that the spirit is willing while the flesh is weak. Here the

language of the petition is neither remote allusion nor an inference imposed upon the ancient

writer by a later controversy. It is his own instruction to the faithful. The man who prays

acknowledges the insufficiency of his unassisted powers; the man who watches acknowledges

the continuing reality of danger; and the man who perseveres exercises the powers he has

received rather than pretending that grace has made their exercise unnecessary. Divine aid and

creaturely resistance are not two equal sources competing for possession of the same act. The

one sustains and orders; the other responds and operates. Thus the prayer does not ask to be

rendered incapable of trial, but to be kept from the dominion of that temptation which would

otherwise seize upon the weakness of the flesh. (Polycarp of Smyrna, Epistle to the Philippians

7.2; Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers, vol. 1.)

     [47] The promises made to those who overcome set the conflict within its final horizon: the tree of

life, freedom from the second death, and the crown appointed to those who contend faithfully. Saint Paul

declares that no man is crowned unless he strives lawfully. Christ Himself announces that He has

overcome the world. The Christian therefore does not battle as though the enemy possessed ultimate

sovereignty or as though victory depended upon the invention of a power independent of Christ. He fights

under the Lord who has already conquered, employing the grace supplied to him and refusing to surrender

the real duty of resistance. The sixth petition is neither a plea for idleness nor an admission that the

powers of darkness are equal to God. It is the prayer of a dependent combatant for the preservation

without which his resistance would fail.


THE SEVENTH PETITION — BUT DELIVER US FROM EVIL
     [48] The seventh petition completes the movement begun in the sixth. There we prayed that

temptation should not carry us into consent and sin; here we pray that if through frailty we have fallen,

God will not leave us in captivity. Deliverance must not be reduced to a momentary feeling of relief while

the offense remains enthroned within the will. We ask that sin be uprooted before it establishes a settled

dominion, that the captive be freed, and that the creature be returned to the order which his transgression

has violated. To be delivered is to have the reign of evil broken, not merely its consequences made

temporarily less painful. The prayer thus reaches beyond rescue from a particular danger to the more

fundamental liberation of the person from the disorder that would destroy him.

     [49] Of all the evils from which man seeks release, sin has the principal place. Disease, poverty,

famine, punishment, and the other afflictions of mortal existence are grievous, and the Lord's Prayer does

not forbid the suffering creature to seek lawful relief from them. Yet they are not evil in precisely the

same sense as sin. Bodily adversity may occur without guilt and may be governed by Divine providence

toward an end beneficial to the sufferer; sin is a perversion of the will and is never to be chosen as an

instrument of obedience. The distinction is therefore qualitative rather than merely quantitative. A man

may be deprived of wealth and remain faithful; he may possess every outward comfort and remain

enslaved to wickedness. The principal evil is not whatever most diminishes present pleasure, but what

most directly contradicts the good for which the creature was made.

     [50] We must therefore ask concerning worldly affliction under the condition of God's pleasure. The

petitioner may desire relief from disease, poverty, contempt, imprisonment, or sorrow, yet he must not

pronounce every trial contrary to Divine goodness. Scripture teaches that the Lord chastens those whom

He loves and disciplines those whom He receives. The present life is a season of trial and correction; the

rest of perfected glory lies beyond it. As gold is proved in fire, so the believer may be purified through

adversity. Conversely, a wicked man may prosper for a season while his uncorrected habits grow more

destructive. Prosperity is therefore no infallible certificate of Divine approval, nor suffering an infallible

proof of rejection. Those who measure God's favour exclusively by comfort have submitted providence to

the impoverished arithmetic of their own appetites.

     [51] Such reasoning must not become indifference to the neighbour's sufferings. Because adversity

can be employed by providence, it does not follow that men are permitted to impose injury, neglect the

sick, despise the poor, or call cruelty an instrument of grace. The creature remains answerable for his own

action. God's capacity to order an evil deed toward a good consequence cannot convert the human agent's

wicked intention into righteousness. The distinction between Divine government and creaturely

responsibility, already established in the previous petitions, must govern this one also. We may pray to

endure what God permits and still act in charity to relieve what the neighbour suffers. The proper

response to another's affliction is not to declare that providence has relieved us of obligation, but to

perform faithfully the good placed within our power.

     [52] Finally we ask deliverance from the Devil himself, that ancient enemy whom Scripture calls a

liar and a murderer from the beginning, the continual instigator of sin and adversary of virtue. His

artifices seek to draw the creature from suggestion into consent, from consent into habit, and from habit

into final ruin. Yet even here the petition is addressed not to the adversary but to the Father, because the

power of deliverance belongs to God. We ask to be protected from deception, strengthened against

malice, restored when fallen, and preserved from everlasting damnation. The final word of the Lord's

Prayer is therefore not the triumph of evil but its defeat beneath Divine grace. The seven petitions form

one continuous movement: the Father is acknowledged, His name honoured, His kingdom sought, His

will obeyed, His gifts received, His mercy reciprocated, His aid requested in trial, and His deliverance

sought against the evil which would dissolve that communion. The prayer returns the creature, in every

condition of life, to the God from whom its life was first received.
