1 Glass envelope and atmospheric loading

Idea
We shall begin with the glass body, since the electrons will require somewhere to go, and the atmosphere,
which has hitherto occupied that space without objection, must now, like a poor house guest, be persuaded
to leave. Give the vessel a curved viewing face and a narrow neck through which we can install the
electrode assembly; the reasons for that curvature will become apparent shortly, when we elect to consider
how much air weighs when there is no longer an equal quantity of it pushing from the other side.
Give the face the curvature and wall specified below, then attend to the narrowing neck and the cooling of
the whole, since the form which our hand gives the hot glass must endure through the subsequent
operations of plastic nature, including those less agreeable consequences of uneven cooling which a

transparent surface may conceal. We shall therefore anneal the body gradually and examine its thickness
before asking it to bear the atmosphere; for nature’s own art will have its effect in the glass whether our
workmanship has made that effect convenient or otherwise. (Cudworth, True Intellectual System, I.iii, pp.
147–150.)
Here that great ladder of procession and return, which the blessed patriarch Jacob bore witness to,
descends to the bench itself: God communicates being and efficacy according to the several conditions of
His creatures, those above and on and below the earth, such as the myriad plants and metals hidden inside
Gaia’s breast; the glass bears, the heated surface emits, the field directs, and the workman understands and
orders their relation, each exercising its proper power within the uncreated activity by which it is
sustained.

Claims
The Romans already possessed the craft upon which this part depends. They blew glass, formed vessels,
and produced transparent examples of sufficient refinement that we need not imagine every Roman
drinking from something resembling the bottom of a pond. A surviving first century mould blown cup
supplies a direct example of the work, and our concern here is to apply that craft to the dimensions and
shape required by the television. [H1]
Now, the glassworker has a considerable advantage over the unfortunate fellow whom we might
otherwise have sent into the woods with instructions to invent transparency. He knows how to work glass
while hot, and how to produce a vessel whose contents remain agreeably on the inside, which is a
substantial accomplishment however little attention we ordinarily give it when drinking from a bottle. We
shall ask him to retain that familiar arrangement while dispensing with the contents, and it is this
apparently modest request which introduces the difficulty. A bottle full of air has pressure on both sides of
its wall; the two parties largely settle their disagreement without requiring the glass to intervene. Once we
remove the air within, the atmosphere outside continues pressing with precisely its former enthusiasm,
and the glass must undertake the work of the party we have dismissed. The better our vacuum, therefore,
the more we ask of our vessel, which is a mildly irritating reward for having done the preceding job
properly.

Proof and worked calculation
Now, a viewing face only fifty millimetres across may seem too small to attract much trouble, but the
atmosphere presses upon it all the same, and once we remove the air within, the glass must carry the
difference. We therefore multiply the pressure difference by the projected area of the face:
  A = πD²/4 = π(0.050)²/4 = 0.001963 m²
  F = Δp A = 101325 × 0.001963 = 199 N ≈ 44.7 lbf
We have, then, nearly forty five pounds of force pressing upon a face approximately two inches across,
which is rather more enthusiasm than one might have expected from emptying a bottle. Taking a radius of
curvature of thirty millimetres and a wall thickness of three millimetres, the spherical shell calculation
gives:
  σ = Δp r/(2t) = 101325 × 0.030/(2 × 0.003) = 0.507 MPa
The curved wall carries this load in compression. Its thickness, the transition into the neck, surface
condition and quality of annealing must therefore be considered together, since a well formed face gains

little from being attached to a neck full of residual stress. External pressure buckling also enters the
choice of the finished envelope dimensions.
Here the curvature promised earlier finally earns its place at the proverbial table. We have spent some
time discussing a craft ordinarily employed to keep wine from escaping, only to discover that our present
concern is keeping the world from entering, and that reversal explains the shape we gave the vessel at the
beginning. The rounded wall carries the pressure through compression, with the neck and the cooled glass
participating in the same structure; an elegant face gains little from a neck already quarrelling with itself.
Thus the annealing, thickness and curvature return together to the glassworker's bench, where they
belong, and our instruction to make a rounded vessel now has a reason beyond its resemblance to an
elderly television.

Fabrication and acceptance
Make and anneal the trial vessels first, examining the thickness and rejecting inclusions, and evacuate
them behind an implosion enclosure. Establish the vessel and its lead seals before installing the cathode,
recording the dimensions, the pressure held and the time for which it holds, together with any crack or
distortion. This leaves us a repeatable glass body upon which to build the rest.


2 Vacuum pump and gas removal

Idea
To remove the air, we shall employ falling mercury drops which carry small pockets of gas down to the
outlet, placing a cooled trap between the pump and the tube so that the mercury remains on its own side of
the arrangement.
Meter the mercury into separated drops and give their descent a narrow passage, then ask how quickly the
gas leaves and what the warm liquid contributes in its place; for plastic nature will maintain its orderly
work on either side of our intentions, and an excellent pump may furnish its own vapour with the same
indifference with which it removes the air. The trap must consequently receive a place in the arrangement
before we congratulate ourselves upon the empty bulb.

Claims
The useful action occurs between the drops: each separates a pocket of gas and carries it downward,
allowing successive pockets to leave the vessel. Mercury supplies the liquid column, while the narrow
passage and separated drops supply the pumping action. Sprengel describes the historical development of
this mechanism in his 1865 paper. [V1]
There is something agreeable about employing a heavy liquid to remove something which appears to
weigh nothing, although the preceding calculation has already deprived the air of any right to plead
insignificance. The mercury falls, and the separated gas pockets travel with it, so the practical question
becomes how to arrange the passage such that the liquid does useful work on its journey. Pouring mercury
into a broad vessel would give us a broad vessel containing mercury, an accomplishment for which the
television would have little gratitude. The narrow fall tube and the succession of drops are what make the
descent into a pump. We therefore return to the glassworker with another request, this time for the
passages through which that succession can be maintained.

Proof and worked calculation

The first dimension is the height of mercury needed to balance atmospheric pressure. Taking its density as
13500 kg/m³, we obtain the height directly from hydrostatics:
  h = p/(ρg) = 101325/(13500 × 9.81) = 0.765 m = 2.51 ft
We may then consider how quickly the remaining gas leaves. For a vessel of volume V and effective
pumping speed S, with leakage and gas release temporarily set aside, pressure falls exponentially:
  V dp/dt = −Sp; p(t) = p₀ exp(−St/V)
  t = (V/S) ln(p₀/p₁)
For our half litre vessel, an effective pumping speed of 0.01 L/s takes the pressure from 100 Pa after
preliminary evacuation to 0.001 Pa in 576 seconds, or 9.6 minutes. Those are the values used in the
calculation above.
Leakage and gas released from the surfaces introduce a continuing supply, which we denote by Q in
Pa·L/s. The pressure then approaches Q/S, so at the same pumping speed our 0.001 Pa operating value
requires:
  Q ≤ S p = 0.01 × 0.001 = 1 × 10⁻⁵ Pa·L/s
Mercury has one further habit to accommodate: at 25 °C, the NIST vapour equation gives approximately
0.264 Pa, well above our pressure for the remaining air. We therefore place a vapour control arrangement
between the warm reservoir and the tube, removing the air while keeping mercury vapour from taking its
place. [V2]
We have, in other words, recruited a liquid to evict the air and must now prevent our assistant from
moving into the vacated premises. This is why the trap belongs to the pumping arrangement from the
beginning. A vessel may have lost almost all its original air and still contain vapour supplied by the very
apparatus which emptied it, so the pressure calculation brings us back to a particular piece of glass
between pump and bulb, cooled and arranged to keep that exchange from spoiling the work.

Fabrication and acceptance
Measure pumping speed against a known volume, then isolate the pump and record the pressure rise. Heat
the vessel within its tested envelope limit to release adsorbed gas, and check the cooled vapour trap as
part of the same assembly. Keep the mercury and recovered material in closed containment throughout.


3 Electron transport in the residual gas

Idea
Having removed most of the air, we must decide how little remains, for the beam needs a reasonably clear
passage from cathode to screen. We shall choose that pressure by calculating how often an electron
encounters a gas molecule along the way.
We shall choose the pressure by the length of the electron’s journey and the molecules remaining in its
path, allowing nature’s regular operation its actual measure in collisions; for a bottle may appear
admirably empty while retaining sufficient company to spoil our beam, and the eye’s approval is no
substitute for counting how often that company shall be met. Set the pumping requirement from the
survival fraction below, then return to leakage and the condition of the surfaces.

Claims

“Sufficient vacuum” becomes a useful instruction when we give it a number. Gas composition, electron
energy and collision cross section determine that number, and the scattering model below relates them to
the length of our tube.

Proof and worked calculation
For a uniform dilute gas, the number of molecules per unit volume is n = p/(kT). If σc is the effective
collision cross section and L the flight length, the fraction crossing without collision is exp(−nσcL). We
shall use σc = 10⁻¹⁹ m² for this illustrative calculation, with T = 300 K and L = 0.30 m:
    Psurvive = exp[−p σc L/(kT)]

The importance of this little journey is that the word empty has ceased to be a matter of appearances. The
vessel looked empty when it contained ordinary air, and will look much the same after we have expended
considerable labour removing it; the electrons, obliged to cross the space rather than admire it, will
encounter a rather different establishment. Their path supplies our measure. Having begun with a bottle
and a pump, we can now return to those parts with a pressure chosen for the length of the journey we
intend the beam to make.

Fabrication and acceptance
Compare beam current and spot stability as pressure changes, recording the residual gas composition
where possible. A glow discharge, wandering focus or rapid cathode deterioration calls for further
pumping or cleaning; the useful result is a steady beam which remains under our control.


4 Cathode heater and electron supply

Idea
The cathode supplies the electrons, and we shall heat its emissive surface until enough of them leave to
form the beam, drawing that current into the gun by the applied electric field.
Prepare the calcium coating upon the iron and warm it under evacuation, observing how the current
answers the heater and the collecting potential; for plastic nature must perform its work upon the surface
actually provided, and neither the name of the ingredient nor our confidence in having acquired it will
settle its condition. The emission curve shall therefore govern the heater allowance, bringing the coating’s
preparation back into the power which our feet must furnish.

Claims

Temperature and effective work function govern this emission, the latter entering exponentially, which
makes the condition of the emitting surface particularly consequential. Our cathode uses iron coated with
calcium oxide; the emission equations tell us what performance the assembly must supply. [E1]
The lime is a familiar material in a thoroughly unfamiliar occupation. A Roman builder may have used it
in work intended to keep a wall standing, while we ask a coating containing it to help furnish a stream of
electrons, which is precisely the sort of change in employment that makes this undertaking entertaining.
The material's familiarity settles where we may obtain it; the condition of its surface and the temperature
at which we operate settle what it will do after we obtain it. We must therefore follow the argument from
the quarry and furnace to the heated surface, since a catalogue of ingredients, however reassuring, has
never yet persuaded a cathode to emit on command.

Proof and worked calculation
We begin with the Richardson Dushman expression, which relates current density to temperature and
work function:
  J = Aeff T² exp[−φ/(kₑT)]
Here J is expressed in A/cm² when Aeff is in A/(cm²·K²), with φ in eV and kₑ = 8.61733 × 10⁻⁵ eV/K. To
examine the sensitivity, take T = 1100 K and the ideal metallic reference Aeff = 120. [E1]

A difference of half an electron volt is easy enough to pass over when reading, especially after the eye has
become tired of small numbers with unfamiliar units. The exponential has no such fatigue. It applies the
change in full, and leaves us with a current nearly two hundred times smaller, which explains why heating
any convenient piece of iron and calling it a cathode would be an unnecessarily expensive way of learning
about surfaces. We return, then, to the coating and its treatment, because those workshop details govern
the quantity which the equation makes so unforgiving.
There is also the space charge between the electrodes to consider. For a flat vacuum diode with zero initial
electron velocity, the Child Langmuir limit is:
  JCL = (4/9) ε₀ √(2e/m) V³ᐟ²/d²
At 100 V with 2 mm spacing, the planar result is 583 A/m², or 583 µA from each square millimetre. That
exceeds our beam current for a 1 mm² emitting area, though the grid and apertures reduce the current
which finally passes into the gun. [E1]
The heater must replace both radiated and conducted heat. Radiation alone is εσS(T⁴ − T₀⁴); using
emissivity 0.5, a hot area of 20 mm², T = 1100 K and T₀ = 300 K gives about 0.83 W. Add the heat
conducted through the supports and leads, and allocate 2 W to this small CRT heater assembly.

Fabrication and acceptance
Begin with a separate diode and record emission against heater power and anode voltage, noting
activation, coating adhesion, insulation leakage and operating lifetime. Carry that curve into the power
budget. If the iron heater assembly fails, make the carbon heater or alternative emissive coating as a
separate specimen, so the cause of a change remains apparent.


5 Electron gun acceleration focus and intensity control

Idea
We now give the electrons their speed, accelerating them through 1000 V, and bring them to a small spot
upon the screen. A control grid near the cathode regulates how many pass, allowing the picture signal to
govern brightness.
Keep the cathode, aperture and focusing cylinder upon one axis, and leave their positions adjustable until
the beam has found its proper spot; for the field will direct the electrons according to the arrangement
before it, carrying nature’s mechanical skill into a result which may differ considerably from the little rifle
we thought we had assembled. We shall settle acceleration first, then focus and grid control, so each
adjustment has a definite effect to disclose. (Conway, Principles, IX §2.)

Claims
Acceleration determines electron speed and beam power, while the spacing, apertures and potentials of
the electrodes determine the focus. The focusing cylinder is therefore part of an electrostatic lens whose
several dimensions we adjust together.

Proof and worked calculation
   eVa = mv²/2; v = √(2eVa/m)
   Va = 1000 V → v = 1.876 × 10⁷ m/s
   tflight = L/v = 0.30/(1.876 × 10⁷) = 16.0 ns
   Pbeam = Va Ib = 1000 × 100 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.100 W
For a 40 × 30 mm picture divided into 32 × 32 elements, the nominal pitches are 1.25 mm horizontally
and 0.938 mm vertically. We shall aim first for a spot about 0.7 mm across or smaller, checking it at the
centre and corners, since scanning alters focus and astigmatism as the beam moves.
The aperture and grid near the cathode give us the brightness control. Obtain its working voltage range
from the gun transfer curve Ib(Vg), marking the point of cut off and the maximum useful current; the
accelerating voltage determines the electron energy, while this curve tells us how the grid admits or
withholds the beam.
Consider how little of the eventual picture exists at this point. We have a source, a field which accelerates
its electrons, and an aperture which admits some portion of them; there is no small image travelling intact
down the neck, waiting to strike the glass. The picture will arise from where the beam goes and how
strongly it arrives at each moment. That observation follows us through the rest of the instrument: focus
makes the mark small, deflection places it, and the grid changes its brightness. We have thus returned
from the rather grand business of making an electron gun to the practical reason for giving this particular
electrode a signal connection.

Fabrication and acceptance
Establish a steady stationary spot before asking it to draw anything. Measure its size, current and grid cut
off, and record the aperture diameters and electrode positions in inches and millimetres. Keep the gun
adjustable while bringing it together with the phosphor and scanning arrangement.


6 Phosphor preparation screen loading and light output

Idea
For the screen, we shall use manganese activated zinc silicate, which turns the arriving electron energy
into visible green light. The glass gives the beam a destination; this coating gives us something to see
when it arrives.
We shall roast the zinc ore and prepare our silica, then weigh the two for the host compound which the
screen requires, allowing approximately 7.30 grams of zinc oxide and 2.70 grams of silica for a nominal
ten-gram batch; and, having mixed them finely, we shall account for the manganese in the activated
composition and attend to the firing atmosphere, since plastic nature will carry the work forward
according to the conditions actually present in the furnace, with little concern for the excellent picture we
have already imagined. Nature’s own art precedes ours here, and our judgment must discover how to
employ it, which is why Lady Conway’s acknowledgment of nature’s mechanical skill returns us quite
comfortably to the balance. (Conway, Principles, IX §2; Cudworth, True Intellectual System, I.iii, pp.
147–150.)

Claims
Manganese activated zinc silicate is a documented green cathodoluminescent material, prepared by
controlled chemistry and high temperature firing. We shall use those material accounts to guide the
preparation of our coating. [M1–M2]

Proof and worked calculation
The proportion follows from the compound we intend to form:
   2 ZnO + SiO₂ → Zn₂SiO₄
Now, the presence of the proper ingredients does not relieve us of preparing their meeting, any more than
placing a smith, an anvil and a lump of iron in the same room obliges a horseshoe to appear; we must
therefore attend to the fineness of the mixture, the temperature and duration of firing, and the conditions
under which the manganese enters the resulting material, allowing nature’s formative operation the
particular means through which our intended phosphor can become actual. Fire the selected preparation
under its specified conditions, then grind and screen the product, retaining the fine powder for comparison
beneath the beam; for the work which began among the metals hidden within Gaia’s breast must now
answer upon the glass, where its brightness and persistence will tell us how well we have prepared it for
its new occupation. The host calculation uses molar masses of 81.38 g/mol for ZnO and 60.08 g/mol for
SiO₂; manganese replaces a small portion of the zinc sites, with concentration, oxidation state and firing
atmosphere controlled during preparation.
One published preparation uses 1000–1300 °C for 1–5 hours with its specified precursor chemistry. [M2]
Our screen retains the resulting green emission, which is quite sufficient for the moving monochrome
picture intended here.

Green will serve us quite adequately. Once one has undertaken to construct a television in an ancient
workshop, a complaint that its picture resembles the view through a bottle seems somewhat ungrateful,
particularly when the alternative at this stage is a clear view of nothing happening. The colour follows
from the emitting material, while the usefulness of the picture depends upon brightness, persistence and
the size of the spot. These considerations bring us back to the powder on the glass: its preparation must
give the beam something which lights promptly enough to follow the moving image and brightly enough
for us to see it.
To calculate light output, let us take 1 percent conversion of beam energy into visible light, optical
luminous efficacy of 500 lm/W and a front escape fraction of 0.5. With those screen properties, the 0.1 W
beam gives:
  Φfront = Pbeam η K f = 0.1 × 0.01 × 500 × 0.5 = 0.25 lm
  Lscreen = Φfront/(πA) = 0.25/(π × 0.040 × 0.030) = 66 cd/m²
The brightness equation treats the screen as approximately Lambertian and uniformly excited across the
full field. Conversion efficiency, charging, electron collection, coating adhesion and penetration all enter
the finished screen performance; reducing conversion efficiency to 0.1 percent reduces the calculated
brightness to 6.6 cd/m².

Fabrication and acceptance
Fire and screen small batches, comparing brightness and decay under the beam before sealing the
complete tube. Provide a collection path for arriving electrons so the surface does not charge
uncontrollably, and keep the coating method within our mineral and glass construction, without
introducing aluminium vacuum deposition or modern binders.


7 Magnetic deflection coils

Idea
A stationary dot, however agreeable it may appear after all this work, will make for rather limited
entertainment. We shall move it with two magnetic fields at right angles, directing the beam horizontally
and vertically across the face.
Wind the two axes at right angles and establish the displacement produced by each before combining their
sweeps, remembering More’s attention to the reach of incorporeal agency when we follow an unseen
operation into a visible displacement; the particular coil must nevertheless answer in turns, gap and
current, and its fringe field will enter the beam’s course whether our first drawing found room to represent
it or not. We shall map that course before selecting the driver. (More, Divine Dialogues, Dialogue I.)
We shall have earned a certain affection for that dot by the time it appears, since it represents the
combined work of the glassmaker, the pump, the heater and the gun, but affection has its limits, and an
evening spent watching it remain in the middle of the screen would test them. The beam must move while
the brightness changes, placing successive parts of the picture where they belong. A magnetic field gives
us the means of changing its direction without inserting a little mechanical hand inside the vacuum, which
would introduce difficulties of its own. The coils therefore take up the work where the gun leaves it,
turning a successful stationary experiment into the beginning of a display.

Claims

The Lorentz force gives the field required to bend the beam. Begin with a uniform field calculation, then
map the fringe fields and the effect of the actual neck geometry when winding and adjusting the coils.

Proof and worked calculation
Take an effective magnetic length of 40 mm, a distance of 150 mm from its centre to the screen, and a
horizontal edge displacement of 20 mm. At small angles, the deflection follows:
   θ ≈ eBl/(mv); y ≈ Dθ; B ≈ ymv/(elD)
   Bedge = 0.355 mT at Va = 1000 V
For an ideal magnetic circuit dominated by a 60 mm air gap, NI ≈ Bg/μ₀ gives 17.0 ampere turns. One
hundred turns therefore require 0.170 A at the edge. Leakage and fringe fields increase the required
current; a fourfold field penalty raises it to 0.680 A with that same winding.
With an effective gap area of 4 cm², we obtain the gap dominated inductance:
   L ≈ μ₀N²Ag/g = 83.8 µH
At the initial 400 Hz line rate, the active 2.125 ms sweep changes current from −0.170 to +0.170 A,
requiring approximately 0.0134 V inductively. Returning in 0.375 ms requires about 0.0760 V before
resistance and losses. For a 2 Ω winding, the edge resistive drop is another 0.340 V. We therefore supply
V = L di/dt + Ri, with core loss and stray inductance also included in the driver.

Fabrication and acceptance
Wind one axis first and map displacement against current, measuring its resistance and inductance before
choosing the output stage. The coil calls for current at low impedance, so use an output arrangement with
that capacity or an impedance matching transformer.


8 Electrostatic deflection comparison

Idea
We may also compare a pair of metal deflection plates with the magnetic coils, retaining the same cathode
ray display while changing the means by which the beam is turned.
Set temporary plates beside the beam and compare their required swing with the current demanded by the
coils, keeping the picture dimensions unchanged; for our choice of artifice must follow the powers
through which nature actually works, and a convenience purchased in copper may return as an
inconvenience in plate voltage. The calculation shall tell us what each arrangement asks before either
acquires a permanent place in the neck.

Claims
The plates trade coil current for voltage swing. That trade can suit a valve voltage amplifier, so it is useful
to work through the dimensions before choosing which arrangement is easier for the workshop to make.
This is one of those occasions upon which a choice that looks like a minor alteration in the drawing may
reorganize a considerable portion of the workshop's labour. A coil asks us to wind wire and supply
changing current; plates ask us to shape electrodes and supply changing voltage. Both ultimately move
the same beam across the same small face, so we have a common result against which to compare them.
Following each demand through its amplifier and supply gives us a more useful answer than deciding that

one looks simpler upon the page, which is an excellent place for machinery to conceal the work it will
later require.

Proof and worked calculation
For plates of length l and gap d, with drift distance D measured from their centre, accelerating voltage Va
and differential plate voltage Vd, the small angle displacement becomes:
   y = Vd lD/(2dVa)
Take l = 40 mm, d = 10 mm, D = 150 mm, Va = 1000 V and y = 20 mm. The required Vd is 66.7 V from
centre to edge, or about 133 V peak to peak, which fits a voltage amplifier more naturally than the coil
current does. Check plate interception, angular limits and distortion while setting the assembly.
If the electrodes and wiring contribute 20 pF, changing 133 V over the initial 0.375 ms return draws about
7.1 µA capacitively. The resistive amplifier loads add their own demand and may consume considerably
more.

Fabrication and acceptance
Make a temporary plate assembly and compare its spot range, distortion and driver power with the
magnetic yoke, keeping the picture dimensions and brightness the same. Choose the arrangement whose
adjusted performance best suits the construction.


9 Raster timing and video bandwidth

Idea
Before building the amplifiers and oscillators, we shall set the first raster, giving ourselves definite line
rates, frame rates and picture bandwidth upon which to work.
Begin with the slower raster and an orderly succession of bars, giving the beam its measured intervals
before asking it to follow a tape; for nature’s mechanical skill will preserve the consequences of our
timing quite impartially, including those which turn a proposed picture into an itinerant band of light.
Count the lines, divide the active interval and provide the return, then set the brightness bandwidth to the
elements which those intervals can actually carry.

Claims
The initial raster is progressive and deliberately coarse, with a compatible test source. The later VHS
sections replace this timing with the pattern recorded on the tape; for the present, we are establishing a
working display.
There is little advantage in asking a new glass vessel, a new cathode, new valves and a new scanning
circuit to explain their faults simultaneously. If the screen remains dark, each may blame the others with
perfect mechanical impartiality, and we should be left to conduct an inquiry into the whole establishment.
A slow, coarse raster gives us a visible pattern whose behaviour is easier to follow. Once the display
draws that pattern, the later change of timing has a known instrument upon which to act. The modest first
picture therefore earns its place by bringing us to the faster one with fewer unexplained variables
accompanying us.

Proof and worked calculation

  fline = Nlines fframe = 32 × 12.5 = 400 Hz
  Tline = 1/400 = 2.50 ms; Tactive = 0.85 Tline = 2.125 ms
  Tpixel = Tactive/32 = 66.4 µs
The quickest alternating pattern contains one light and one dark element in two element times, giving a
fundamental of 1/(2Tpixel) = 7.53 kHz. A 10 kHz first video passband carries that pattern with limited
edge detail.
For a single pole amplifier at 10 kHz, the approximate rise time is 0.35/B = 35 µs, roughly half an
element time. A wider passband sharpens the transitions at the cost of greater gain and stray capacitance
demands. At 12.5 frames per second, we shall also see flicker and motion judder in this first display
mode.
To keep horizontal timing error below half an element across a line, |ΔT/T| must remain below 0.5/32 =
1.56 percent. Reset the free running oscillators with line and frame synchronization each cycle, so their
drift does not accumulate across the picture.

Fabrication and acceptance
Begin with stationary bars, then a moving edge, recording line rate, frame rate, active interval, retrace
blanking and drift. When changing the raster, recalculate bandwidth and power before connecting the
source.


10 Triode amplification and synchronization circuits

Idea
We shall use gridded thermionic valves to amplify the picture signal and generate the synchronized ramps,
assigning each stage its part in controlling the beam.
Choose the bias and load from the valve’s measured curve, then keep the input and output leads short and
separated; for plastic nature will execute the unintended coupling as regularly as the intended
amplification, and an omitted capacitance is chiefly omitted from our account of the work. We shall
include the valve and its wiring in the same reckoning, following gain through the load until the next
stage receives the signal we intended to give it.

Claims
In a triode, the grid controls the current between cathode and plate, and the gain follows from
transconductance, plate resistance, bias and load. The following relations connect those properties to the
amplification we require.

Proof and worked calculation
  Av ≈ −gm (rp ∥ RL)
With gm = 1 mS, rp = 20 kΩ and RL = 20 kΩ, the estimated unloaded gain magnitude is ten. A 1 V
detector signal consequently gives roughly 10 V before loading and the available voltage swing impose
their limits.
  Bpole ≈ 1/(2π Rout Ctotal)

For Rout = 10 kΩ and effective capacitance of 100 pF, the pole is 159 kHz; at 1 nF it falls to 15.9 kHz.
Include grid to plate feedback capacitance and the Miller effect, for the wiring has its own capacitance
whether or not we have been considerate enough to draw it.
The last point deserves a moment, since a circuit diagram can encourage an unfortunate confidence that
only the components represented by respectable symbols possess electrical properties. Two nearby
conductors will store charge whether we have labelled them a capacitor or merely called them untidy
wiring. At low frequency we may get away with neglecting the distinction; as the signal changes faster,
the same overlooked capacitance takes an increasing share of the current. The diagram consequently
returns us to the physical bench, where short leads and deliberate spacing do some of the work we might
otherwise have tried to obtain by adding another valve.
A capacitor charged by approximately constant current follows dV/dt = I/C. Raising it by 100 V in 2.125
ms with C = 10 nF requires 0.471 mA. A valve switch discharges it during return, and a sync pulse sets
the reset. For magnetic scanning, the output stage converts this timing into the required current ramp
through the coil.

Fabrication and acceptance
Measure each valve transfer curve before selecting the resistors. Build the sync separator, horizontal
oscillator, frame oscillator, blanking path and output stages separately, bringing them together once each
performs its assigned function.


11 Capacitors resistors and insulation

Idea
The capacitors, resistors and insulation come next, all of them made as separate parts from conductors and
suitable insulating materials. Their appearance is simple enough; the labour becomes more apparent when
we count how many layers and turns the finished instrument requires.
Separate the leaves with clean, dry mineral insulation and count the active overlap through the whole
stack, allowing plastic nature no easier conducting path along a damp edge than the one our circuit
provides; for the distinction we have drawn between a capacitor and a short circuit requires a material
expression, and dampness is quite capable of revising it. Area gives the capacitance, while the prepared
surfaces and clearances determine whether it remains useful at its working voltage.

Claims
A small tuning capacitor and a large smoothing capacitor place quite different demands upon the
workshop. In either case, examine the glass or mineral insulation for leakage, breakdown and loss under
the conditions in which it will operate.

Proof and worked calculation
  C = ε₀ εr A/d; R = ρl/Awire
An air capacitor with 0.01 m² total overlap and 0.5 mm spacing gives 177 pF. Add gaps in parallel to
increase capacitance, counting the active overlap across all of them; the footprint of the stack tells us how
much bench space it occupies, while the summed overlap tells us its capacitance.

For a dielectric of εr = 6 and thickness 50 µm, a 20 µF capacitor requires 18.8 m² of total overlap. At 0.05
m² per gap, that is about 377 gaps, which explains why this seemingly modest component may occupy
rather more of the workman's afternoon than the tuner.
We have arrived at an excellent example of how a perfectly ordinary equation can conceal a thoroughly
unreasonable afternoon. Twenty microfarads occupies very little space in a sentence. Eighteen point eight
square metres of carefully separated conducting surface occupies considerably more space in a workshop,
particularly when somebody must prepare the layers, keep them clean and assemble them without
introducing a short circuit somewhere near the middle. The arithmetic has not made the component
mysterious; it has made its labour visible. Keep that labour in mind when we reach rectification and
filtering, since a different supply arrangement may spare the glass and leaf workers a substantial part of it.
Across a 0.5 mm air space, 1 kV gives an average field of 2 MV/m. Round the edges, increase clearances
and test the insulation with humidity, dust and surface tracking in mind, since these determine where the
actual discharge begins.

Fabrication and acceptance
Keep the parts inside the vacuum inorganic and low outgassing, using the external fibre or resin insulation
only where its own tests permit. Choose resistor materials by their resistance and temperature behaviour,
and mount them to dispose of the heat produced at the operating point.


12 Generator rectifier and supply smoothing

Idea
The motion of our feet must become heater power and electrode voltage. We shall obtain those supplies
from the generator, then rectify and smooth the branches which require a steady polarity.
Wind trial coils before committing the full supply to copper, measuring the field and loaded voltage of the
assembled generator; for nature’s orderly agency will answer the actual flux and speed, leaving the
impressive number of turns in our notebook to explain only what we expected. Then rectify and smooth
the required branches, carrying their losses into the load at the treadles and their ripple into the conditions
under which the cathodes must remain hot.

Claims
The generator develops voltage as magnetic flux changes through its windings. Its iron circuit, insulation
and losses belong in the calculation with the turns and speed, as do those of any transformer used in the
supply. [P1–P2]

Proof and worked calculation
   E = −N dΦ/dt; Esine,rms ≈ 4.44 fN Bmax Acore
At 50 Hz, Bmax = 0.8 T and core area 2 cm², a 100 V winding requires about 2815 turns; a 1 kV winding
under the same conditions requires about 28150. Use the measured field of the assembled magnetic circuit
when setting the winding, and laminate the core or adopt another low loss geometry. The 0.8 T value
belongs to this calculation of the core flux.
Copper has now become a question of patience. The voltage rises with the number of turns, but every
additional turn must exist outside the equation as an actual length of wire, placed beside other lengths
from which it must remain insulated. A large number is consequently a manufacturing instruction

delivered in an unusually economical form. The magnetic circuit deserves attention for the same reason:
improving its usable flux can reduce the winding demanded of us. We shall therefore settle the assembled
field and the speed before committing anyone to the full winding, lest the arithmetic become a
retrospective explanation of why the bench is covered in copper.
After rectification, the smoothing capacitor supplies current between charging peaks. Neglecting diode
conduction details and source impedance, ripple is approximately ΔV ≈ I/(fripple C). Full wave
rectification of 50 Hz gives 100 Hz ripple:
   C = I/(fripple ΔV) = 0.010/(100 × 5) = 20 µF
This supplies a 100 V, 10 mA rail with about 5 V ripple. Half wave operation requires twice the
capacitance for the same ripple, giving us another reason to consider the extra rectifier work. For the 100
µA beam rail, allowing 20 V ripple at 100 Hz gives 50 nF; add bleeder and divider currents before
choosing its final value.
   Estored = CV²/2
The 20 µF capacitor stores 0.10 J at 100 V, while the 50 nF capacitor stores 0.025 J at 1 kV. Add the
energy in the other capacitors and windings, fit controlled discharge paths, and enclose the high voltage
connections. Our feet supply the energy, but the resulting voltage deserves the same treatment as any
other high voltage supply.

Fabrication and acceptance
Record open circuit and loaded voltage, frequency, winding temperature, diode drop and ripple. Choke
filtering or additional phases may reduce capacitor labour at an acceptable cost in copper and iron.
Regulate the heaters separately enough that a change in pedal speed does not make the cathode, and
consequently the picture, brighten and dim with every stroke.
This returns us to the great smoothing capacitor of the preceding section. Its hundreds of gaps were a
consequence of the current it had to supply between charging peaks; change the spacing of those peaks or
insert a useful choke, and its assignment changes. The supply is therefore worth considering as a whole
before we distribute its labour among the several craftsmen. Our final objective remains steady heater
temperature and electrode voltage, so that the picture follows the recording rather than providing an
illustrated account of how vigorously we happen to be pressing the pedals.


13 Press and release foot treadles

Idea
The power comes from two footboards pressed downward and released, each pulling a strap around a
drum through its own ratchet or one way clutch. While a board returns, its drive overruns and the
flywheel continues turning. We have thus the press and release motion intended from the beginning.
Fit the two boards for a downward press and a free return, then seat the operator where his legs can make
the stated travel without disputing the furniture; for the same nature whose formative work we employ in
the glass has supplied the body with conditions of its own, which practical judgment ought consult before
appointing it the power station. The lever trades travel for strap force, and the pawl carries that force into
the common shaft while the other foot returns.

Claims
The operator supplies the work; the levers determine how force and travel exchange, while the springs
return the boards and the flywheel carries the shaft through the pauses. The action resembles a treadle
lathe, and we shall dimension the mechanism below around that movement.
The distinction matters because the footboard is only the place where work enters the machine. We may
arrange levers to make the strap pull harder, but the strap then travels less; we may let the flywheel carry
us through a pause, but it does so with energy given to it earlier. Such arrangements are useful precisely
because our legs provide an intermittent motion and the reader prefers continuous rotation. We are
translating one pattern of effort into another. By following that translation through the lever, strap and
ratchet, we can give the operator the downward press and release intended here while still supplying a
turning shaft to the generator.

Proof and worked calculation
Make each footboard about 300 mm long and 120 mm wide, placing it on a longer wooden rocker with
foot contact 450 mm from the pivot and strap connection 100 mm from it. An adjustable stop allows 200
mm foot travel. Use forged iron pivots and pawls, bronze bearing surfaces and leather straps, returning
each rocker with a light spring or counterweight.
  Lever ratio ≈ 450/100 = 4.5; strap travel ≈ 200/4.5 = 44.4 mm
  Wstroke = ∫F ds ≈ Faverage s; Pfoot = Faverage s ftotal
Count the downward strokes which actually supply power: one per second from each alternating foot
gives two strokes per second altogether. With 250 N average force and 200 mm travel, each stroke
supplies 50 J and the pair gives 100 W; at 500 N, the pair gives 200 W. The ideal strap forces are
respectively 1125 N and 2250 N, with linkage friction adding to the force required at the foot.
A 200 N/m spring acting through 200 mm reaches 40 N restoring force and stores 4 J from the preceding
stroke. Set its linkage to return the board gently, since persuading the operator to lift his foot is easier if
the machine refrains from striking it.
The spring has a small but honourable office in this arrangement. Without it, the operator must lift the
board as well as press it, which soon makes a supposedly agreeable seated occupation into a dispute with
the furniture. Giving the return to a spring makes the movement repeatable, though the energy placed in
that spring still came from the preceding stroke. We have now followed the mechanism around its
complete cycle and returned to the foot: its travel and average force must supply the work, while the
linkage makes that work convenient to deliver.

Fabrication and adjustment
Fit independent pawls so either foot may drive the flywheel, and guard the teeth, straps and wheel. Set the
seat and stroke stops before applying electrical load. Section 22 gives the final force and cadence: the
movement is press and release, with the longer travel required to supply the calculated work.

Idea
We shall now give the set an ordinary prerecorded VHS tape and recover its picture directly into the CRT
brightness control. The cassette supplies the programme, and our reader supplies the means of retrieving

Set the reader for the recording it receives and let recovered synchronization govern the display, retaining
the coarse spot which our small screen requires; for nature’s mechanical skill makes no exception for a
cassette merely because it contains a familiar programme, and the head must still meet the inscription in

track geometry through which the picture is recovered.

Claims
Begin with NTSC VHS recorded at SP speed and carrying normal linear audio. The cassette brings the

The cassette is an especially opinionated guest. It arrives containing a programme, but it also arrives with
decisions already made about the speed, track pattern and timing by which that programme can be
recovered. Our freedom lies in constructing a reader which follows those decisions and omitting the
conveniences we do not require. We need no motorized invitation for the cassette to enter, no colour
picture and no elaborate concealment of every blemish. We do, however, need to pass the heads over the
recording in the proper manner. The useful economy therefore comes from understanding which functions
recover the programme and which merely make its recovery more agreeable.

15 Manual cassette loading and tape transport

Idea
Open the cassette lid and release its reel brakes by hand lever, then draw the tape around the guides and
rotating scanner. A lever will do this work quite adequately, and has the agreeable property of needing no
loading motor or control circuit.
Give the capstan charge of linear speed and let the take-up reel gather what arrives through a slipping
clutch, since the growing pack changes its circumference throughout the programme; plastic nature will
apply that change as readily near the end as near the beginning, with no special affection for the setting
which happened to look correct when the reel was empty. We shall therefore set the clutch and brake
against tension, leaving the fixed working diameter to govern travel.

Claims
The capstan sets the tape speed, a slipping clutch gathers the used tape, and a lightly braked supply reel
prevents slack. These three functions remain distinct even though they occupy the same transport. [R3]
A reel is a deceptive speed control because its effective circumference changes while we watch. At the
beginning it gathers tape around a small pack; later it gathers the same length around a larger one, and a
fixed rotational speed would consequently pull more tape through the machine. The capstan avoids that
growing circumference by presenting a fixed working diameter. Once it sets the linear motion, the reel

need only gather what arrives, yielding through its clutch as the pack grows. We can now return to our
three apparently similar rotating parts and give each a different, definite occupation.

Proof and worked calculation
Give the cassette carrier three seating pads and adjustable side stops, locating the reel spindles from the
supplied cassette. Arrange the lever to release the brakes before drawing tape out. Use rounded, polished
bronze or glass guides, and bring the loading arms against screw adjusted stops so the tape height returns
to the same setting each time.
  Nominal half wrap length = πD/2 = π × 62/2 = 97.39 mm
Allow a little more than the half wrap calculated above, giving overlap for the change between heads.
Guide height and entry angle set the helix. A 58 µm track pitch with 0.5564 mm advance per field agrees
with a slope near 5.98°; use the format drawing to transfer the actual guide positions. [R2]
  Advance per field = 33.35/59.9401 = 0.5564 mm
  nreel = 60 vt/(2πr); take up torque ≈ T r

16 Shared mechanical scanner and capstan drive

Idea
Drive the scanner and capstan from a common governed shaft, keeping their mean speeds in the required
ratio and providing a separate manual adjustment for tracking phase.
Keep scanner and capstan upon the common governed drive, cutting the ratio before setting the tracking
phase; for proportion must continue in motion if the head is to remain upon its path, and nature’s
regularity will make a small disagreement accumulate with admirable patience. Dress the capstan from

measured travel, then adjust phase for the strongest steady signal, returning to the ratio whenever the
improvement refuses to remain with us.

Claims
A shared shaft and gearing establish the average relationship mechanically. The adjustment then deals
with tape slip, recording tolerances and guide errors, rather than requiring separate electronic motors for
the two motions.

Proof and worked calculation
Choose a scanner to capstan speed ratio of 17 to 1, and calculate the capstan diameter which belongs to
that ratio:
  Dc = 17 vt/(π fframe) = 6.021 mm approximately
  ncapstan = 1798.20/17 = 105.776 rpm
  Tape advance per scanner turn = πDc/17 = 1.11278 mm
A 17 tooth scanner gear driving a 289 tooth capstan gear gives the required reduction, or we may divide it
among smaller gear pairs. The integer ratio is convenient to preserve. Dress the capstan accurately: at
about 3.01 mm radius, a 5 µm radius error changes speed by 0.166 percent.
A relative feed error of 0.1 percent accumulates 33.35 µm/s along the tape, projecting to about 3.49 µm/s
across the track at 6°. Fit a fine phase differential or controllable clutch in the capstan branch, adjusting
for the strongest RF envelope and least noise. If the optimum keeps drifting, correct the feed ratio or
effective diameter as well as the phase.
Tracking is where a very small error acquires the patience to become a large nuisance. A fraction of a
percent looks harmless beside the dimensions of the frame, but the frame is not the thing we are
following; the recorded tracks are narrow, and the error continues accumulating for as long as the tape
moves. A phase adjustment can place the head upon a track at one instant without correcting the ratio
which carries it away again. We must therefore return from the immediate improvement on the screen to
the capstan diameter and feed relationship whenever the improvement refuses to remain with us.
The governor controls the shared shaft, while recovered tape video supplies line and field synchronization
to the display. Slow common speed variation therefore changes running time and sound pitch, with a ±1
percent variation giving roughly ±17 cents. Tracking still requires its own geometrical alignment.
  Scanner periphery speed = π × 0.062 × 29.970 = 5.838 m/s
Use 5.80 m/s as the relative head speed in recording wavelength calculations, keeping the peripheral
figure above as a consistency check. At 31 mm radius, the acceleration is about 112 g; even a 0.10 g
pickup mounting experiences roughly 0.110 N radial force. Balance the opposed assemblies and enclose
the scanner.

Fabrication and adjustment

Use adjustable bronze bearing blocks on a rigid frame, with separate fine settings for head height,
azimuth, protrusion and guide tilt. The operator's controls are speed, tracking, contrast, brightness,
horizontal hold and vertical hold, all fitted for adjustment by hand.

Idea

has to lie flat and remain free of folds where the gap matters, so the journey from gilded ornament to

18 Head winding and valve amplification

Idea
Bring each pickup through short, low capacitance wiring to its valve amplifier, then limit the amplified
carrier before recovering the picture.
Bring the head signal into its first valve by the shortest practicable path, then build the shared chain in
screened sections; for plastic nature will carry a coupling back toward the input with the same regularity
with which it carries our intended signal forward. Establish each stage’s stable gain before multiplying it,

so the eventual large voltage retains an intelligible relation to the small recording which first entered the
winding.

  Vhead,peak = 2πfNΦlinked,peak
At 3.9 MHz, twenty turns linked by 0.50 pWb give 245 µV peak; reducing linked flux to 0.05 pWb gives
24.5 µV. We shall carry the smaller value into the amplifier calculation, since it asks more of the gain.
Give each head its own stationary preamplifier, select between their outputs, and follow the selector with
nine shared RF stages. Eleven stages are installed, with ten in either active path. For each stage take gm =
3 mS, effective plate load 1 kΩ and total output capacitance 10 pF, using short leads, screening between
stages and separate input and output feedthroughs.
  Astage(f) = −gmR/[1 + j2πfRC]; fp = 15.92 MHz
  |A10(f)| = 3¹⁰/[1 + (f/fp)²]⁵
The calculated gain is 47,200 at 3.4 MHz, 40,900 at 4.4 MHz and 24,400 at 7 MHz. Thus 24.5 µV at 4.4
MHz becomes about 1.00 V before limiting. Stronger inputs deliberately drive the later stages into
limiting; trim the small signal gain slope with adjustable capacitance and loading.
RCA's RF valve equations give gm/Ip ≈ 3/(2Veffective), so a 3 mA plate current and 1.5 V effective
potential correspond to 3 mS in that approximation. Set the grid geometry, current partition and cathode
emission together, winding the control and screen grids on removable mandrels with mineral supports,
and using the cathode work from Section 4. [R7]

Fabrication and adjustment
Use two separate copper slip ring pairs, or adjustable rotary inductive couplers, between the rotating
heads and stationary amplifiers. Slip rings are the simpler workshop arrangement; fit fine spring brushes
and short pickup leads to limit contact noise. After the preamplifiers, a scanner position cam or pickup

controlled valve gate selects the active channel. Set the changeover near the bottom of the raster, where
we accept the remaining switching streak.


19 Simple FM recovery and brightness signal

Idea
We shall recover brightness by limiting the head signal and counting its frequency variations through
transferred charge. The counting occurs electrically in capacitors and valves.
Make the transitions transfer repeatable charges before averaging their recurrence through the load,
providing limiting and sufficient time for recovery between them; for nature’s own art will count an
unequal payment as faithfully as an equal one, and a detector which varies charge with amplitude will
have acquired an opinion about brightness which we did not request. Set the pulse conditions first, then
follow their mean current into the recovered voltage.

Claims
Each accepted transition transfers nearly the same charge, making average current follow the transition
rate. Pulse counting FM detection is an established valve era principle, and the dimensions below apply it
to the coarse picture required here. [R8]

Proof and worked calculation
Use the limiter and phase splitter to produce a repeatable bipolar swing, coupling it through small
capacitors into full wave vacuum diode steering. One polarity charges a coupling capacitor and the next
restores it by the opposite path, with both transitions contributing to the same output polarity. Reset the
charge between transitions so successive transfers remain equal.
  q = Cp ΔV; Iaverage = 2qf; Vaverage = 2qfRL
With effective Cp = 5 pF and a 5 V transition, each transfer is 25 pC. A 2 kΩ load gives sensitivity of 0.10
µV/Hz, producing 0.340–0.440 V across the 3.4–4.4 MHz reference span. Subtract the sync tip reference,
then amplify the remaining 0.100 V span by ten for the nominal 1 V brightness and synchronization
waveform.
Begin with 330 Ω in the coupling path, giving CpR = 1.65 ns against a 114 ns half cycle at 4.4 MHz. Add
valve transition and diode conduction time. Transferring 25 pC in 20 ns requires about 1.25 mA at that
instant, so give the steering diodes the emitting area and close spacing to carry the pulse.
Follow the detector with three buffered RC low pass sections, each of 2 kΩ and 100 pF total capacitance.
Their individual poles are 796 kHz and their combined −3 dB bandwidth is about 406 kHz. At 6.8 MHz,
the ideal attenuation is about 56 dB. We surrender picture detail in exchange for suppressing carrier
ripple, isolating the sections with valve followers.
  B3 = fp √(2^(1/3) − 1) = 0.406 MHz
The recording emphasizes luminance before FM modulation, so add an adjustable RC de-emphasis shelf
between recovery and the final brightness amplifier. Begin with 1 kΩ in series and a branch of 2 kΩ in

series with 160 pF to ground. Low frequency gain approaches one and high frequency gain 2/3, with a
pole near 332 kHz and zero near 497 kHz. The remaining edge brightness and contrast errors belong to
our accepted soft picture. [R9]

Fabrication and adjustment
Set limiter swing and charge transfer with an RF oscillator at the two reference frequencies, including
stray capacitance in the effective 5 pF. Adjust output offset, contrast and the shelf using broad black, grey
and white areas. Take synchronization from a separate branch before the shelf, so turning the contrast
control does not also move the scan trigger threshold.

Idea

Fabrication and adjustment

Assembly and operating sequence

1. Begin with the glass, vacuum, cathode, phosphor and stationary spot work in Sections 1–6. Establish
the small green screen before attaching the reader, so any fault has fewer places in which to conceal itself.
2. Assemble the treadles, guarded flywheel and generator, checking the selected electrical load and the
available range of speed control.
3. Fit the cassette carrier, loading guides, reel brakes and slipping take up clutch. Use a sacrificial tape to
establish the scanner and capstan relationship.
4. Make a matched pair of video pickups, winding and polishing them before setting their azimuths.
Connect through the rotating electrical interface and obtain the RF envelope while the transport turns.
5. Adjust the RF chain, limiter and charge transfer detector against known carrier frequencies, then
recover broad changes of brightness and synchronization from the tape.
6. Lock the line and field scans to recovered synchronization, blank the returns and adjust the coarse spot.
Add the fixed audio head and align sound with picture.
7. Operate the set with its manual speed, tracking, hold, brightness and contrast controls. Stop before a
falling flywheel speed damages the tape path, and use the separate clutch setting to rewind.
We have now brought the treadle drive, display and VHS reader into one arrangement. The picture is soft,
the controls require a hand, and the sound includes the hiss and speed related pitch changes already
described, but the cassette supplies the programme and the machine supplies its moving image and sound.
Material properties, valve characteristics and efficiencies accompany their calculations, and the cassette is
the sole pre-existing VHS component.
The several inconveniences have acquired a common purpose. The glass no longer contains air, the heated
coating supplies electrons, the beam follows the recovered timing, and the same feet which began by
moving two wooden boards now keep the tape passing the pickup. It would have been difficult to see that
whole arrangement while considering a single capacitor or a piece of gold leaf, yet those were the places
where its requirements became concrete. We return at last to the original ambition, which was to insert a
recorded programme and watch it: the intervening apparatus is the long answer to what that apparently
simple request asks of the workshop.

23 Ancient materials mechanisms and theoretical foundations

Construction premise
The builder receives the design, dimensions and operating principles given here, and carries them out with
materials and workshop methods obtainable in the Roman world. We shall therefore look at the crafts,
mechanisms and broad physical observations already present, then follow their application through the
television and reader.
We shall take the ancient crafts at their actual offices, using the glassworker’s vessel, the smith’s pivot and
the beater’s leaf where the construction calls for them; for plastic nature was no less industrious in the
Roman workshop than in our own, and the supplied design must employ the materials before the
craftsman with their several powers intact. Follow the accounts into the particular operations below,
where the knowledge of the instrument receives its expression in hands, furnace and tool.

Glass vessels and metal leaf
Pliny describes remelting, blowing, turning and transparent colourless glass in Natural History
36.192–198. Those crafts serve our envelope, neck, exhaust tubing and insulating supports; the surviving
vessel cited as H1 puts an archaeological example beside the written account. [A1 H1]
Gold beating appears in Natural History 33.61–62, and the measured Hellenistic and Roman leaves in R4
give us the thickness range used for the spacer. The head construction sections then set out the flatness,
local thickness and assembly which turn that leaf into a controlled gap. [A5 R4]

This is the sort of connection which rewards looking closely at an old craft before dismissing it as merely
decorative. The craftsman was beating gold for an object with no magnetic recording in its future, but the
resulting leaf has a physical thickness independent of what he intended to do with it. Our design finds a
use for that property. Following the evidence through the measured material therefore brings us back to
the head spacer with something more concrete than an assurance that ancient people were clever: it gives
the particular scale of sheet upon which the assembly depends.

Gears levers pistons and valves
Vitruvius gives us geared milling in On Architecture 10.5, the brass pump of Ctesibius with alternating
smooth pistons and nonreturn valves in 10.7, and the hydraulic organ with its levers and leather covered
moving parts in 10.8. Here are the relevant transmission, bearing, linkage and valve crafts, which our two
downward acting treadles apply to the required power. [A2]

Pressure evacuation and electrical observations
Hero's Pneumatics treats trapped air, suction and artificially produced partial evacuation, giving the
pressure difference and removal of air their ancient mechanical foundation. We have already put
dimensions, pumping rate and operating pressure to that work in the vacuum sections. [A3]
Pliny also records rubbed amber attracting light material, comparing it with the magnet's attraction of iron
in Natural History 37.48. Electrical attraction and magnetism were thus observable phenomena in the
ancient world. The builder receives the induction, emission and amplifier relations in this design and
follows their application to the parts. [A4]
There is a considerable pleasure in finding the familiar observations at the beginning of such an
unfamiliar machine. Amber attracts light objects; a magnet attracts iron; a pressure vessel moves a piston;
a lever carries motion to another place. The television collects different applications of these physical
relationships into one instrument, and the design supplies the intervening knowledge by which the crafts
are assigned their new work. We return to the ancient workshop with those assignments in hand, asking
what it must shape, heat, polish, wind and join, since that is where the supplied knowledge becomes a
material object.

Application to the complete machine
The prerecorded VHS cassette brings the programme and the recording geometry set out in the format
references. We construct the reader, CRT and treadle drive from the materials and processes in their
respective sections, using the numbered drawings for the assemblies and the calculations for loads,
timing, tolerances and material performance. [R1 R2]

25 Pump assembly baking and seal off




Figure 2. Join the tube above the falling drop section. The drawing shows the pumping relationship,
simplifying the mercury return and leaving the trap geometry to the assembly.

Construction and operating order
1. Mount the glass drop pump and collecting vessel on a rigid stand, giving the fall more than the
approximately 30 in mercury barometric height. Meter separated drops through a restriction into a narrow
fall tube, with the gas pockets discharging below the barometric seal. The separated pockets between
drops are the feature which carries the gas out.
2. Fit an isolation branch and condensing trap between pump and bulb, keeping mercury in closed vessels
with contained transfer; never heat an open reservoir. While pumping, bake the glass and electrodes to
release adsorbed gas, protecting phosphor and cathode from excessive temperature.
3. Run the heater during late pumping and collect a small anode current, observing the gas released under
working conditions. Cool and isolate the tube, comparing current and beam behaviour over time.
Reconnect and repeat until the rapid changes subside, then seal the exhaust sidearm.
The heater now contributes to the pump's business as well as to the beam's. Surfaces which behaved
quietly when cold may release gas when the instrument reaches working temperature, so a tube sealed
immediately after a cold evacuation would reserve part of its trouble for the first evening's viewing.
Running it while still connected allows that released gas to leave by the route already provided. We have

thus come back to the pump after beginning to operate the cathode, because the condition in which the
tube is used is the condition its evacuation must accommodate.
Run the heater during the later pumping and observe the gas released while the electrodes approach their
working condition, since plastic nature continues its operations after the cold vessel has given us a
satisfactory first impression. Heat, pump, cool and isolate in sequence, returning to evacuation while rapid
changes remain, so that the condition established before closure corresponds to the condition in which the
beam will actually be used.
  V dp/dt = Q − Seff p; p(t) = Q/Seff + (p0 − Q/Seff)e^(−Seff t/V)
With vessel volume 0.5 L, effective pumping speed 0.01 L/s and gas load 10⁻⁵ Pa L/s, the equation gives

Set trap temperature and conductance to control the mercury vapour, comparing gas release before and

31 Head transfer spacing loss and electrical noise

What the gap calculation does establish
  λ = vrelative/f; Ggap = |sin(πg/λ)/(πg/λ)|
  Lspacing = 54.58 d/λ dB; Vhead,peak = 2πfNΦpeak
At 4.4 MHz and 5.8 m/s, the wavelength is 1.318 µm. A 0.30 µm gap loses about 0.75 dB, and 0.10 µm
separation adds 4.14 dB, giving a combined amplitude factor near 0.57. At 0.50 µm separation, spacing
loss alone reaches 20.7 dB. The soft picture therefore still requires close, smooth contact where the
recording enters the head. [R6]
Follow the carrier through gap and spacing loss before assigning gain to the amplifier, then compare it
with the first stage’s noise; for nature’s mechanical skill will enlarge both contributions together once they
have entered the same chain. Improve contact and initial reception where the ratio is being lost, returning
to the head and its first valve before offering another stage as a universal remedy for a signal which was
already obscured.
The broad CRT spot has spared us a demand for fine displayed detail, but the tape has already committed
its information to these short magnetic wavelengths. We must recover that carrier before deciding how
much picture detail to preserve. This is why a soft image can still require a very small pickup gap, and
why a barely visible separation between tape and head can take such a large portion of the signal. The
wavelength calculation returns us directly to polishing, cleanliness and contact, which now have an
electrical consequence expressed in decibels.
With twenty turns, 3.9 MHz and 0.5 pWb already coupled into the core, ideal winding voltage is 245 µV
peak; at 0.05 pWb it is 24.5 µV. Apply gap and spacing loss either before calculating that linked flux or to
a voltage calculated before the losses, counting each loss once.

Why voltage gain alone is insufficient
An amplifier enlarges noise along with signal, so we shall examine the first stage before relying upon the
gain of the whole chain. For a conservative illustrative calculation, treat 3 mA valve current as
uncorrelated full shot noise and divide by gm = 3 mS. Space charge suppression, partition effects and
other contributions enter the actual valve noise.
  en = √(2qI)/gm = 10.3 nV/√Hz; vn,rms ≈ en √B
  B = 5 MHz ⇒ vn,rms ≈ 23.1 µV
The 245 µV peak case is 173 µV rms and gives about 17.5 dB input signal to noise ratio in this model; the
24.5 µV peak case gives about −2.5 dB. Thus head coupling and first stage noise determine whether the
carrier stands clear of noise before later amplification and limiting. Set those first, and the subsequent
stages can perform their proper work.
There is a temptation, upon finding the first signal disappointing, to prescribe more amplification with the
confidence of a man ordering another length of rope. Yet the unwanted fluctuations enter the same
amplifier, and emerge enlarged beside what we wished to recover. The ratio at the beginning consequently
follows us through the chain. Improving head contact or the first stage can accomplish something which a
later multiplication of both signal and noise cannot, so the long amplifier brings us back to its smallest
and earliest input.
With tape stopped and heater running, measure noise, then compare the carrier during motion using a
rectifying indicator. Repeat with a substitute electrical input and a shielded head, separating mechanical
pickup, head loss and amplifier noise through comparisons available to the workshop.

remains intact; the arithmetic now tells us the dimensions and cadence with which that choice must be
carried out.

Assembly order
1. Commission the treadles, guarded flywheel and generator on passive loads, measuring shaft speed,
electrical power and foot force over a timed run. Once speed and temperature are steady, connect the tape
transport.

2. Make the vacuum diode, characterize the cathodes and establish sound glass seals. Produce a steady
phosphor dot and two independent deflections, then operate the low line count test raster before changing
to tape timing.
3. Build the stationary audio pickup, manual cassette carrier and slow tape transport, setting reel tension
and capstan feed. Add the balanced rotating scanner and examine the sacrificial tape for damage.
4. Compare the head specimens, then set the first RF stage, shared amplifier and limiter separately.
Recover known frequency inputs before connecting the CRT grid, lock line and field scans to recovered
synchronization, and then add brightness.
5. Align guide height, tracking phase and the two head outputs before setting picture focus and size.
Record foot force, cadence, supply currents, valve temperatures and tape condition after warmup. We are
setting the machine for steady recognizable moving monochrome imagery with linear sound, accepting
the soft picture and switching streak described above.
Build and align in that order, carrying the dimensions, numbered drawings and operating calculations to
the bench with the work. Keep the fitted values together so transport, signal and scans can be adjusted as
one instrument, which, after all these separate components have occupied our attention, is what we set out
to make.
The temptation at this point is to regard the picture as the work of the screen, since that is where we
finally see it. Yet its steadiness includes the flywheel, its grey levels include the detector, its detail
includes the contact between tape and head, and its very existence includes the glassworker who kept the
atmosphere outside. Our long journey through separate components returns them to one another. Pressing
a board and watching a recorded programme were the two visible ends of the original idea; everything
between them has now acquired its particular place at the proverbial table.
